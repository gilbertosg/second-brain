## Why it matters

Hematocrit measures the percentage of red blood cells in the blood, which are responsible for carrying oxygen to tissues throughout the body. Since oxygen is essential for energy production, muscle function, and overall health, hematocrit tells how well the blood is delivering oxygen. It also helps assess hydration levels and cardiovascular function, as imbalances can signal issues like anemia, dehydration, or other underlying health concerns. Evaluating hematocrit provides an understanding of how efficiently the body is transporting oxygen and whether further investigation is needed.

Hematocrit is closely related to other blood markers, including hemoglobin and red blood cell count, which together provide a clearer picture of oxygen delivery and red blood cell health. Low hematocrit levels, when paired with low hemoglobin, often indicate anemia, a condition where the blood lacks enough red blood cells to carry sufficient oxygen. On the other hand, when hematocrit, hemoglobin, and red blood cell count are all elevated, it may point to dehydration or conditions that cause the body to produce excess red blood cells, such as living at high altitudes or chronic lung disease. Understanding these relationships helps identify the root cause of imbalances and determine the best course of action for restoring optimal function.

Hematocrit levels outside the normal range can signal different health concerns:

- **Low hematocrit** : Often linked to anemia, which can result from iron, vitamin B12, or folate deficiencies, chronic disease, or blood loss. Symptoms may include fatigue, dizziness, weakness, and shortness of breath.
- **High hematocrit** : May indicate dehydration, where reduced fluid levels make red blood cells more concentrated, or conditions that increase red blood cell production, such as lung disease or living at high altitudes. In some cases, it can suggest polycythemia, a disorder where the body makes too many red blood cells, increasing the risk of blood clots.

**Summary**

Hematocrit measures the percentage of red blood cells in the blood and helps assess oxygen delivery and overall blood health. Abnormal levels can indicate anemia, dehydration, or other conditions, and addressing these imbalances often involves dietary changes, hydration, and managing underlying health issues.

**Optimal ranges**

Hematocrit values at both the low and high ends are linked with higher mortality risk, so approximately 40-45% for men and 37-44% for women are viewed as practical mid‑normal target zones within the overall normal range.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Hematocrit levels can naturally fluctuate due to a variety of factors, and small changes from day to day are completely normal. Hydration status plays a big role. When you're dehydrated, the concentration of red blood cells appears higher because there's less fluid in the blood, whereas drinking plenty of water can dilute the blood and make hematocrit levels temporarily lower. Exercise, altitude, and even the time of day can also affect readings; for example, spending time at high elevations (where you need more red blood cells to carry oxygen) can stimulate more red blood cell production, and levels may be slightly higher in the morning compared to later in the day. Illness, inflammation, or recent blood loss (for example, losing blood from an injury or heavy menstrual cycle) can lead to temporary drops, while conditions that stimulate red blood cell production, like lung disease or sleep apnea, can gradually push hematocrit levels higher. Because of these natural variations, a single test result may not tell the full story, and tracking trends over time provides a clearer picture of overall health.

## Summary

A hematocrit value in the normal range indicates that the proportion of red blood cells in the blood is balanced and sufficient to support efficient oxygen transport to tissues and organs. This typically reflects a healthy balance of nutrients such as iron, vitamin B12, and folate, which are essential for red blood cell production and function. Normal hematocrit levels also suggest the absence of significant anemia or polycythemia, conditions that can disrupt oxygen delivery. However, it is important to interpret hematocrit levels alongside other blood markers like hemoglobin and red blood cell count to ensure a complete understanding of overall blood health and identify subtle imbalances that may not yet cause symptoms.

To maintain normal hematocrit levels, a root cause approach focuses on ensuring optimal nutrient intake and addressing any underlying factors that could impact red blood cell production or survival. This may include consuming a nutrient-dense diet rich in iron (from organic red meats or plant sources paired with vitamin C for better absorption), vitamin B12 (from animal products or supplements), and folate (from leafy greens). Supporting gut health to enhance nutrient absorption and managing chronic inflammation through anti-inflammatory botanicals, stress management, and lifestyle changes can further promote balanced hematocrit levels.

Regular follow-up testing may be advised to monitor trends and make adjustments to dietary, pharmaceutical, or supportive therapies to maintain overall blood health and prevent future complications.
