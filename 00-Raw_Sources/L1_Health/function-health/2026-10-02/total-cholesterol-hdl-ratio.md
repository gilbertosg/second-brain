## Why it matters

The cholesterol/high-density lipoprotein (HDL) ratio is a simple, but key marker that helps understand the balance between total cholesterol (a measure of the total amount of cholesterol in the bloodstream) and HDL cholesterol, which is often referred to as the “good” cholesterol that is responsible for removing excess cholesterol from the arteries and protecting against plaque buildup in the blood vessels of the heart. Instead of just looking at total cholesterol, this ratio shows how well the body is managing it. A higher ratio generally indicates a higher risk of heart disease. A lower ratio reflects having more protective HDL to offset the cholesterol that may cause plaque buildup in the blood vessels. It provides a more comprehensive view of heart disease risk rather than looking at LDL (low-density lipoprotein) cholesterol or total cholesterol alone, because it evaluates the balance between the atherogenic (plaque-forming) particles like LDL cholesterol versus the protective HDL cholesterol in the blood.

The cholesterol/HDL ratio should be evaluated alongside other key markers like LDL small, LDL particle number, LDL peak size, LDL pattern, lipoprotein(a), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), triglycerides, and markers of inflammation like hs-CRP. If the cholesterol/HDL ratio is high, but LDL particle size is small and dense or ApoB is elevated, an individual may be at increased cardiovascular risk, even if total cholesterol is within the normal range. On the other hand, a lower ratio with healthy triglyceride levels and low levels of inflammation suggests a lower risk of cardiovascular disease.

In addition to the cholesterol/HDL ratio, another critical indicator of cardiovascular disease risk is the triglyceride-to-HDL ratio. Elevated ratios in either case are strong markers of poor metabolic health and insulin resistance, which are often linked to small, dense HDL and LDL particles—factors that increase cardiovascular risk. Ideally, the triglyceride-to-HDL ratio should be below 2.0, and the cholesterol/HDL ratio should be 3.5 or lower. These ratios together give a better reflection of cardiovascular health than standard cholesterol levels alone and can indicate the presence of atherogenic small, dense LDL particles that increase heart disease risk.

In summary:

- **High ratio:** A higher cholesterol/HDL ratio suggests a greater likelihood that excess cholesterol may build up in the walls of the arteries, increasing the risk of future cardiovascular events like heart attacks and strokes. This risk becomes even more significant when paired with low HDL, high LDL, or signs of inflammation. Cardiovascular risks are also often linked to underlying issues like poor blood sugar control or insulin resistance.
- **Low ratio:** Generally reflects a healthier balance between total cholesterol and HDL. Often seen in individuals with good heart and metabolic health.

Regular monitoring of the cholesterol/HDL ratio can help identify potential risks early on. Lifestyle modifications like a well-balanced diet, regular exercise, and smoking cessation can positively influence this ratio and contribute to overall cardiovascular fitness.

**Summary**

The cholesterol/HDL ratio is a key indicator of cardiovascular health. The ratio shows the balance between atherogenic (plaque-forming) cholesterol particles, which can lead to plaque buildup in arteries, and the protective cholesterol particles, which help remove excess cholesterol from the bloodstream. From a root cause approach, a higher ratio increases the risk of cardiovascular disease and often points to deeper issues like inflammation, poor blood sugar regulation, or insulin resistance that need to be addressed to protect overall heart health.

**Optimal ranges**

The optimal range for the cholesterol/HDL ratio is typically below 3.5. Maintaining a lower ratio is associated with a reduced risk of heart disease.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Your cholesterol/HDL ratio is not fixed, and can change over time and even from day to day, depending on what’s happening in your body. This ratio reflects the balance between total cholesterol and HDL cholesterol which helps remove excess cholesterol from your blood. Factors like what you eat, how active you are, how well you sleep, how stressed you feel, and even short-term inflammation from illness or injury can all affect this ratio. For example, eating a diet high in refined carbohydrates and added sugars the day before your test can temporarily raise your cholesterol levels, while regular exercise can gradually improve your HDL levels over time. One test is just a snapshot, and it is more useful to look at trends over time and consider this result in the context of your overall health.

## Summary

A cholesterol/HDL ratio in the normal range (typically between 3.5 to 5.0) suggests a healthy balance between the total cholesterol and HDL cholesterol in the blood. This ratio reflects how efficiently the body is managing reverse cholesterol transport, the process by which HDL removes excess cholesterol from arteries and delivers it to the liver for elimination, which ensures cholesterol is not building up in ways that promote plaque or heart disease. This is a strong indicator of low cardiovascular risk when paired with stable blood sugar levels, low triglycerides, and low systemic inflammation.

From a root cause perspective, even a normal cholesterol/HDL ratio is worth evaluating further to support optimal cardiometabolic fitness. A comprehensive assessment of advanced lipid biomarkers, including apolipoprotein B (ApoB) and LDL particle size and number, alongside inflammation markers like hs-CRP, and metabolic markers like fasting glucose and insulin, may be beneficial. Nutrition strategies can include eating whole, unprocessed, anti-inflammatory foods rich in healthy fats (like omega-3s from wild-caught fish and extra-virgin olive oil and alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) from flax or chia seeds), leafy greens, nuts, and seeds to help maintain healthy lipid levels while stabilizing blood sugar. Regular movement, including resistance training and aerobic activity, stress management, adequate restorative sleep, and maintaining a healthy weight, all help improve HDL functionality and insulin signaling and lower inflammation naturally.
