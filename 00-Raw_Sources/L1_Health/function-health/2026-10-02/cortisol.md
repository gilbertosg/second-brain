## Why it matters

Cortisol is a steroid hormone made by the adrenal glands, which sit on top of the kidneys. It plays a major role in how the body handles stress, manages energy, controls inflammation, supports memory formation, and keeps systems like the immune and nervous systems in balance. Cortisol follows a daily rhythm, usually peaking in the early morning to help you wake up and get going, then dropping at night to prepare for sleep.

It’s also a key player in the body’s acute stress response—commonly known as the “fight or flight” response—helping to mobilize energy, raise blood pressure, and modulate immune activity when you're under threat. Acting like a built-in alarm system, cortisol helps the body stay alert in challenging situations and return to a steady state afterward. While it’s best known as a “stress hormone,” cortisol plays a wide range of essential roles in keeping the body healthy and resilient.

This particular test measures cortisol in the blood, which helps assess how much cortisol the body is producing overall. While, saliva testing of cortisol is better for understanding daily rhythm and short-term fluctuations.

Cortisol is closely tied to other important markers in the body. For example, it works alongside insulin to regulate blood sugar and can influence thyroid hormones, which affect energy and metabolism. If cortisol is out of balance, it can impact these other systems as well. Measuring cortisol—especially along with tests like DHEA-S (another hormone from the adrenal glands), ACTH (a brain hormone that tells the adrenal glands what to do), or blood glucose—can help reveal deeper patterns related to stress, fatigue, inflammation, or metabolic health. Testing cortisol levels can guide decisions about how to support adrenal health, improve energy, reduce inflammation, or restore sleep and mood balance.

- **High cortisol** can occur when the body stays in stress mode for too long. It may also be linked to certain health conditions, like Cushing’s syndrome, or show up in people with anxiety, depression, or trouble sleeping. When cortisol stays elevated, it can lead to weight gain—especially around the belly—and make it harder for the body to manage blood sugar.
- **Low cortisol** may point to adrenal fatigue, Addison’s disease, a rare condition where the adrenal glands don’t make enough hormones, showing up as extreme tiredness, salt cravings, or even low blood pressure.

**Summary**

Cortisol is a hormone that helps the body respond to stress, regulate inflammation, and maintain energy balance. Imbalances—whether too high or too low—can signal deeper issues like chronic stress, poor sleep, or adrenal dysfunction that warrant further investigation.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Cortisol follows a daily rhythm—with the highest values in the morning, around 30 to 45 minutes after waking, and then gradually drops throughout the day, hitting its lowest point at night. Levels can shift from day to day based on stress, sleep, illness, activity, and even diet, making a single test result hard to interpret in isolation. Because of this natural variation, results may look different depending on when and how the test is done. It can even be impacted if the drive to the lab was particularly stressful. Because of how dynamic cortisol is, it’s important to interpret the results in context, with the timing, lifestyle at the time of the test, and symptoms all taken into account.

## Summary

A cortisol result in the normal range generally indicates that the body is effectively managing stress and maintaining a healthy balance of hormone production through the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. Normal cortisol levels help stabilize blood sugar, regulate blood pressure, reduce inflammation, and support cognitive clarity throughout the day. People will often feel the difference in their energy, sleep, and emotional regulation when their cortisol is in the healthy range. There are typically no symptoms associated with a normal cortisol level, but it’s always important to consider the timing of the test, since cortisol naturally fluctuates throughout the day, peaking in the morning and dropping at night.

To maintain optimal cortisol levels, a root cause approach focuses on keeping the HPA axis functioning smoothly through targeted nutrition, lifestyle habits, and occasional supportive therapies. Nutrients like vitamin C, B vitamins, and magnesium support adrenal health and cortisol synthesis. Lifestyle practices like regular movement, quality sleep, and stress-reduction techniques such as breathwork or meditation can also support the body’s natural cortisol rhythm. A Mediterranean-style diet, rich in antioxidants, healthy fats, and high-quality protein supports hormone production and reduces inflammation, helping to protect adrenal function over time. Periodic follow-up testing may include morning salivary cortisol or other panels to ensure rhythm remains intact. By addressing foundational drivers of stress and nutrient depletion, this strategy helps preserve long-term adrenal health and resilience.
