## Why it matters

Lipoprotein(a), often called Lp(a), is a type of cholesterol particle that is largely determined by your genes and inherited from your family. Lp(a) is made of a low-density lipoprotein (LDL) particle and carries an extra protein called apolipoprotein(a) that makes it stickier and more likely to contribute to plaque buildup in your arteries. An elevated level of Lp(a) is an independent risk factor for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) like heart attacks, ischemic strokes, aortic valve stenosis (narrowing of the aortic valve), cardiovascular mortality, and all-cause mortality. However, because it promotes clotting and inflammation, it can silently increase the risk for heart disease, even if other cholesterol metrics look normal. Since Lp(a) typically remains stable throughout life, Function measures it during your first Annual Test to establish your inherited cardiovascular risk.

Lp(a) is part of a broader picture of cardiovascular risk, and it interacts with other markers like LDL cholesterol, apolipoprotein B (ApoB, the main protein found on LDL particles that accounts for the number of atherogenic particles), and markers of inflammation like hs-CRP. If someone has normal LDL but high Lp(a), their cardiovascular risk may be underestimated using standard cholesterol testing alone. Identifying elevated Lp(a) can change how aggressively a clinician, typically a cardiologist or lipidologist, might approach prevention, including the use of lipid-lowering medications, supplements like omega-3 fatty acids, or advanced imaging to look for early plaque formation. It can also influence decisions around family screening since elevated Lp(a) tends to run in families. Because Lp(a) changes very little over time without certain medications (e.g., PCSK9 inhibitors) or major health changes, your initial result remains clinically meaningful for future heart risk assessment. Repeat testing may be useful if the initial result is near a clinical decision threshold (approximately 125 nmol/L) or you have started lipid-lowering therapies. If this applies to you, Lp(a) testing will be available as a Function Add-on Test.

- **Elevated lipoprotein(a):** If Lp(a) is elevated, it signals a higher lifelong risk for cardiovascular disease, even if LDL cholesterol appears normal, because these particles are more likely to stick to artery walls, promote inflammation, and form plaque. High levels of Lp(a) are also linked to an increased risk of blood clots and narrowing of the aortic valve, known as aortic stenosis. Lp(a) levels are largely inherited, and lifestyle changes have limited impact on this specific marker, but knowing it's elevated allows for more proactive and personalized strategies to manage overall heart risk.
- **Low lipoprotein(a):** If Lp(a) is low or within the normal range, it generally points to a lower inherited risk for cardiovascular disease, particularly when other markers like LDL, ApoB, and inflammation are also in a healthy range. This result can offer reassurance and help focus attention on other areas of metabolic or heart health that may still benefit from support. Because Lp(a) is largely determined by genetics, this result is expected to remain stable over time.

**Summary**

Lipoprotein(a) is largely a genetically determined cholesterol particle that can increase the lifelong risk of heart disease and stroke, even when routine cholesterol levels appear normal. Measuring Lp(a) during your first Annual Test helps identify inherited cardiovascular risk early, allowing you and your healthcare provider to take personalized prevention strategies to manage other modifiable risk factors.

**Optimal ranges**

​The optimal range for lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), is generally considered to be below 50 nmol/L. Levels above 125 nmol/L are associated with a higher risk of cardiovascular diseases, such as heart attacks and strokes, even if other cholesterol levels are normal.

Since Lp(a) levels are largely determined by genetics and remain relatively constant throughout life, measuring Lp(a) levels once in a lifetime is recommended to understand a person's inherited risk early to prevent any major future cardiovascular events. If elevated, a healthcare provider may suggest more personalized strategies to manage overall heart health.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

While Lp(a) levels are primarily determined by genetics and usually remain stable throughout life, modest biological variations can occur. Temporary fluctuations may occur during acute illness, infection, inflammation, hormonal changes (e.g., menopause or hormone replacement therapy), tissue injury, or recent surgery. Certain medical conditions, particularly kidney disease or advanced liver disease, can also influence Lp(a) levels. These changes do not mean that your underlying genetic baseline has changed, but rather reflect temporary changes in how the liver produces Lp(a), how the body clears it, or the body’s inflammatory response at the time of testing. Dietary changes, body weight, and physical activity generally have little or inconsistent effects on Lp(a). In addition, laboratory testing methods can sometimes create small apparent differences in Lp(a) measurements.

For the most reliable assessment of Lp(a), it is best to measure levels when you are not having an acute medical issue.

## Summary

A lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), test result below the reference range is not considered a health concern. In fact, low Lp(a) levels are typically seen as favorable and are associated with a lower inherited risk for heart disease, stroke, and aortic valve narrowing. Because Lp(a) is mostly determined by genetics, a below-range result generally reflects a naturally lower inherited risk for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD).
