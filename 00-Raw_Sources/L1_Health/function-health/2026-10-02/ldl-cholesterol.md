## Why it matters

Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, often called “bad” cholesterol, is a type of lipoprotein that carries cholesterol through the bloodstream to different parts of the body. Cholesterol itself isn’t innately bad; it’s a fat-like substance the body uses for cellular growth and repair and to make hormones. But when too much LDL cholesterol is circulating, it can stick to the walls of the arteries. Over time, this buildup forms plaque, narrowing the arteries and reducing blood flow. Think of it like a delivery truck that drops off too many packages in the middle of the road—eventually, the streets get blocked and traffic slows down. That’s what can happen inside blood vessels, raising the risk of heart disease, heart attack, or stroke.

While lower LDL is generally beneficial, cholesterol is more complex than just "good" or "bad." The quality and size of cholesterol particles provide more insight than the standard tests, which only measure the number of HDL or LDL by weight (measured in milligrams per deciliter or mg/dL of blood) and don't reveal the health of the particles themselves. Cholesterol particles can be small and dense, which increases risk, or large and fluffy, which are more protective. The goal is to have more light, fluffy, and functional particles. For instance, an LDL level of 150 mg/dL could represent either 1,000 small, harmful particles or 10 large, protective ones, yet standard tests only measure the total weight, which doesn’t fully reflect heart disease risk. In your membership, you can refer to LDL small, medium, peak size, and particle number.

Also, LDL cholesterol doesn’t work alone. It’s part of a bigger picture that includes HDL cholesterol, total cholesterol, and triglycerides (a type of fat in the blood that stores unused calories for energy). For example, one of the best indicators of cardiovascular risk is not LDL cholesterol alone; it is the triglyceride-to-HDL ratio, ideally 1:1. A ratio above 2:1 raises risk, with a 5:1 ratio strongly linked to cardiovascular disease, insulin resistance, and prediabetes.

Optimizing HDL cholesterol is especially important when looking at LDL as a whole because large, more buoyant HDL particles are protective and can help counterbalance the associated risks. While smaller, more dense HDL particles are often caused by insulin resistance, prediabetes, and type 2 diabetes, and increases the risk of heart disease. In addition to the size and quality of HDL particles, there are other tests to consider when lipid markers point to an increased risk. Advanced tests like the pCAD score and pCEC, together can capture one of HDL’s most important protective roles and reveal cardiovascular risk factors that standard HDL cholesterol measurements can miss.

In a root cause approach, it’s really important not to look at LDL alone but rather patterns across all lipids, markers of inflammation like high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and insulin levels, which help understand not just where things are now but where they’re headed.

For example, high LDL paired with high triglycerides and low HDL might suggest insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome, even before blood sugar numbers rise. Knowing LDL level helps guide decisions about diet, lifestyle, and when necessary, medications or supplements to bring things back into balance.

- **High LDL cholesterol:** May signal an increased risk of plaque buildup in arteries. This is linked to conditions like coronary artery disease, stroke, and atherosclerosis. It can also be a clue that inflammation or poor metabolic health is in play, even if blood sugar looks normal.
- **Low LDL cholesterol:** While less common, low LDL may be seen in people with certain genetic conditions, severe liver disease, or hyperthyroidism (an overactive thyroid). It’s not usually a concern unless levels are extremely low or paired with other symptoms.

Testing LDL cholesterol through a root cause lens goes beyond just hitting a target number; it helps uncover the underlying factors driving imbalance, such as inflammation, insulin resistance, or poor liver function. By identifying and addressing these root causes early, we can take meaningful steps to protect heart health, improve metabolic function, and prevent chronic disease.

**Summary**

LDL cholesterol matters because when it's too high, it can build up in the blood vessels and block blood flow, which raises risk for heart disease. A root cause approach looks at why it's high—like poor diet, stress, inflammation, or insulin resistance—so you can fix the problem at its source, not just treat the symptoms.

**Optimal ranges**

The optimal range for LDL cholesterol is 20-70.00 mg/dL. It is generally considered to be less than 100 mg/dL (2.6 mmol/L) for healthy individuals (without pre-existing heart disease), and less than 70 mg/dL (1.8 mmol/L) for those at higher risk of cardiovascular disease, such as individuals with diabetes or a history of cardiovascular events, such as heart attacks or strokes. In individuals at very high risk, including those with familial hypercholesterolemia or elevated lipoprotein(a), an LDL cholesterol of less than 55 mg/dL should be considered.

These ranges are not a one-size-fits-all. A root cause medicine approach considers metabolic health, inflammation levels, and family history to determine the most protective range for every individual. This underscores the importance of personalized care, taking into account individual risk factors and health status to determine optimal targets for LDL cholesterol.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

LDL cholesterol levels aren’t fixed and can shift over time, even from day to day, depending on what’s happening in your body and lifestyle. What you eat, how much you move, how well you sleep, and even your stress levels can all influence your numbers. For example, eating a high-fat meal the night before a blood test or being under chronic stress can raise LDL levels temporarily. Certain medications, weight changes, hormone fluctuations, or conditions like thyroid dysfunction can also cause ups and downs. That’s why one high or low result doesn’t always mean something is wrong—it’s a snapshot, not the whole story. When we track LDL over time, we get a clearer picture of your true pattern, especially when paired with other markers like triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, and inflammation. Looking at trends and not just isolated numbers, points to patterns and underlying causes, not just one test result.

## Summary

An LDL cholesterol level in the normal range is typically a good sign, indicating that the body has enough cholesterol to support essential functions like hormone production, brain health, and cellular repair, without an excess that could build up in arteries. This level reflects a healthy balance between cholesterol production, transport, and clearance. While a normal LDL level lowers the risk for cardiovascular disease, it doesn’t completely rule it out. For example, someone can have normal LDL but still be at risk due to other factors like inflammation, insulin resistance, or high levels of small, dense LDL particles (a more damaging form of LDL that is more likely to contribute to plaque buildup). There are usually no symptoms tied to a normal LDL level itself. What matters most is whether this number is part of a broader pattern of metabolic health.

From a root cause perspective, a normal LDL level is worth evaluating in context of your other biomarkers, like the comprehensive lipid assessment, including particle size analysis, inflammation markers like hs-CRP, fasting insulin, and thyroid function.

Nutrition strategies for lipid balance can include eating whole, unprocessed foods rich in healthy fats (like omega-3s from wild-caught fish and alpha-linolenic acid from flax or chia seeds) to help maintain. Regular movement, stress management, and maintaining a healthy weight all help keep LDL in the optimal range by improving how the liver processes fats and cholesterol.
