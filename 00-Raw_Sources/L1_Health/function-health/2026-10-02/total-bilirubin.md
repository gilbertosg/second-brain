## Why it matters

Bilirubin is a yellow pigment made when red blood cells break down as part of the body’s recycling process. The liver filters and processes bilirubin so it can leave the body through bile and stool. Measuring bilirubin is important because it gives insight into how well the liver is working and whether red blood cells are breaking down too quickly or not being replaced as they should.

Bilirubin is typically evaluated alongside other liver tests, like ALT and AST, which can indicate overall liver health, and albumin, a key protein produced by the liver, to provide a more complete picture of liver health. High bilirubin with low albumin can point to liver problems. High bilirubin with other normal liver tests may suggest red blood cells are breaking down faster than usual. These patterns help clarify whether changes are related to the liver, blood, or another cause ([Guerra Ruiz, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10197288/); [Kalas, 2021](https://www.wjgnet.com/1948-5182/full/v13/i11/1688.htm)).

- **High bilirubin** may signal liver dysfunction, a blockage in the bile ducts (like gallstones), or rapid breakdown of red blood cells. When extremely elevated, it can cause jaundice, which is the yellowing of the skin and eyes.
- **Low bilirubin** levels are typically not of clinical significance and may be caused by certain medications, although more testing of liver function may be indicated in certain instances.

**Optimal ranges**

The optimal range for bilirubin is 0.58-1.20 mg/dL.

Recent research suggests bilirubin can act as an important signaling molecule with hormone-like effects on metabolism, inflammation, and oxidative stress. Low bilirubin levels, known as hypobilirubinemia, have been associated with a higher risk of metabolic issues such as obesity, insulin resistance, MASLD (Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease), and possibly stroke. This suggests that lower bilirubin may help identify people at higher long-term metabolic risk, even when they otherwise appear healthy.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Certain stressors on the liver, such as a high intake of alcohol, some medications, or illnesses, may cause temporary changes in bilirubin levels. Other potential changes may occur due to certain fasting states and intense exercise, although the clinical research is not as well established. Because of these day-to-day changes, a single bilirubin test may not reflect long-term patterns, so trends over time and other liver tests (like ALT, AST, and albumin) may help give a clearer picture.

## Summary

A bilirubin level within the normal range generally reflects that the body is effectively breaking down old red blood cells and clearing waste through the liver and bile ducts. It suggests that bilirubin production and excretion are functioning well and there’s no immediate sign of liver dysfunction, bile flow blockage, or excessive red blood cell breakdown. However, bilirubin is just one piece of the picture and should be considered alongside other liver and metabolic markers.

To help maintain liver health and balanced metabolism, foundational lifestyle practices can make a meaningful difference. A diet rich in antioxidants (such as berries and leafy greens) and fiber (like whole grains and legumes) may help reduce oxidative stress and support detoxification pathways. Regular physical activity and stress management practices (e.g., mindfulness or breathwork) can contribute to overall metabolic flexibility and mitochondrial function, which are key for optimal liver function.

Limiting processed foods and minimizing exposure to environmental toxins may also help lower the burden on the liver.

## Foods to limit

Avoid toxic foods that add stress to your liver and the breakdown of molecules. Try to stay away from non-organic fruits and vegetables if you can. The pesticides used in non-organic agriculture can seep into your food and then into your body when you eat it.
