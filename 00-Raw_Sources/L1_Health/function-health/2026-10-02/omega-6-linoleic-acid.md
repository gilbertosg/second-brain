## Why it matters

Linoleic acid (LA) is an essential omega-6 fatty acid that the body cannot make on its own and must be obtained from food. It’s most abundant in vegetable oils (especially soybean, corn, safflower, and sunflower oils), as well as in nuts, seeds, meats, and eggs. LA is essential for building healthy cell membranes and supporting skin health by helping produce ceramides, which protect against moisture loss and irritation.

However, in modern diets, especially the standard American diet, LA intake is often excessive. Chronically high levels can disrupt the balance between omega-6 and omega-3 fats, increasing the body’s inflammatory load. LA is also prone to oxidation, producing compounds called OXLAMs that contribute to oxidative stress and tissue damage. Because LA is converted into arachidonic acid (AA), too much can further drive inflammation if not properly balanced with omega-3 fats like eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA). Chronically elevated LA levels have been linked to increased risks of chronic diseases such as heart disease and Alzheimer’s disease.

Measuring LA, especially alongside omega-3 levels, offers valuable insight into how diet and lifestyle may be influencing systemic inflammation and immune function.

- **High LA levels** : Often linked to a diet high in processed and refined oils. This may increase the production of oxidative byproducts and contribute to chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and conditions like cardiovascular disease or metabolic syndrome.
- **Low LA levels** : May impair skin health by reducing ceramide production, leading to dryness, a weakened skin barrier, and increased susceptibility to infections or irritants. LA deficiency may also impact immune function.

**Summary**

LA is an essential omega-6 fatty acid that affects inflammation. Measuring LA levels is important because it helps find imbalances that can lead to chronic diseases, allowing for dietary adjustments to support better health and balance.

## Summary

Linoleic acid (LA) levels within range indicate that the body is getting an adequate, but not excessive, amount of this essential fatty acid, supporting healthy immune signaling, skin barrier function, and energy metabolism. In-range levels also suggest a balanced intake of dietary fats and reflect stable inflammatory regulation when considered alongside other fatty acids like omega-3s.

To keep LA within a healthy range, prioritize whole-food sources like seeds, nuts, and avocados while minimizing highly processed oils. Supporting a healthy omega-6 to omega-3 ratio through regular consumption of fatty fish or high-quality omega-3 supplements helps buffer pro-inflammatory pathways. Antioxidants like vitamin E can protect LA within cell membranes from oxidative damage.

## Foods to eat

**Foods that support healthy LA levels:**

- Algae
- Anchovies
- Cod liver (not oil)
- Egg yolks
- Oysters
- Salmon
- Sardines
- Soybean
- Tuna

**Nuts and seeds:**

- Almonds
- Cashews
- Chia seeds
- Flaxseeds
- Sunflower seeds
- Walnuts

## Foods to limit

In general, omega balance can be helped by avoiding refined, hydrogenated fats and oils, solid fats (like butter), and plant-based products with hydrogenated oils (like alternative milks), which are often used to create a silky and smooth texture. Check food labels for these ingredients, as they are in many processed foods.

- Canola oil
- Fried foods
- Margarine
- Safflower oil
- Shortening
- Soybean oil
- Sunflower oil

## Supplements

Supplements with a well-balanced EPA+DPA help support healthy omega levels. If taking supplements to support omega health, look for a reputable supplement maker that certifies its products are free of mercury and other contaminants. It’s also important to check if the brand processes it in a way that prevents the oils from oxidizing.

- Omega-3 Fatty Acids EPA/DHA
