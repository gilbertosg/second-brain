## Why it matters

Triglycerides are a type of fat found in the blood. When we eat, the body converts any extra calories, particularly from carbohydrates and sugar, into triglycerides and stores them in the liver and fat cells. Later, between meals, hormones release these fats so your body can use them for energy. However, when your blood consistently has too many triglycerides, it can signal that something is out of balance. Think of triglycerides as stored energy. If you're always storing and never using that energy because of too much food, too little movement, or insulin resistance, it starts to build up.

High triglycerides often show up when insulin is not working well, or what is known as insulin resistance, which is common in prediabetes and type 2 diabetes. Insulin resistance typically occurs due to the overconsumption of refined carbohydrates, sugars, and starches. Elevated triglyceride levels are a key component of metabolic syndrome, a cluster of conditions that increases the risk of heart disease, stroke, and type 2 diabetes. Triglyceride levels are very responsive to diet and lifestyle modifications and can help guide decisions around nutrition, weight management, inflammation, and cardiovascular risk.

In summary:

- **High triglycerides:** Often linked to insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, obesity, fatty liver disease, inflammation, and a higher risk of heart attack or stroke. The triglyceride-to-HDL ratio (ideally 1:1) is a good indicator of heart disease risk alongside elevated triglyceride levels. A ratio above 2:1 raises risk, with a 5:1 ratio strongly linked to heart disease, insulin resistance, and prediabetes.
- **Very high triglycerides (over 500 mg/dL):** Raise the risk of pancreatitis, a painful and potentially dangerous inflammation of the pancreas.
- **Low triglycerides (under 40 mg/dL):** Can sometimes be seen in people with very low fat diets, malnutrition, hyperthyroidism (overactive thyroid), or some genetic conditions. While not as concerning as high levels, very low triglycerides might suggest the body isn't absorbing nutrients well or lacks enough energy reserves.

Elevated triglycerides are best understood in the context of comprehensive lipid and metabolic markers, which includes small and large low-density lipoprotein (LDL), LDL particle number, LDL pattern, HDL (the “good” kind that typically helps clear extra cholesterol away), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), and lipoprotein(a). It is a useful marker to help detect early warning signs of heart disease and metabolic issues to guide clinical decisions around diet, lifestyle, and medical interventions, if needed.

Optimizing HDL cholesterol is also important in the context of elevated triglycerides, as larger, more buoyant HDL particles are protective and can help reduce any associated risks. Smaller, more dense high-density lipoprotein (HDL) particles are often caused by insulin resistance, prediabetes, and type 2 diabetes, and increase the risk of heart disease, and may be seen alongside elevated triglycerides. In addition to the size and quality of HDL particles, there are other tests to consider when lipid markers point to an increased risk. Advanced tests like the pCAD score and pCEC can together capture one of HDL’s most important protective roles and reveal cardiovascular risk factors that standard HDL cholesterol measurements can miss.

Understanding triglyceride levels lets you look past the standard cholesterol numbers and focus on how well your metabolism, blood sugar, and inflammatory systems are working together (or not), and can guide more personalized choices around nutrition, supplements, or medications to support long-term cardiometabolic health.

**Summary**

Triglycerides are a type of fat found in the blood that the body uses for energy. High levels often point to deeper issues like a diet high in refined carbohydrates and sugars, insulin resistance, obesity, and lack of exercise, which can raise the risk for heart disease and other health problems.

**Optimal ranges**

The optimal range for triglyceride levels are as follows:

- **Women:** 20-70 mg/dL
- **Men:** 30-70 mg/dL

Lower triglycerides levels may better protect against heart disease and metabolic issues.

This range isn’t one-size-fits-all. A root cause approach looks at your overall cardiometabolic health by factoring in inflammation, insulin resistance, and family history alongside triglycerides to find the most protective range for you. It highlights why personalized care matters, tailoring health goals to your unique risk factors and health status for better long-term outcomes.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Triglyceride levels can change based on what's happening in your body and lifestyle. Factors like what you eat, how much you exercise, stress levels, blood sugar control, and even how much sleep you get can all influence it. For example, eating a meal high in refined carbs or sugars can temporarily raise triglycerides. Similarly, if you're under a lot of stress or not sleeping well, your body may have more inflammation, which can also shift your triglyceride levels. Because of all these influences, your levels can fluctuate slightly from day to day or week to week. That's why it's important to look at trends over time, not just a single test result, and to understand the bigger picture of your heart and metabolic health.

## Summary

A triglyceride level in the normal range indicates that the body is efficiently converting excess calories into fat and using that fat as needed for energy. It suggests a healthy balance between energy storage and metabolism, and is often a reflection of good insulin sensitivity, stable blood sugar, and liver health. Triglycerides in the normal range are associated with a lower risk of heart disease, stroke, and metabolic disorders.

From a root cause perspective, even a normal level of triglycerides is worth evaluating to support optimal cardiometabolic fitness. A comprehensive assessment of advanced lipid biomarkers, alongside inflammation markers like hs-CRP and metabolic markers like fasting glucose and insulin, may be beneficial. Nutrition strategies can include eating whole, unprocessed, anti-inflammatory foods rich in healthy fats (like omega-3s from wild-caught fish and alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) from flax or chia seeds), leafy greens, nuts, and seeds to help regulate healthy fat and sugar metabolism, while maintaining proper insulin sensitivity. Supportive supplements like omega-3 fatty acids, berberine, and fiber can further support balance. Regular movement, stress management, adequate restorative sleep, and maintaining a healthy weight all help optimal insulin sensitivity.

## Foods to eat

It is helpful to keep triglycerides in check by limiting ultra-processed food, all liquid sugar calories in sugar-sweetened beverages, sugar in any form, and refined carbohydrates.

**Beneficial foods include:**

- Grass-fed, regeneratively- or pasture-raised meats, poultry, and fish (if accessible).
- Good fats, such as olive oil, avocados, nuts, and seeds.
- Vegetables (all except for starchy root vegetables, like potatoes, which are best eaten in moderation).
- High-fiber, prebiotic, and probiotic foods are helpful to the microbiome.

**Time-restricted eating is also helpful in improving metabolic control:**

- Avoid eating three hours before bed.
- Leave at least 12 hours—ideally 14 to 16 hours—between dinner and breakfast.

**Order of eating foods:**

- Eat vegetables, fat, and protein before any carbohydrates.
- Start the day with protein and fat for breakfast. Try reducing or avoiding carbohydrates or sugar at breakfast.
- Avoid bread, alcohol, or any refined carbohydrate foods before eating vegetables, fat, or protein in a meal.

## Foods to limit

All ultra-processed food, all liquid sugar calories in sugar-sweetened beverages, sugar in any form, and refined carbohydrates can impact triglycerides and the rest of the lipid profile. So, to stay within a healthy range, it’s best to consider reducing them.
