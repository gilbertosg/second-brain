## Why it matters

Insulin is a peptide hormone (a small protein) essential for regulating blood glucose levels. Glucose, a sugar derived from carbohydrates such as fruits, grains, legumes, and starchy vegetables, is the body’s primary source of energy.

After carbohydrates are digested, glucose and fructose are absorbed through the intestines and enter the bloodstream. As blood glucose levels rise, the pancreas releases insulin, which helps transport glucose into cells to be used for energy or stored for later use. Insulin acts like a key, unlocking cells so glucose can enter. It regulates glucose uptake in muscle, liver, and fat tissue and suppresses new glucose production from the liver.

In addition to managing blood sugar, insulin also affects how the body uses and stores fats and proteins, helping to keep overall metabolism in balance. Insulin also plays a wide range of roles within the body, including the brain, and helps support cognition, neuronal plasticity, and certain glandular functions.

Measuring fasting insulin levels can give an early picture of how well the body is controlling blood sugar—sometimes long before glucose levels become abnormal. When fasting insulin is high but blood sugar is still normal, it may be a sign of insulin resistance, meaning the body needs extra insulin to keep sugar in check. This can go on for years before prediabetes or type 2 diabetes may show up in standard tests.

In the early stages of insulin resistance, the pancreas often responds to the body's demand by secreting more insulin. Glucose levels may stay in the normal range during this phase, masking the problem. Over time, if insulin resistance worsens or pancreatic beta cells begin to fail, glucose levels begin to rise, marking the onset of prediabetes, diagnosed when fasting glucose reaches 100-125 mg/dL, or type 2 diabetes, diagnosed when fasting glucose exceeds 126 mg/dL. Elevated insulin is often present long before elevated glucose appears, making it a valuable early biomarker for chronic metabolic stress. On the other hand, low insulin may suggest that the body isn’t making enough, as seen in type 1 diabetes or other conditions that affect the pancreas.

Chronically elevated insulin levels—even when blood glucose remains normal—are increasingly recognized as significant risk factors for a range of chronic diseases. Hyperinsulinemia has been linked to:

- Cardiovascular diseases, including high blood pressure, heart attacks, and strokes, through mechanisms such as endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and vascular stiffness.
- Abnormal insulin levels, which over time may impact the ways in which cells reproduce, potentially increasing the risk for certain abnormal cell growth patterns or cancer.
- Neurodegenerative conditions like Alzheimer’s disease, where insulin resistance impairs glucose metabolism in the brain, contributing to what some researchers have termed “type 3 diabetes”.

Understanding insulin dynamics is important not only for preventing diabetes but also for identifying early metabolic stress that contributes to many chronic diseases long before blood sugar becomes abnormal. Interpreting fasting insulin values provides valuable insights:

- **Elevated fasting insulin** often points to insulin resistance, which is linked to conditions like obesity, metabolic syndrome, and an increased risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease.
- **Below-range fasting insulin** may indicate issues like pancreatic dysfunction, as seen in type 1 diabetes, where the body struggles to produce enough insulin.

The optimal range for *fasting insulin* levels is generally considered to be below 5 µIU/mL. Some practitioners may aim for a narrower range of insulin levels, around 2-5 uIU/mL, to ensure optimal metabolic health.

## Causes

**Root causes of elevated fasting insulin**

Elevated fasting insulin **above the optimal range** often stems from underlying factors such as insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, excess body fat, hormonal imbalances, and poor glucose metabolism. Identifying these root causes can help address the factors driving elevated insulin levels and restore metabolic balance.

**Diet and lifestyle factors**

- **Poor diet** : High intake of refined and processed carbohydrates, sugary foods, and processed foods can lead to insulin resistance and elevated insulin levels.
- **Chronic stress** : Prolonged stress can cause hormonal imbalances that contribute to insulin resistance.
- **Sleep deprivation** : Poor sleep quality and insufficient sleep can disrupt insulin regulation.
- **Obesity** : Excess body fat, particularly around the abdomen, is closely linked to insulin resistance.
- **Sedentary lifestyle** : Lack of physical activity reduces muscle glucose uptake, leading to higher insulin levels.

**Other factors**

- **Insulin resistance/metabolic syndrome** : When cells become less responsive to insulin, the pancreas produces more insulin to compensate.
- **Chronic inflammation** : Persistent inflammation disrupts insulin signaling, contributing to elevated insulin levels.
- **Gut health imbalances** : Dysbiosis, or an imbalance in gut bacteria, can promote inflammation and affect glucose metabolism.
- **Oxidative stress** : Excess free radicals damage cells, impairing insulin signaling and increasing insulin levels.
- **Excess body fat** : Visceral fat, especially around the abdomen, releases inflammatory compounds that worsen insulin resistance.
- **Hormonal imbalances** : Elevated cortisol (from chronic stress or Cushing disease) and excess androgens (as seen in PCOS) can interfere with insulin regulation.
- **Nutrient imbalances** : Deficiencies in magnesium, chromium, and vitamin D may reduce insulin sensitivity.
- **Genetics** : Genetic predispositions can influence insulin signaling pathways and increase susceptibility to insulin resistance.
- **Insulinoma** : A rare, benign insulin-producing tumor.

**Medications**

Certain medications can contribute to elevated insulin levels and insulin resistance as side effects. For example:

- **Corticosteroids** : These drugs, used for inflammatory conditions, can increase blood sugar and insulin levels by causing insulin resistance and increasing glucose production. Corticosteroids increase glucose output from the liver and reduce glucose uptake by cells, leading to higher insulin levels.
- **Antipsychotics** : Some antipsychotic medications can cause weight gain and insulin resistance by affecting glucose metabolism and appetite.
- **Beta-blockers** : These medications for heart conditions can interfere with insulin regulation and increase blood sugar levels.
- **Oral contraceptives** : Can modestly raise glucose and insulin levels by affecting liver metabolism and hormone balance, potentially reducing insulin sensitivity and increasing insulin production in response to glucose.
- **Sulfonylureas (used for treating type 2 diabetes)** : Stimulate the pancreas to release more insulin, which can lead to higher insulin levels, especially if glucose levels are not elevated or over-secretion occurs.

**Environmental toxin exposure**

Exposure to various environmental pollutants and chemicals has been linked to metabolic disturbances and insulin resistance.

- **Air pollution** : Particulate matter and other pollutants can cause inflammation, leading to insulin resistance by disrupting insulin signaling pathways.
- **Heavy metals** : Exposure to metals like lead and mercury can disrupt insulin signaling and glucose metabolism.
- **Pesticides** : Certain pesticides can interfere with metabolic processes and increase insulin resistance.
- **Volatile organic compounds (VOCs)** : Exposure to VOCs can cause oxidative stress and inflammation, disrupting insulin function.

Visit [Environmental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/environmental-health-101) for more information about these toxins, their impact, and general guidance.

**Substance use**

Substance use can affect insulin levels and metabolic health.

- **Alcohol** : Chronic alcohol consumption can lead to insulin resistance by causing liver damage and disrupting glucose metabolism.
- **Tobacco and secondhand smoke** : Smoking can contribute to insulin resistance by increasing inflammation and oxidative stress.
- **Cocaine** : Cocaine use raises stress hormones like cortisol, which can lead to higher blood sugar levels and increased insulin production.

## Associated conditions

Elevated insulin levels **above the optimal range** are often linked to various health conditions, primarily related to metabolic health. Here is an overview of some common conditions and systems associated with elevated insulin levels.

**General conditions and diseases**

**Metabolic conditions**

- **Insulin resistance** : Insulin resistance is a condition where the body’s cells become less responsive to insulin, causing elevated blood sugar levels. Over time, it can lead to type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and other metabolic disorders.
- **Type 2 diabetes** : Elevated insulin levels frequently precede the development of type 2 diabetes due to insulin resistance.
- **Metabolic syndrome** : This condition includes a group of risk factors such as high blood pressure, high blood sugar, excess body fat around the waist, and abnormal cholesterol levels.
- **Obesity** : High insulin levels can encourage fat storage and weight gain, especially around the abdomen.
- **Cardiovascular diseases** : Insulin resistance and high insulin levels are risk factors for heart disease and atherosclerosis.
- **Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)** : Those with PCOS often have elevated insulin levels, which can worsen symptoms of the condition.
- **Cognitive decline** : Insulin resistance may impair how the brain uses glucose, contribute to inflammation, and promote the buildup of abnormal proteins associated with dementia, increasing the risk of cognitive decline and dementia, including Alzheimer’s disease.

**Liver diseases**

- **Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)** : Elevated insulin levels can lead to fat buildup in the liver.
- **Non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)** : This severe form of NAFLD can cause liver inflammation and damage.

**Other medical conditions**

- **Insulinoma** : A rare pancreatic tumor that produces excess insulin.
- **Acromegaly** : A hormonal disorder leading to increased insulin levels due to excess growth hormone.
- **Cushing's syndrome** : A condition caused by prolonged high levels of cortisol, which can elevate insulin levels.

**Mental health conditions**

Elevated insulin levels can also be linked to certain mental health conditions:

- **Depression** : Insulin resistance has been associated with a higher risk of depression.
- **Anxiety** : Insulin resistance can also be linked to increased anxiety symptoms.

## Foods to eat

Nutrition plays a key role in metabolic health. Visit [Nutrition 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/nutrition-101) for a science-based overview of foundational nutritional principles that support health. It is founded on three core principles:

- **Whole, real food** : Eat real, whole, or minimally processed foods and avoid ultra-processed foods.
- **Food is medicine** : All food constituents, including protein, fat, carbohydrates, fiber, phytochemicals, vitamins, and minerals, regulate every process and cellular function in the body.
- **Personalization** : Age, sex, genetics, preferences, health conditions, and individual goals should guide the optimal diet for every unique individual.

Food is important for managing and potentially reversing elevated insulin levels. Choosing the right foods supports metabolic health and improves insulin sensitivity.

- **Anti-inflammatory foods** : Certain foods may help manage the inflammatory processes involved in autoimmune conditions like type 1 diabetes. Foods rich in omega-3 fatty acids, such as fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines), flaxseeds, and walnuts, along with colorful fruits and vegetables high in antioxidants (like berries, leafy greens, and peppers), may help support immune function and inflammatory balance.
  - **Leafy greens** : Leafy greens like spinach, kale, and Swiss chard are rich in fiber, magnesium, and antioxidants, which help improve insulin sensitivity and reduce oxidative stress.
  - **Cruciferous vegetables** : Cruciferous vegetables like broccoli, Brussels sprouts, kale, and cauliflower contain glucosinolates, which support detoxification and help support healthy insulin balance.
  - **Berries** : Blueberries, strawberries, and raspberries are rich in anthocyanins and fiber, which help maintain healthy blood sugar and insulin balance.
  - **Nuts and seeds** : Almonds, walnuts, and chia seeds are high in healthy fats, fiber, and magnesium, all of which support insulin sensitivity, inflammatory balance, and promote metabolic health.
  - **Whole grains** : Quinoa and brown rice are rich in fiber and nutrients that help stabilize blood sugar levels and support healthy insulin balance.
  - **Legumes** : Beans, lentils, and chickpeas contain dietary fiber and protein, which help stabilize blood sugar and support insulin balance.
- **Balancing macronutrients** : Consuming a mix of carbohydrates, protein, and healthy fats may help stabilize blood sugar levels and prevent spikes and crashes. Whole food unrefined carbohydrates such as whole grains, beans, seeds, and starchy veggies like sweet potatoes and butternut squash provide a steady release of glucose into the bloodstream. Protein and healthy fats, such as those found in lean meats, fish, nuts, seeds, and avocados, can slow the absorption of glucose and help maintain stable insulin levels. Meats, poultry, and fish from grass-fed, regeneratively farmed, or pasture-raised sources, when accessible, may provide high-quality nutrients.
- **Low glycemic index (GI) foods** : Choosing low glycemic index foods, which cause a slower and more gradual increase in blood sugar, may help support insulin levels and reduce the body's demand for insulin. Foods such as non-starchy vegetables (leafy greens, broccoli, bell peppers, etc.), whole grains, berries, and nuts have lower glycemic indexes and do not cause rapid blood sugar spikes.
- **High fiber intake** : Dietary fiber, particularly soluble fiber found in foods like oats, legumes, fruits, and vegetables, can slow down the absorption of sugar into the bloodstream, helping to maintain steady blood glucose levels and reducing the demand for insulin. Dietary fiber also supports gut health, which can impact metabolism and inflammation levels.
- **Nutrient-dense foods** : Ensuring adequate intake of key nutrients that support insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism, such as magnesium, chromium, zinc, and vitamin D, can be helpful. Magnesium-rich foods like leafy greens, nuts, seeds, and whole grains, as well as foods high in zinc like shellfish, meat, and legumes, may support insulin function and metabolic health.
- **Supporting gut health** : A healthy gut microbiome is linked to improved glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity. Including fermented foods such as yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi, and foods high in prebiotic fiber, like garlic, onions, and asparagus, can help maintain a diverse and healthy gut microbiome.
- **Beneficial spices and foods** : Ingredients like bitter melon, fenugreek, ginger, and turmeric have the potential to support glucose control.
- **Ketogenic diet** : This diet, which emphasizes low carbohydrates and higher fat, may improve insulin sensitivity in some individuals but is best done under medical guidance.
- **Time-restricted eating** : Leaving at least 12-16 hours between dinner and breakfast and avoiding meals within three hours of bedtime may support blood sugar stability.
- **Food order during meals:** Eating vegetables, fats, and proteins before carbohydrates may reduce blood sugar spikes. Starting the day with protein and fat, while avoiding processed and refined carbohydrates or sugary foods for breakfast, can further support stable blood sugar levels.

## Foods to limit

Understanding which foods impact insulin can help reduce the risk of developing insulin resistance and improve metabolic health.

- **Sugary foods and beverages:** Soft drinks, candies, pastries, sweetened cereals, and energy drinks cause blood glucose spikes and increased insulin secretion, leading to insulin resistance over time.
- **Refined grains:** White bread, white rice, pastries, and refined pasta are high in simple carbohydrates and low in fiber, contributing to insulin resistance and inflammatory imbalance.
- **Trans fats and hydrogenated oils** : Margarine, partially hydrogenated oils, and many commercially baked goods may contribute to inflammatory imbalance and oxidative stress, potentially impairing insulin sensitivity.
- **Processed foods and fast foods** : Fast-food meats, french fries, and processed packaged foods are often high in unhealthy fats, sugars, and refined carbohydrates, potentially promoting inflammation imbalances and insulin resistance.

Avoiding these foods can help manage elevated insulin levels by reducing inflammation, oxidative stress, and blood sugar levels. For more information, visit [Nutrition 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/nutrition-101).

## Symptoms

Elevated insulin levels **above the optimal range** can affect various systems in the body, leading to a range of symptoms. Recognizing these symptoms can help identify underlying issues related to insulin resistance and metabolic health. Some classic symptoms of elevated insulin include:

- **Increased hunger (polyphagia)** : Persistent hunger even after eating can be a sign of high insulin levels, as the body struggles to regulate blood sugar effectively.
- **Weight gain** : Excess insulin can lead to fat storage, particularly around the abdomen, contributing to weight gain and difficulty losing weight. Fat stored in this area also tends to have more inflammatory cytokines, which contribute to insulin resistance.
- **Fatigue** : Elevated insulin levels can cause fluctuations in blood sugar, leading to feelings of tiredness and low energy.
- **Frequent urination (polyuria)** : High insulin can affect kidney function, leading to increased urination.
- **Dark patches on the skin (acanthosis nigricans)** : This skin condition, often seen in body folds, can be a marker of insulin resistance.
- **Cravings for sugary foods** : High insulin can cause blood sugar drops, triggering cravings for sweets and carbohydrates.
- **Difficulty concentrating** : Fluctuations in blood sugar levels can affect brain function, leading to issues with focus and concentration.

## Self care

Effective self-care involves lifestyle changes and healthy habits that can help manage insulin levels and support metabolic health.

**Exercise and movement**

Regular physical activity positively impacts fasting insulin by improving insulin sensitivity, helping to balance inflammation, and enhancing the muscles' uptake of glucose. Exercise increases the ability of muscle cells to absorb glucose from the bloodstream, lowering blood sugar levels and reducing the need for excess insulin. It also supports a healthy inflammatory response by helping decrease levels of inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein, which are closely tied to insulin resistance. Enhanced insulin receptor sensitivity in muscle cells further supports efficient glucose metabolism and helps address elevated fasting insulin. Additionally, exercise supports weight management, which may help prevent and manage insulin resistance.

Different types of exercise provide specific benefits for reducing fasting insulin levels. Cardiovascular activities like walking, cycling, and jogging support healthy insulin sensitivity and help support weight control. Strength training, such as weightlifting or resistance band exercises, may help build muscle mass, which may in turn help increase glucose uptake and improve insulin regulation. High-intensity interval training (HIIT) has been shown to significantly improve insulin sensitivity and help reduce fat mass, while flexibility-focused activities like yoga help reduce stress, which can improve hormonal balance and insulin function. Together, these exercise strategies offer comprehensive support for managing elevated fasting insulin and promoting metabolic health.

For more detailed information on exercise, please refer to [Exercise 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/exercise-101).

**Weight management**

Maintaining a healthy weight is important for managing insulin levels and improving metabolic health. If living with excess weight or obesity, weight loss can reduce visceral fat, a key contributor to insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome, often helping to improve the body's response to insulin and helping lower blood glucose levels over time. Managing weight also helps balance inflammation and insulin sensitivity, reducing the risk of conditions like metabolic syndrome while supporting metabolic health. Additionally, maintaining a healthy weight can help regulate other markers of metabolic health, such as blood pressure, cholesterol, and triglycerides, which are often elevated in insulin-resistant states.

Being underweight can also contribute to elevated insulin levels due to factors like malnutrition, poor diet, or chronic stress. These factors may impair insulin effectiveness, prompting the pancreas to overproduce insulin to stabilize blood sugar. Addressing elevated insulin levels in underweight individuals involves identifying underlying metabolic imbalances, supporting nutrient sufficiency, managing stress, and addressing contributing health conditions to promote balanced insulin function.

Poor sleep quality or short sleep duration can disrupt glucose metabolism, causing blood sugar fluctuations that may lead to insulin resistance. Consistent, high-quality sleep helps stabilize blood sugar and supports metabolic function.

Lack of sleep can also disrupt hunger-regulating hormones, increasing appetite and the risk of weight gain, which contributes to insulin resistance. Restorative sleep promotes weight management and helps balance inflammatory responses, both of which are critical for maintaining healthy insulin levels and preventing conditions like type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome.

**Mental health and stress reduction**

Chronic stress triggers the release of hormones like cortisol and adrenaline, which increase glucose production and decrease insulin sensitivity. Effective stress management practices, such as mindfulness, have been shown to reduce cortisol levels, improve insulin sensitivity, and support metabolic health, especially in conditions like type 2 diabetes and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.

## Additional tests

**Root cause approach to diagnosing elevated fasting insulin**

A root cause approach to diagnosing elevated fasting insulin focuses on identifying underlying factors such as insulin resistance, inflammation, hormonal imbalances, nutrient deficiencies, and environmental toxin exposure to guide targeted evaluation and treatment.

The need for specific tests depends on your individual risk profile, clinical symptoms, family history, and the presence of other health conditions, as determined by your healthcare provider. *Here is a list of tests that may be relevant if fasting insulin levels are out of range, with those available through your Function membership noted below.*

**Blood testing**

Laboratory assessment for diagnosing insulin resistance aims to evaluate how effectively the body processes glucose and how insulin functions. This involves measuring various biomarkers to understand glucose metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and related metabolic processes.

- **Fasting blood glucose:** Helps to assess initial metabolic status.
- **C-peptide** : C-peptide levels assess insulin production. High C-peptide levels with elevated insulin suggest excessive insulin secretion, often seen in insulin resistance. Differentiates between high fasting insulin due to endogenous overproduction or exogenous insulin use, often alongside autoimmune markers.
- **Homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR)** : The HOMA-IR is calculated from fasting glucose and insulin values to provide a more detailed understanding of insulin sensitivity. It provides a simple snapshot of how well the body is maintaining glucose and insulin balance.
- **Insulin resistance score** : An insulin resistance score is a valuable tool for assessing insulin resistance (IR) and identifying the risk of prediabetes and diabetes. It combines measurements of insulin and C-peptide from a single fasting blood sample, offering insights into how well the body processes glucose and insulin​. This score is especially useful for detecting IR in individuals who might have normal glucose and HbA1c levels but are still at risk due to underlying metabolic issues​.
- **Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c)** : This test provides a long-term view of blood sugar levels by measuring the average glucose concentration over the past 2-3 months. Elevated HbA1c alongside high insulin can suggest insulin resistance and a higher risk for type 2 diabetes.
- **Advanced lipid biomarker assessment:** Helps in assessing the risk of cardiovascular diseases more accurately than a standard lipid panel, and includes total cholesterol/HDL ratio, HDL large, LDL cholesterol, LDL particle number, LDL peak size, lipoprotein (a), total cholesterol, apolipoprotein B (ApoB), HDL cholesterol, LDL medium, LDL pattern, LDL small, non-HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides.
- **Liver function** : Elevated insulin is often associated with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Liver enzymes such as ALT and AST may be tested to assess liver health.
- **Leptin** : Measures leptin levels to understand appetite regulation and its relationship with insulin resistance.
- **Adiponectin** : Evaluates levels of adiponectin, a hormone involved in glucose regulation and insulin sensitivity.
- **Thyroid function** : Since thyroid dysfunction can affect metabolic health and insulin sensitivity, checking TSH, T3, and T4 levels may help identify contributing factors.
- **Uric acid** : Elevated uric acid levels are associated with insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome, as high insulin can reduce the kidneys' ability to excrete uric acid, increasing the risk of gout, kidney stones, and cardiovascular complications.
- **Amylase and lipase:** Elevated insulin can sometimes indicate the pancreas is working too hard or is inflamed. Checking amylase and lipase helps rule out conditions like pancreatitis (inflammation of the pancreas) or tumors causing insulin overproduction.

**Urine testing**

- **Urine microalbumin** : Screens for early kidney damage linked to elevated insulin and metabolic dysfunction.
- **Urine cortisol metabolites** : Evaluates stress hormone output, as chronic stress and elevated cortisol can contribute to insulin resistance.
- **24-Hour urine collection** : A test to measure various substances in the urine over 24 hours, including glucose, which can help in diagnosing diabetes or kidney function issues.

**Diagnostic imaging (CT, MRI, ultrasound, etc.)**

- **Echocardiogram (ECHO)** : An imaging test that uses sound waves to create a picture of the heart’s structure and function. It can help detect heart disease, which may be related to insulin resistance.
- **Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)** : A non-invasive imaging technique to visualize internal organs and tissues. MRI can be used to evaluate conditions such as fatty liver disease or pancreatic abnormalities.
- **Computed tomography (CT)** : An imaging method that provides detailed cross-sectional images of the body. It can help identify liver fat content, pancreatic tumors, or other structural abnormalities.
- **Endoscopy** : A procedure using a flexible tube with a camera to examine the gastrointestinal tract. It can be used to detect gastrointestinal issues like inflammation or ulcers, which may be linked to metabolic syndrome.
- **Colonoscopy** : An examination of the colon and rectum using a flexible tube with a camera. It is used to screen for colorectal cancer or polyps, which can be influenced by insulin resistance.
- **Ultrasound** : An imaging test that uses sound waves to create images of internal organs. It is commonly used to assess liver health for conditions such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD).
- **Barium swallow** : An X-ray exam of the esophagus and stomach using a contrast medium. It can help diagnose swallowing disorders and other gastrointestinal problems.

**Nutrient imbalances**

- **Vitamin D** : Linked to insulin sensitivity and metabolic health.
- **Magnesium** : Supports insulin sensitivity and regulates glucose metabolism.
- **Chromium** : Plays a role in improving glucose tolerance and supporting insulin action.
- **Omega-3 fatty acids** : Helps support inflammatory pathways and improve insulin sensitivity.
- **Zinc** : Supports insulin storage and secretion while reducing oxidative stress in pancreatic cells.

**Microbiome and gut health assessment**

- **Comprehensive stool analysis** : Assesses the balance between beneficial and harmful bacteria in the gut. Looks for markers of dysbiosis, yeast overgrowth, or parasitic infections. Stool testing can also evaluate gut permeability ("leaky gut") by measuring zonulin levels, which indicate whether inflammatory triggers are escaping the gut and contributing to systemic inflammation that can influence insulin resistance and metabolic health.
- **Gut microbiome sequencing** : Identifies specific bacteria, such as *Akkermansia muciniphila* and *Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes* ratios, that are linked to insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism.
- **Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)** : Measures SCFAs like butyrate, which are gut bacterial byproducts that improve insulin sensitivity and reduce inflammation.
- **Zonulin levels** : Assesses intestinal permeability ("leaky gut"), which can contribute to systemic inflammation and exacerbate insulin resistance.
- **LPS (lipopolysaccharides)** : Detects bacterial endotoxins associated with gut dysbiosis and systemic inflammation, both of which can impair insulin regulation.
- **Calprotectin** : Identifies gut inflammation, which may disrupt glucose metabolism and worsen insulin resistance.
- **Secretory IgA** : Evaluates gut immune function, which influences the microbiome’s role in regulating blood sugar and insulin sensitivity

See [Gut Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/gut-health-101).

**Immune system evaluation**

- **Inflammatory markers:** Measures markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) to gauge the level of systemic inflammation.
- **Cytokine panel** : Assesses levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-alpha and IL-6, which are linked to insulin resistance and chronic inflammation.
- **Food sensitivity testing** : Identifies potential sensitivities to foods that may trigger gut inflammation, contributing to systemic inflammation and insulin dysregulation.
- **Allergy testing (IgE panel)** : Detects food or environmental allergens that can lead to immune activation and exacerbate inflammatory processes related to insulin resistance.
- **Autoimmunity** : Testing for autoantibodies can help evaluate how autoimmune activity influences other biomarkers by identifying immune dysregulation, inflammation, or tissue-specific damage that may affect health. In addition to antinuclear antibodies, some relevant autoantibodies include:
  - **GAD antibodies (glutamic acid decarboxylase)** : Detects autoimmune activity targeting pancreatic beta cells, potentially linked to insulin dysregulation.
  - **Islet cell antibodies** : Identifies autoimmune reactions against insulin-producing cells, relevant in conditions like LADA (latent autoimmune diabetes in adults).
  - **Insulin autoantibodies** : Assesses the presence of antibodies directed at insulin, which may contribute to abnormal fasting insulin levels.
  - **Thyroid antibodies (TPO and TgAb)** : Screens for coexisting autoimmune thyroid conditions, which may influence metabolic health and insulin regulation.

**Environmental and toxin testing**

- **Mycotoxins** : Identifies mold toxins that may contribute to inflammation and insulin resistance.
- **Heavy metals** : Screens for metals like mercury, lead, and arsenic, which can impair insulin signaling and glucose metabolism.
- **Pesticide residues** : Detects exposure to organophosphates and other pesticides that may act as endocrine disruptors and affect insulin regulation.
- **BPA and bisphenol analogs** : Measures bisphenol exposure, linked to insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction.
- **Phthalate metabolites** : Evaluates exposure to phthalates, which can disrupt hormonal balance and worsen insulin resistance.
- **PFAS** : Assesses levels of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, associated with impaired metabolic and insulin function.
- **Volatile organic compounds (VOCs)** : Screens for industrial solvents that may interfere with glucose metabolism and contribute to insulin resistance.
- **Glyphosate** : Identifies exposure to this common herbicide, which may influence gut health and systemic inflammation affecting insulin sensitivity.

**Advanced diagnostics**

- **Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)** : This measures the body’s response to glucose over time and can identify impaired glucose tolerance or early signs of insulin resistance.
- **Oxidative stress markers** : Oxidative stress impairs insulin signaling and promotes inflammation, both of which disrupt the body’s ability to regulate glucose effectively. Evaluate oxidative stress through tests like urinary 8-isoprostane or plasma malondialdehyde (MDA), which help gauge the level of oxidative damage in the body.
- **Organic acids testing** : Detects imbalances in microbial byproducts, mitochondrial dysfunction, and nutritional deficiencies that contribute to dysbiosis and systemic oxidative stress.
- **Cortisol testing** : Measures cortisol levels throughout the day to assess adrenal function and the impact of chronic stress on metabolism and immune function. Elevated cortisol can contribute to insulin resistance through its effect on glucose metabolism.
- **Genetic testing** : Genetic tests can identify predispositions to metabolic disorders or specific genetic variations that affect insulin sensitivity. Understanding genetic risk factors can guide personalized treatment plans.

**Cognitive assessments**

Elevated fasting insulin can negatively impact the brain and cognition by contributing to insulin resistance in brain cells, which disrupts glucose metabolism and reduces energy availability for neural functions. This can impair memory, focus, and processing speed and may increase the risk of neurodegenerative conditions like Alzheimer’s disease, often referred to as "type 3 diabetes." Chronic inflammation and oxidative stress associated with elevated insulin further exacerbate cognitive decline by damaging neurons and disrupting brain signaling pathways. Cognitive testing can help by identifying specific areas of impairment, such as memory or executive function, providing insights into how metabolic imbalances affect brain health, and guiding targeted interventions to improve both cognitive and metabolic outcomes.

For more information about maintaining optimum brain health, including cognition, memory, and ways to reduce the risk of age-related neurodegenerative disorders, visit [Brain Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/brain-health-101).

## Follow up

**Medical interventions**

Collaboration with your healthcare provider ensures a personalized approach to managing elevated fasting insulin levels. Consideration of all symptoms, laboratory test results, and lifestyle factors can guide a treatment plan tailored to your specific needs and health goals.

- **Confirming insulin resistance:** Conducting further testing, such as an insulin resistance score, oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) with insulin measurements, and HbA1c, to help determine the extent of insulin resistance and glucose management issues.
- **Evaluating metabolic health:** Assessing other biomarkers associated with insulin resistance, such as a lipid panel (to check for dyslipidemia), liver function tests (to assess for non-alcoholic fatty liver disease or NAFLD), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) for inflammation levels, looks at potentially related issues. Measuring C-peptide levels can differentiate between endogenous insulin production and exogenous sources of insulin.
- **Lifestyle modifications:**
  - Dietary approaches are essential, focusing on a low-glycemic diet, rich in dietary fiber, healthy fats, and lean protein to improve insulin sensitivity and reduce glucose spikes.
  - Regular exercise, especially cardiovascular and resistance training, can help improve insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake in muscle cells.
- **Addressing underlying causes** : If necessary, investigating other contributors such as thyroid function and chronic inflammation, can be helpful, as these may worsen insulin resistance. Stress management strategies, such as mindfulness, yoga, or meditation, can help improve the body’s response to stress that may negatively affect insulin sensitivity.
- **Medical interventions:** In certain cases, medications such as metformin, which improves insulin sensitivity, or GLP-1 receptor agonists, which help regulate glucose levels and insulin function, may be used. Continuous monitoring and follow-up testing are essential to evaluate the effectiveness of interventions and to adjust strategies accordingly.
- **Regular monitoring** : Follow-up testing can track insulin, glucose, and other relevant biomarkers to ensure that interventions are improving insulin dynamics and metabolic health.

This comprehensive approach helps address the root causes of elevated insulin and prevents further metabolic complications, such as type 2 diabetes or cardiovascular disease.

**Medications**

Elevated insulin levels are often linked to metabolic conditions and diseases. Here are some common medications prescribed for these conditions:

**Type 2 diabetes**

- **Metformin** : Lowers blood glucose levels by improving insulin sensitivity and decreasing liver glucose production.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists (e.g., liraglutide, semaglutide)** : Enhance insulin secretion, suppress glucagon release, and promote weight loss.
- **SGLT2 inhibitors (e.g., empagliflozin)** : Increase glucose excretion through the urine, which helps lower blood sugar levels.
- **Insulin therapy (e.g., insulin glargine)** : Provides exogenous insulin to help regulate blood glucose levels when oral medications are insufficient.

**Metabolic syndrome**

- **Statins (e.g., atorvastatin, simvastatin)** : Manage dyslipidemia by helping lower cholesterol levels.
- **Antihypertensives (e.g., beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors)** : Help normalize blood pressure.

**Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)**

- **Pioglitazone** : Improves insulin sensitivity and reduces liver fat content in individuals with NAFLD.
- **Vitamin E** : Acts as an antioxidant to reduce liver inflammation and oxidative stress associated with NAFLD.
- **N-acetylcysteine (NAC)** : Supports pathways related to inflammatory and antioxidative status in the liver

**Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)**

- **Metformin** : Improves insulin sensitivity and regulates menstrual cycles in those with PCOS.
- **Oral contraceptives (e.g., ethinyl estradiol/levonorgestrel)** : Regulates menstrual cycles and reduces symptoms of hirsutism in women with PCOS.
- **Spironolactone** : Reduces symptoms of hirsutism and acne by blocking androgen receptors.
- **Clomiphene citrate** : Helps stimulate ovulation in those with PCOS who are trying to conceive.

**Cardiovascular disease**

- **Statins (e.g., atorvastatin, simvastatin)** : Lower LDL cholesterol levels and reduce cardiovascular risk.
- **ACE inhibitors (e.g., lisinopril, enalapril)** : Lower blood pressure and reduce the workload on the heart.
- **Calcium channel blockers (CCBs) (e.g., amlodipine, diltiazem)** : Lower blood pressure and manage conditions such as chest pain and irregular heartbeat.

**Procedures**

If diagnosed with a condition related to elevated insulin levels, several procedures and surgical options may be considered as part of care. Here is an overview of common procedures and their purposes for managing these conditions.

**Type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes**

While lifestyle changes and medications like metformin are the typical first-line treatments for type 2 diabetes, more advanced management strategies may be considered in complex or severe cases. These can include:

- **Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM)** : Tracks glucose levels throughout the day for better diabetes management.
- **Insulin pump therapy** : Delivers insulin continuously to manage blood glucose levels.

**Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)**

Persistent high insulin levels can contribute to the development and progression of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), as excess insulin promotes fat buildup in the liver. When NAFLD is suspected, the following tests may be used to evaluate liver health and determine disease severity:

- **CT:** Provides imaging to detect liver fat and assess for signs of advanced liver disease.
- **Liver ultrasound** : Evaluates liver fat accumulation and assesses liver health.
- **Liver biopsy** : Diagnoses the severity of liver inflammation and fibrosis in NAFLD.

**Cardiovascular disease**

- **Coronary angiography** : Diagnoses potential blockages in coronary arteries.
- **Stress echocardiography** : Assesses heart function under the stress of exercise or medication to diagnose coronary artery disease.
- **Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or angioplasty** : Opens narrowed or blocked coronary arteries to improve blood flow to the heart.
- **Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG)** : Surgical procedure to improve blood flow to the heart by redirecting blood around blocked arteries.

**Retesting**

Retesting might be helpful to check how well a management plan is working for conditions associated with elevated insulin levels.

- **After 3-6 months of following lifestyle recommendations** : This is a good time to evaluate whether changes in diet, exercise, and other lifestyle habits are affecting biomarker levels and health.
- **After 3-6 months of starting medication** : This interval allows for an assessment of how well new medications are managing blood glucose levels, insulin resistance, or other factors related to the condition.
- **After 6-12 months of implementing new treatment strategies** : This period is suitable for reviewing the effectiveness of new treatments or interventions and making adjustments as necessary.
- **Annually for ongoing conditions** : For chronic conditions such as type 2 diabetes, regular annual retesting can help ensure that the management plan is still effective and help prevent complications.

In most cases, retesting might be recommended after 3-6 months or annually for long-term management of chronic conditions. It is important to work with a healthcare provider to decide the best timing for retesting based on your health needs and treatment goals.
