## Why it matters

Sodium is an essential electrolyte commonly consumed as part of table salt (sodium chloride). The primary sources of dietary salt include processed foods, premade meals, and salt added during cooking or at the table. Human cells require approximately 0.5 grams of sodium per day to support essential functions.

Measuring sodium levels is important for understanding overall health because sodium plays a central role in many body functions. It helps regulate fluid balance, supports nerve signaling, and enables muscle contractions, including those of the heart. However, imbalances in sodium—whether too high or too low—can contribute to serious health concerns. **This is especially relevant when interpreting lab results, as recent hydration habits can temporarily affect sodium concentration** .

- **Too much sodium** : Excess sodium can increase blood pressure and raise the risk of heart disease and stroke. It strains the kidneys, which must work harder to excrete sodium, potentially accelerating kidney damage or dysfunction. Chronic high sodium intake may also impair liver health by contributing to fluid retention and worsening outcomes in conditions like nonalcoholic fatty liver disease or cirrhosis. Additionally, excess sodium can cause tissue swelling due to water retention and increase the workload on the cardiovascular system..
- **Too little sodium:** Low sodium levels, known as hyponatremia, can disrupt neurological function, leading to headaches, nausea, fatigue, confusion, and muscle cramps. In more severe cases, it may trigger seizures, loss of consciousness, or coma. Chronic low sodium may also impact hormonal regulation, liver metabolism, and adrenal function, especially in the context of underlying illness or medication use.

Several factors influence sodium levels, including dietary intake, hydration, physical activity, medications, and stress. Understanding how these factors affect sodium balance is key to managing this essential mineral. Regular monitoring allows healthcare providers to help prevent or manage conditions such as high blood pressure, cardiovascular disease, liver dysfunction, and kidney disorders..

**Summary**

Sodium is important for controlling fluid balance, nerve function, and muscle contractions, but too much or too little can lead to health problems. Understanding how lifestyle, diet, and other factors influence sodium levels is important for correcting nutrient imbalances related to sodium.

**Optimal ranges**

The optimal range for serum sodium follows the reference range, considered to be 135-146 mmol/L.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Serum sodium levels are dynamic and can fluctuate day to day based on several factors. Hydration status is one of the most common influences: drinking a lot of water can slightly dilute sodium levels, while dehydration can make them appear higher. Other factors include diet, medications (like diuretics), and certain health conditions that affect fluid balance, such as kidney or adrenal disorders. Because sodium plays a key role in regulating blood pressure, nerve signals, and muscle function, the body tightly controls its levels. Still, small variations are normal and reflect how the body is responding to current needs or changes in fluid and electrolyte balance.

## Summary

A sodium level in the normal range means the body is effectively balancing fluids and electrolytes, supporting healthy nerve function, muscle contraction, and blood pressure. It suggests that systems like the kidneys, adrenal glands, and antidiuretic hormone (ADH) signaling are working well, lowering the risk of symptoms like confusion, fatigue, or muscle cramps. To maintain healthy sodium levels, focus on balanced hydration, especially during illness or exercise; eat mineral-rich whole foods; and support adrenal and kidney function through stress reduction, quality sleep, and a balanced diet. Regular monitoring can potentially help catch early signs of imbalance.

## Causes

Sodium levels in the body can both decrease and increase due to various reasons, and it's important to be aware of what can impact these levels.

Decreases in sodium can be caused by factors such as overhydration, the use of diuretic medications, adrenal gland issues, or conditions that lead to fluid retention, like congestive heart failure or kidney disease.

On the other hand, increases in sodium levels might result from a high salt diet or certain health conditions that affect the body's water balance. Understanding these factors is key to maintaining healthy sodium levels, which is crucial for overall health and the proper functioning of the body.

## Self care

According to John Hopkins Medicine, people should limit their sodium intake to 1,500 mg daily.

Grab coconut water or other fluids with electrolytes if dehydrated from diarrhea, a hangover, high altitude, a long flight, or hot weather.
