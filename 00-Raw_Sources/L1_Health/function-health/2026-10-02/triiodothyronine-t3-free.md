## Why it matters

Triiodothyronine (T3) is a hormone produced by the thyroid gland that regulates how the body uses energy. It plays a key role in metabolism, body temperature, heart rate, digestion, muscle function, and brain activity. T3 works by entering cells and attaching to special receptors, which help turn certain genes on or off to support these functions. There are two forms of T3:

- **Free T3** , which is active and available for immediate use by the body.
- **Bound T3** , which is attached to proteins and stored until needed.

Most T3 is made by converting thyroxine (T4) into its active form with the help of an enzyme called 5' deiodinase. Think of T4 as the raw ingredient and T3 as the final product the body actually uses. Measuring free T3 helps assess how much active thyroid hormone is circulating and available to cells.

Free T3 does not act alone; it works alongside other thyroid biomarkers like thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and free T4, which are key regulators in the thyroid hormone feedback loop. While TSH is often the first biomarker assessed for thyroid function, it does not always reveal the full picture, particularly in cases of non-thyroidal illness syndrome or issues with how T4 is converted into T3. If free T3 levels are too high or too low, it may indicate an imbalance in thyroid function, autoimmune disease, or metabolic stress. Measuring free T3 alongside these other thyroid markers helps healthcare providers pinpoint specific thyroid disorders.

- **High T3 (above normal range)** : This may indicate an overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) , commonly seen in Graves’ disease , toxic nodular goiter, or excessive iodine intake, or toxic nodular goiter, where abnormal thyroid tissue produces excess hormone. It can also result from taking too much thyroid hormone replacement therapy. High free T3 is associated with an increased risk of irregular heartbeats (arrhythmias), weight loss, anxiety, bone loss (osteoporosis), and metabolic imbalances.
- **Low T3 (below normal range)** : This suggests an underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) or a condition called non-thyroidal illness syndrome, where chronic illness, stress, or nutrient deficiencies (such as low selenium or iodine) reduce T3 production. Symptoms can include fatigue, brain fog, depression, slow metabolism, and cold intolerance. Low free T3 is also linked to chronic kidney disease and other systemic conditions affecting hormone metabolism.

When thyroid hormones are balanced, they help regulate body temperature, maintain a healthy heart rate, balance cholesterol levels, enhance memory, and maintain the health of hair, skin, and nails. Additionally, balanced thyroid hormones optimize fertility and libido while promoting healthy digestion and regular bowel movements. Addressing the root cause ensures long-term, sustainable improvement in thyroid health.

Free triiodothyronine (T3) is a key thyroid hormone that directly influences metabolism, energy production, and cellular function throughout the body , playing a critical role in maintaining heart rate, temperature, and cognitive function. A root cause approach to maintaining healthy T3 levels involves addressing factors that influence thyroid hormone conversion and utilization, including nutrient status, stress levels, and gut health.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Free T3 levels can fluctuate due to a variety of internal and external factors, including stress, nutrition, sleep, and seasonal changes. The body constantly adjusts how much T3 it makes and converts from T4 based on energy needs, immune activity, and environmental conditions. Stress hormones like cortisol can interfere with this process, and since much of T3 conversion happens in the liver and kidneys, temporary changes in these organs can also impact levels. Because free T3 varies day to day, a single test result may not always tell the full story. If levels are out of range, follow-up testing or a full thyroid panel—including TSH, free T4, and thyroid antibodies—can give a clearer picture of thyroid health.

**Optimal range**

The optimal range for free T3 in adults is between 3.0-4.4 picograms per milliliter (pg/mL).

However, even within this range, individualized variations exist, and some people may feel hypothyroid symptoms at the low-to-normal end while others may experience hyperthyroid effects at the upper end of normal. Some evidence has indicated that maintaining free T3 levels in the upper half of the reference range may optimize metabolic function.

## Summary

A free triiodothyronine (T3) level within the normal reference range indicates that the body is producing and converting thyroid hormones effectively, supporting a balanced metabolism, energy production, and overall thyroid health. Free T3 is the active form of thyroid hormone, responsible for regulating metabolic rate, body temperature, and cardiovascular function. A normal free T3 suggests that the thyroid gland, hypothalamus, and pituitary gland are working in harmony to regulate hormone production appropriately. However, it is important to interpret free T3 in the context of other thyroid markers like thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) and free thyroxine (T4), as some individuals may still have underlying metabolic or inflammatory conditions despite a normal free T3 reading.

From a root cause perspective, maintaining free T3 within an optimal range involves focusing on underlying factors that influence thyroid hormone production and conversion. Nutritionally, ensuring adequate intake of iodine, selenium, and zinc supports efficient conversion of T4 to T3 while reducing processed foods and excess sugar can help prevent inflammation that interferes with thyroid function. Botanical approaches such as Ashwagandha and Rhodiola can support the thyroid, potentially enhancing T4 to T3 conversion.

Additionally, addressing adrenal function is important, as chronic stress and high cortisol can suppress T3 levels. Stress management strategies like mindfulness meditation and sufficient sleep are keys to maintain thyroid health. Regular monitoring of thyroid function, especially for individuals with autoimmune tendencies or metabolic disorders, ensures that free T3 remains within a healthy range, supporting optimal energy and well-being.
