## Why it matters

Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol is a type of lipoprotein that carries cholesterol through the bloodstream to different parts of the body. While lower LDL is generally beneficial, cholesterol is more complex than just "good" or "bad." The size, quality, and number of cholesterol particles provide more insight than standard tests, which only measure the number of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) or LDL by weight (measured in milligrams per deciliter or mg/dL of blood) and don't reveal the health of the particles themselves. This test is called lipoprotein fractionation.

Cholesterol particles can be small and dense, which increases risk, or large and fluffy, which are more protective. Think beach ball versus golf ball. The golf ball hurts if it hits you. The beach ball does not. The goal is to have more light, fluffy, and functional particles. Two people can have the same LDL cholesterol level—say, 150 mg/dL—but very different risk profiles depending on the size and number of particles. One person might have mostly large, buoyant particles and fewer than 1,000 total LDL particles, putting them at low risk. Another person with the same LDL level might have 1,000 small, dense particles, significantly increasing their risk for a heart attack. Same number on paper, vastly different implications for treatment. The first is relatively benign, and the second requires an aggressive treatment strategy. Yet standard cholesterol tests only measure the total weight, which doesn’t fully reflect heart disease risk.

LDL particle number (LDL-P) measures the actual number of LDL cholesterol particles that are circulating in the bloodstream. This is different from a standard LDL cholesterol test, which only tells how much cholesterol is inside the particles. A high LDL-P means there are more vehicles carrying cholesterol, increasing the chances that they will penetrate artery walls and contribute to plaque buildup, even if the total LDL cholesterol looks normal. LDL-P is often a better predictor of cardiovascular events than LDL alone.

Elevated LDL-P is also commonly associated with insulin resistance or metabolic dysfunction, which is often seen in individuals with prediabetes, type 2 diabetes, or excess abdominal fat (visceral fat). Chronic inflammation makes LDL-P more likely to become oxidized, which is a sign of an early stage of atherosclerosis. In contrast, a low or within-range level of LDL-P usually reflects healthier lipid metabolism and lower cardiovascular risk.

LDL-P is best understood in the context of comprehensive lipid and metabolic markers, which includes LDL small, LDL peak size, LDL pattern, triglycerides (a type of fat in the blood that stores unused calories for energy), HDL (the “good” kind that typically helps clear extra cholesterol away), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), and lipoprotein(a). It is a useful marker to help detect early warning signs of heart disease and guide clinical decisions around diet, lifestyle, and medical interventions, if needed. Knowing your LDL-P can add an additional layer of clarity to a standard cholesterol test.

**Optimizing HDL if there is lipid imbalance**

Optimizing HDL cholesterol, in particular, is essential when LDL particle number is high because large, more buoyant HDL particles are protective and can help counterbalance the associated risks. While smaller, more dense HDL particles are often caused by insulin resistance, prediabetes, and type 2 diabetes, and increases the risk of heart disease. In addition to the size and quality of HDL particles, there are other tests to consider when lipid markers point to an increased risk. Advanced tests like the pCAD score and pCEC, together can capture one of HDL’s most important protective roles and reveal cardiovascular risk factors that standard HDL cholesterol measurements can miss.

**Summary**

LDL particle number shows how many LDL cholesterol-carrying particles are in the blood. More LDL particles mean a higher chance of plaque buildup in the arteries. An elevated LDL particle number can point to deeper issues like poor diet, insulin resistance, or inflammation, which are all underlying causes that need to be addressed to protect cardiovascular health.

**Optimal ranges**

The optimal range for LDL-P is <1,138 nmol/L. A low LLDL-P is associated with reduced risk of future cardiovascular disease.

*This range isn’t one-size-fits-all. A root cause approach looks at the overall cardiometabolic health by factoring in inflammation, insulin resistance, and family history alongside the LDL particle number to find the most protective range. It highlights why personalized care matters, tailoring health goals to your unique risk factors and health status for better long-term outcomes.*

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

LDL particle number levels can change based on your lifestyle and what's happening in your body. Factors like what you eat, how much you exercise, stress levels, blood sugar control, and even how much sleep you get can all influence it. For example, eating a diet high in refined carbohydrates and processed foods may have an impact on the number of LDL particles. Similarly, if you're under a lot of stress or not sleeping well, your body may have more inflammation, which can also impact your LDL particle numbers. It's important to look at trends over time, not just a single test result, and to understand the bigger picture of your heart and metabolic health.

## Summary

An LDL particle number (LDL-P) above the reference range (typically above 1,000 nmol/L) means there are too many LDL cholesterol particles circulating in the blood. This increases the likelihood that these particles will oxidize, enter artery walls, and trigger inflammation, which can lead to plaque buildup over time. Elevated LDL-P is strongly associated with a higher risk of atherosclerosis (the buildup of plaque in artery walls), heart attacks, and strokes, even if the total LDL cholesterol appears normal. It’s also commonly seen in people with insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, or a diet high in processed foods and refined carbohydrates.

An elevated LDL particle number reflects a shift in how the body is processing and packaging cholesterol, often driven by inflammation, poor blood sugar control, or early metabolic changes. These particles tend to stay in circulation longer, giving them more time to be damaged by oxidative stress, which makes them more likely to stick to artery walls and cause inflammation. Over time, it can increase the risk for heart disease, especially if other markers like ApoB and LDL small are also high. An elevated LDL particle number signals the need to address root causes such as poor diet (especially refined carbs and added sugars), sedentary lifestyle, chronic stress, or hidden inflammation to restore long-term cardiometabolic health.

When LDL-P is elevated, it’s essential to dig deeper by evaluating further for the size and quality of LDL particles to get a more accurate picture of cardiovascular health:

- **LDL pattern** : Classifies LDL particles as pattern A (large, buoyant, and less risky LDL) or pattern B (smaller, denser, and higher risk for atherosclerosis).
- **LDL small:** These are the smallest and most dense LDL particles within the total LDL particle number, and they carry a higher risk for plaque buildup and cardiovascular disease.
- **LDL peak size** : Identifies the predominant LDL particle size, where smaller peak sizes are associated with increased cardiovascular risk.

**Root cause approach**

A root cause approach to addressing elevated LDL particle number involves identifying and managing the underlying contributors, such as chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, poor blood sugar control, insulin resistance, or genetic predispositions, while promoting protective elements like antioxidants, essential nutrients, quality sleep, and stress management. By addressing these underlying causes, rather than just managing symptoms, a root cause approach seeks to restore the body’s natural ability to regulate lipid metabolism and promote long-term health and vitality. Greater detail on diet, lifestyle, and other considerations is provided in the sections that follow, but a general overview includes:

- **Nutrition** : A heart-healthy, Mediterranean or anti-inflammatory diet is key to managing an LDL particle number that is elevated. Focus on increasing healthy fats (from sources like extra virgin olive oil, avocados, fatty fish, flaxseeds, and walnuts) and fiber-rich foods (from fruits, vegetables, and whole grains) while avoiding refined carbohydrates, trans fats, and ultra-processed foods.
- **Lifestyle modifications:** Engaging in regular aerobic and strength-training exercises can help improve overall lipid profiles. Aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate exercise weekly, paired with stress-reduction techniques such as yoga, breathwork, meditation, or other mindfulness practices.
- **Weight management** : If living with excess weight or obesity, gradual, sustainable weight loss can reduce insulin resistance and lower cardiovascular risk.
- **Liver support:** The liver is responsible for cholesterol homeostasis. Supporting liver function by avoiding overconsumption of alcohol and including liver-supportive nutrients can help improve cholesterol metabolism.
- **Gut health support** : Supporting gut health with probiotics, prebiotics, fermented foods (e.g., kefir, sauerkraut), and a diet high in fiber can reduce inflammation and improve lipid metabolism.
- **Targeted supplementation** : Omega-3 fatty acids, CoQ10, and antioxidants can help reduce oxidative stress and inflammation and support cardiovascular health.
- **Pharmaceutical interventions:** If diet and lifestyle changes are not enough, pharmacological interventions with lipid-modifying medications such as statins, ezetimibe, or PCSK9 inhibitors may be beneficial, as deemed necessary by a clinician, especially for high-risk individuals.
- **Metabolic health** : Insulin resistance is closely linked to elevated LDL particle number. Regular monitoring of fasting glucose, insulin, and HbA1c levels, particularly if living with insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, or metabolic syndrome, ensures appropriate glycemic control and cardiometabolic health.
- **Follow-up testing** : Follow-up tests may include LDL small, LDL pattern, LDL peak size, triglycerides, HDL, apolipoprotein B (the main protein found on LDL particles), inflammatory markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and homocysteine, and lipoprotein(a) to better assess cardiovascular risk.

## Causes

**Root causes of a high LDL particle number**

A high LDL-P often reflects deeper imbalances in the body, like inflammation, insulin resistance, poor liver function, or hidden toxin exposures. Understanding these root causes helps address the *why* behind the number, not just lower it.

**Diet and lifestyle factors**

Here are common diet and lifestyle factors that can contribute to an elevated LDL particle number:

- **Poor diet:** Eating a diet high in saturated and trans fats, particularly from processed foods, can promote inflammation in blood vessels. Additionally, consuming refined carbohydrates and added sugar drives insulin resistance.
- **Sedentary lifestyle:** Lack of regular physical activity can lower HDL and raise triglycerides, which can shift LDL particles into more atherogenic (plaque-forming) forms.
- **Chronic stress:** Long-term stress or hormonal shifts (like during menopause) can affect lipid levels by increasing cortisol and disrupting fat metabolism.

**Other factors**

- **Insulin resistance:** Insulin resistance is an important driver of an elevated LDL particle number. The average person now consumes over 150 pounds of sugar each year, mostly through ultra-processed foods, which causes repeated spikes in blood sugar, which then forces the pancreas to release large amounts of insulin. Over time, chronically high levels of both sugar and insulin overwhelm the system, causing cells to stop responding properly. This triggers a vicious cycle: the body pumps out even more insulin, leading to increased belly fat, inflammation that raises cardiovascular risk.
- **Oxidative stress:** Oxidative stress occurs when there’s an imbalance between free radicals (unstable molecules that can damage cells) and the body’s ability to neutralize them with antioxidants. When LDL particles are exposed to oxidative stress, they become oxidized, making them even more harmful (atherogenic). Oxidized LDL is more likely to trigger inflammation in the arteries, drive foam cell (cholesterol-filled immune cells) formation, making them more likely to stick to artery walls and resulting in plaque buildup.
- **Chronic inflammation:** Inflammation from hidden sources like stress, infections, or poor sleep can disrupt cholesterol metabolism.
- **Gut dysbiosis:** Poor gut health or dysbiosis (an imbalance of gut bacteria) can increase inflammation, impair bile acid metabolism, and reduce cholesterol excretion.
- **Obesity:** Having excess abdominal fat (visceral fat) contributes to insulin resistance and increases cardiovascular risk.
- **Genetics:** Inherited conditions like familial hypercholesterolemia can cause the body to produce or retain too much LDL cholesterol regardless of lifestyle.
- **Hypothyroidism:** An underactive thyroid slows metabolism, which can lead to higher LDL levels.
- **Liver dysfunction:** The liver plays a key role in cholesterol metabolism and helps clear cholesterol from the blood, and dysfunction can lead to buildup.

**Environmental toxin exposure**

​Exposure to certain environmental toxins has been shown to directly elevate LDL cholesterol levels.

- **Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS):** PFAS is a “forever chemical” commonly found in consumer products, including non-stick cookware, water-repellent fabrics, and food packaging.​ Higher plasma levels of PFAS are associated with higher levels of LDL.
- **Air pollution:** Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is a component of air pollution from vehicle emissions, industrial processes, and wildfires.​ Long-term exposure to PM2.5 has been linked to elevated LDL cholesterol.​

Reducing exposure to these toxins may be a valuable component of strategies aimed at maintaining healthy cholesterol levels and reducing cardiovascular risk.

For more information, visit [Environmental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/environmental-health-101).

**Substance use**

Substance use, particularly cigarette smoking, creates an internal environment that promotes the formation of LDL particles that are more likely to cause artery damage. Smoking directly increases oxidative stress, flooding the body with free radicals that damage LDL particles, making them more likely to stick to artery walls and trigger inflammation, eventually leading to plaque buildup. Even in people with normal cholesterol, smoking has been shown to worsen LDL quality and raise cardiovascular risk.​

## Associated conditions

**General conditions and diseases**

LDL-P above the normal range is linked to a higher risk of several chronic conditions, especially those involving the heart and blood vessels. Over time, excess LDL-P can contribute to plaque buildup in the arteries (atherosclerosis), which narrows them and limits healthy blood flow. Detecting and managing it early can help prevent these more serious outcomes.

- **Atherogenic dyslipidemia:** Characterized by elevated LDL, high triglycerides, and low HDL cholesterol. This combination increases the risk of plaque buildup in the arteries (atherosclerosis), leading to heart disease and stroke. It's commonly seen in people with insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, or type 2 diabetes.
- **Coronary artery disease (CAD)** : Caused by plaque buildup in the arteries that supply the heart, leading to chest pain, heart attacks, or heart failure.
- **Stroke** : A result of reduced or blocked blood flow to the brain, often due to plaque or a blood clot from narrowed arteries.
- **Peripheral artery disease (PAD)** : A condition where narrowed arteries reduce blood flow to the legs or arms, causing pain, numbness, or even the risk of amputation.
- **Carotid artery disease** : Narrowing of the arteries in the neck that supply blood to the brain, increasing stroke risk.
- **Type 2 diabetes** : Elevated LDL particle number may appear alongside insulin resistance, eventually leading to higher blood sugar and the development of diabetes.
- **Metabolic syndrome** : This is a cluster of risk factors, including high blood pressure, high blood sugar, excess belly fat, and abnormal cholesterol levels, that raise the risk for heart disease, stroke, and diabetes.
- **Chronic inflammation:** Small and medium-sized LDL particles are more prone to becoming oxidized, which can trigger a chronic, low-grade inflammatory response that damages tissues over time.
- **Cognitive decline, dementia,** and **Alzheimer’s disease** : Reduced blood flow and inflammation can damage brain cells, increasing the risk of memory loss and conditions like Alzheimer’s disease.

**Mental health conditions**

While a high LDL-P is mainly linked to heart disease, it may also play a role in certain mental health conditions. This connection is likely due to the effects of chronic inflammation, poor circulation, and the impact of metabolic imbalances on the brain. When arteries become narrowed or stiff from plaque buildup, blood flow to the brain can be reduced, which may impact mood, memory, and cognitive performance over time.

Some mental health conditions that may be associated with an elevated LDL particle number include:

- **Depression** : Imbalances in cholesterol may affect the production of brain chemicals like serotonin and disrupt hormone levels, contributing to low mood and emotional instability.

## Foods to eat

Nutrition plays a key role in overall health. [Nutrition 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/nutrition-101) is a science-based review of foundational nutritional principles and eating practices that promote health and well-being. It is founded on three core principles:

- **Whole real food** : Eat real, whole, or minimally processed foods and avoid ultra-processed foods.
- **Food is medicine** : All food constituents, including protein, fat, carbohydrates, fiber, phytochemicals, vitamins, and minerals, regulate every process and cellular function in the body.
- **Personalization** : Age, sex, genetics, preferences, health conditions, and individual goals should guide the optimal diet for every unique individual.

Using food as medicine to manage an elevated number of LDL particles means choosing foods that reduce inflammation and oxidative stress, improve insulin sensitivity, and support gut and liver health to help the body process fats more efficiently. Time-restricted eating can improve metabolic control by avoiding food 3 hours before bed and leaving at least 12 hours—ideally 14 to 16 hours—between dinner and breakfast. Here’s how to use food to naturally improve LDL levels:

**Anti-inflammatory foods**

An anti-inflammatory diet reduces systemic inflammation, improves insulin sensitivity, and decreases oxidative stress. It also promotes healthy fat intake, particularly monounsaturated fats and omega-3 fatty acids, which can naturally improve overall lipid profiles.

- **Fruits and vegetables:** Rich in antioxidants, vitamins, and minerals. Berries, leafy greens (e.g., collard greens, kale, spinach, Swiss chard), and cruciferous vegetables are particularly beneficial. Starchy root vegetables, like potatoes, are best consumed in moderation due to their higher carbohydrate content, which can affect blood sugar levels and insulin sensitivity when eaten in excess.
- **Healthy fats:** Omega-3 fatty acids found in wild-caught fatty fish (e.g., salmon, mackerel, anchovies, sardines, and herring), avocados, ground flaxseed, chia seeds, and walnuts help reduce inflammation.
- **Whole grains:** Foods like quinoa, brown rice, and steel-cut oats provide essential nutrients and fiber that improve cholesterol profiles and reduce inflammation. While whole grains and beans can be part of a healthy diet, individuals with impaired glucose control, such as prediabetes or type 2 diabetes, should limit these foods.
- **Nuts and seeds:** Almonds, chia seeds, and sunflower seeds provide healthy fats and antioxidants that protect the heart.
- **Extra-virgin olive oil:** Rich in monounsaturated fats and antioxidants, olive oil is a staple of the Mediterranean diet, supporting healthy cholesterol levels and reducing cardiovascular risk.
- **Curcumin:** The active ingredient in turmeric, curcumin, has powerful anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties that can support cardiovascular health.

**Antioxidant foods**

Antioxidant-rich foods protect LDL particles from oxidative stress and reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease.

- **Berries:** Blueberries, strawberries, and blackberries are high in antioxidants.
- **Dark leafy greens:** Spinach and kale.
- **Cruciferous vegetables:** Broccoli and Brussels sprouts.
- **Green tea:** Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) in green tea has cardioprotective effects.
- **Dark chocolate (70% cocoa or higher):** Cocoa polyphenols induce the release of nitric oxide (NO) through activation of endothelial NO synthase, which, in turn, accounts for vasodilation and cardioprotective effects.

**A gut-friendly diet**

Certain foods enhance diversity in the gut microbiome and can help lower inflammation in the gut. Fiber, in particular, binds to cholesterol in the gut to support its removal.

- **Fermented food:** Yogurt (preferably unsweetened), kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut, and miso.
- **Fiber-rich foods:** Cruciferous vegetables, apples, whole grains, beans, and legumes.
- **Prebiotics:** Onions, garlic, and asparagus.

**Fiber-rich foods:** Prioritizing fiber-rich foods is essential for managing cholesterol levels, as soluble fiber binds to bile acids in the digestive tract, reducing cholesterol absorption and promoting its excretion. Foods to include are steel-cut oats, legumes (such as lentils, chickpeas, and beans), fruits like apples, pears, and berries, vegetables like carrots and Brussels sprouts, and whole grains.

**High-quality protein:** If accessible, choosing high-quality protein sources from grass-fed, regeneratively, or pasture-raised meats, poultry, and fish is important for managing weight, balancing blood sugar, and improving insulin sensitivity, which helps reduce metabolic syndrome. Foods that are particularly beneficial include fatty fish, lean poultry, pasture-raised eggs, and plant-based proteins such as quinoa, tofu, and tempeh.

**Polyphenol-rich foods:** Polyphenols, found in a variety of deeply pigmented vegetables like leafy greens, beets, and bell peppers, are high in antioxidants that may protect LDL from oxidation and reduce artery damage.

**Plant sterols and stanols:** Plant sterols and stanols in plant-based spreads, nuts (e.g., almonds), seeds, and avocados block cholesterol absorption in the gut, reducing total cholesterol levels and promoting a better HDL-to-LDL ratio.

## Foods to limit

To lower the number of LDL particles, it is important to limit certain foods that increase inflammation, oxidative stress, and promote insulin resistance. Here are some foods to eliminate or reduce that may disrupt cardiometabolic health:

- **Refined carbohydrates and added sugars:** Consuming excess sugar, especially in forms like sucrose and high fructose corn syrup, raises blood levels of unhealthy cholesterol like triglycerides, VLDL (a type of lipoprotein that carries triglycerides), and ApoB, all of which increase cardiovascular risk. At the same time, it lowers protective markers like HDL and ApoA-I, which are essential for clearing cholesterol from the bloodstream. Additionally, foods high in refined carbohydrates and added sugars spike blood sugar levels and contribute to insulin resistance, which further increases cardiovascular risk over time.
  - Sugary snacks and beverages: Soft drinks, candies, and desserts.
  - Refined carbohydrates: White bread, pasta, and pastries.
- **Trans fats:** Found in processed and fried foods, trans fats lower HDL cholesterol and raise LDL cholesterol, increasing the risk of heart disease.
  - Fried foods like french fries, fried chicken, and other deep-fried foods.
  - Margarine and shortening: These contain partially hydrogenated oils, a source of trans fats.
- **Processed meats:** Regular consumption of processed meats promotes inflammation and oxidative stress, and lowers protective HDL cholesterol.
  - Processed meats: Sausages, hot dogs, and deli meats often contain preservatives and high levels of sodium.
- **Advanced glycation end products (AGEs):** AGEs are compounds formed when proteins or fats combine with sugar. They promote oxidative stress and inflammation.
- **High-sodium foods:** Excessive salt intake can lead to hypertension and other health issues that can add to cardiovascular risk.
  - Packaged snacks such as chips, pretzels, and salted nuts.
  - Canned soups and vegetables.
- **Artificial additives and preservatives:** Artificial additives like preservatives, flavor enhancers (e.g., MSG), and food colorings contribute to inflammation and poor lipid metabolism.
  - Artificial sweeteners such as aspartame and saccharin.
  - Preservatives and food dyes are found in many packaged and processed foods.

For more information, visit [Nutrition 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/nutrition-101).

## Supplements

Supplements can play an important role in supporting overall health and well-being by providing essential nutrients that may be lacking in a regular diet, regulating hormones and metabolic processes, and contributing to the prevention and management of various conditions. [Supplements 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/supplements-101) provides more information on supplements.

*Please remember, before introducing a new supplement, it’s wise to speak with your healthcare provider. If you have a medical condition or are on medication, this step helps you stay safe and make sure everything works together to support your health.*

**Targeted**

The following supplements help lower the number of LDL particles by addressing inflammation, lipid metabolism, and liver function, which are all key drivers of cholesterol imbalances:

**Omega-3 fatty acids (DHA and EPA)**

- **Benefits:** Have anti-inflammatory effects that may reduce triglyceride and non-HDL cholesterol levels and improve vascular health.
- **Dosage:** 1,000-3,000 mg.
- **Frequency:** Once daily.
- **Mechanism of action:** Reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines, improve endothelial function, and lower liver production of very low-density lipoproteins (VLDL).

**Fiber supplements (β-glucan and konjac glucomannan)**

- **Benefits** : Highly viscous, soluble fibers help lower LDL cholesterol while improving HDL levels and overall lipid profiles by slowing the absorption of cholesterol in the intestines.
- **Dose** : 3-5 g.
- **Frequency** : Once daily or split into two doses, before meals.
- **Mechanism of action** : Bind bile acids in the gut, prompting the liver to use more cholesterol to make bile, which lowers circulating LDL.

**Coenzyme Q10 (ubiquinol form)**

- **Benefits:** Supports cardiovascular health, reduces oxidative stress, and counters statin-related side effects.
- **Dosage:** 100-200 mg.
- **Frequency:** Once daily with a fat-containing meal.
- **Mechanism of action:** CoQ10 acts as a mitochondrial antioxidant and may reduce LDL via gene expression modulation (e.g., inflammation/fat metabolism genes) and inhibition of LDL oxidation.

**Plant sterols/stanols**

- **Benefits:** Block the absorption of dietary cholesterol.
- **Dosage:** 2,000-3,000 mg.
- **Frequency:** Once daily with meals.
- **Mechanism of action:** Compete with cholesterol for absorption in the small intestine, lowering circulating LDL levels.

**Generalized**

Generalized supplements reduce inflammation, improve endothelial function, and prevent oxidative damage to blood vessels to help slow plaque buildup in arteries (atherosclerosis) and promote long-term cardiovascular health.

**Curcumin (with piperine)**

- **Benefits** : Has anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties that may reduce vascular inflammation and plaque formation.
- **Dose** : 500-1,000 mg of standardized curcumin extract.
- **Frequency** : Once or twice daily.
- **Mechanism of action** : Inhibits NF-κB signaling, suppresses inflammatory cytokines, and protects endothelial cells from oxidative damage.

**Vitamin D3**

- **Benefits** : May reduce inflammation and improve lipid profile, especially in individuals with a vitamin D deficiency.
- **Dose** : 2,000-5,000 IU.
- **Frequency** : Once daily with food, especially those containing fat.
- **Mechanism of action** : Regulates inflammatory pathways that can contribute to atherosclerosis and may also improve insulin sensitivity.

**Berberine**

- **Benefits** : Berberine has gender-specific lipid-modifying effects. It improves HDL, lowers LDL and triglycerides, while supporting blood sugar regulation and insulin sensitivity.
- **Dose** : 500-1,500 mg.
- **Frequency** : Three divided doses daily with meals.
- **Mechanism of action** : Berberine activates AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a cellular enzyme that reduces lipid synthesis and enhances cholesterol clearance by the liver.

**Resveratrol**

- **Benefits** : May improve endothelial function and reduce LDL oxidation.
- **Dose** : 150-300 mg.
- **Frequency** : Once daily.
- **Mechanism of action** : Activates SIRT1 pathways, supports mitochondrial health, and reduces oxidative stress in blood vessels.

**Probiotics (*Akkermansia muciniphila*)**

- **Benefits** : Strengthen the gut barrier and reduce systemic inflammation.
- **Dose** : 1-10 billion CFUs.
- **Frequency** : Once daily.
- **Mechanism of action** : Probiotics enhance gut barrier integrity and may influence lipid metabolism by modulating the gut microbiome.

## Symptoms

An elevated LDL particle number typically doesn’t cause noticeable symptoms unless it’s linked to other medical conditions.

- **Chest pain or discomfort (angina):** May occur if plaque buildup in the arteries begins to restrict blood flow to the heart.
- **Shortness of breath:** Particularly seen with activity and could reflect reduced oxygen delivery due to narrowed arteries or poor circulation.
- Dizziness or lightheadedness
- Palpitations or irregular heat rhythm
- **Fatigue:** Can result from limited blood flow to muscles and tissues, especially during physical exertion.
- **Numbness or cold extremities:** May indicate poor circulation with diminished pulses, especially in the legs or feet, linked to peripheral artery disease.
- **Eruptive xanthomas:** Rare, small, yellowish bumps on the skin (often elbows, knees, or buttocks) caused by very high cholesterol (particularly triglyceride) levels.
- **Corneal arcus (arcus senilis):** A gray-white ring around the iris of the eye, sometimes seen in younger people with inherited high LDL.

**Insulin resistance**

- **Acanthosis nigricans:** Dark, velvety patches of skin on areas like the neck, armpits, or groin.
- **Acne**
- **Arcus senilis (cornea senilis)** : A white or gray ring around the cornea.
- **Fatigue**
- **Frequent infections**
- **Frequent urination**
- **High blood pressure**
- **High blood sugar**
- **Increased hunger**
- **Increased thirst**
- **Increased waist circumference**
- **Infertility**
- **Weight gain**
- **Xanthomas** : Visible deposits of cholesterol in the skin, especially around the eyelids (xanthelasma) or on the tendons.

## Self care

**Exercise and movement**

Exercise plays a key role in promoting long-term cardiovascular health. A combination of aerobic and resistance exercise is cardioprotective.

- **Aerobic exercise:** Regular aerobic activities, such as brisk walking, running, swimming, and cycling, are highly effective at improving cholesterol metabolism. Aerobic exercise enhances fat metabolism, reduces body fat, and improves insulin sensitivity, all of which contribute to better metabolic and cardiovascular health.
- **Resistance and strength training:** Resistance training, such as weightlifting or bodyweight exercises (e.g., push-ups, squats), can improve lipid profiles, particularly when combined with aerobic exercise. Resistance training helps build muscle, reduces fat mass, and improves metabolic health.

Exercise is a foundational intervention for addressing core metabolic imbalances such as systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and insulin resistance, which are all critical for long-term health and well-being. Focus should be on developing a personalized exercise regimen tailored to your health status, fitness level, and personal preferences. Combining regular physical activity with a nutrient-dense diet rich in antioxidants, healthy fats, and fiber, along with stress-reducing practices like yoga or meditation, enhances the benefits of exercise. This well-rounded approach ensures sustained improvements in metabolic function, cardiovascular health, and overall vitality.

For more detailed information on exercise, please refer to [Exercise 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/exercise-101).

**Weight management**

Sustainable and personalized weight management strategies are essential for achieving lasting improvements in metabolic and cardiovascular health. Being overweight or living with obesity, especially with excess abdominal fat (also known as visceral fat), is strongly associated with insulin resistance and chronic inflammation, both of which can raise the number of LDL particles and increase the risk of plaque buildup in arteries (atherosclerosis). Healthy weight loss promotes long-term cardiovascular health by contributing to the following:

- Lowers pro-inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6).
- Improves insulin sensitivity.
- Restores the balance of hormones like leptin and adiponectin.
- Significantly reduces triglyceride levels.

Refer to [Weight Management 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/weight-management-101) for foundational principles for healthy weight management.

**Sleep optimization**

Adequate, restorative sleep (7-9 hours per night) supports long-term metabolic and cardiovascular health by helping regulate hormones like cortisol and insulin, which directly affect cholesterol metabolism.

- **Reduces inflammation and oxidative stress:** Chronic sleep deprivation leads to an increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 and CRP, which are closely tied to cardiovascular risk. Improving sleep duration and quality helps reduce inflammation and oxidative stress that can be damaging to arteries.
- **Improves insulin sensitivity:** Poor sleep is linked to insulin resistance. Improving sleep quality regulates blood sugar levels and enhances insulin sensitivity.

Visit [Sleep 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/sleep-101) for practical tips on improving sleep.

**Mental health and stress reduction**

Mental health practices and stress reduction are powerful tools in improving cardiovascular wellness. Techniques like mindfulness and yoga improve heart rate variability, lower blood pressure, and enhance overall cardiovascular fitness. Personalized lifestyle interventions to address both the physical and emotional aspects of health lead to long-term improvements in overall well-being.

- **Reduces inflammation:** Chronic stress increases the production of cortisol and other stress hormones, which promote inflammation. Stress reduction techniques, such as mindfulness, meditation, cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), and yoga, lower inflammation and improve cardiovascular health.
- **Enhances insulin sensitivity:** Practices like mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) also improve glucose regulation and insulin sensitivity by lowering cortisol levels.
- **Supports healthy weight management:** Stress management helps regulate appetite hormones, such as ghrelin and leptin, which influence hunger and satiety and reduce emotional eating and weight gain, and the accumulation of visceral fat, all of which negatively affect cardiovascular health.

For more information on mental health and stress management, visit [Mental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/mental-health-101) and [Stress Management 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/stress-management-101).

**Detoxification**

The liver plays a central role in regulating cholesterol—both producing it and breaking it down—so when it's overburdened by processed foods, alcohol, or environmental toxins, its ability to eliminate waste and manage lipid metabolism can become impaired. Supporting detoxification pathways through nutrient-rich foods (like cruciferous vegetables), adequate hydration, regular sweat-inducing activities, and targeted liver-supportive nutrients like glutathione or milk thistle may help the liver function more efficiently. A well-functioning detox system reduces internal stress, supports cholesterol balance, and helps protect your arteries from inflammation and damage.

For more information, visit [Environmental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/environmental-health-101).

**Gut health support**

A healthy gut helps manage inflammation, supports liver function, and influences how the body processes cholesterol. Beneficial gut bacteria, especially species like *Akkermansia muciniphila* and *Lactobacillus*, can help reduce cholesterol absorption and promote the excretion of excess cholesterol from the blood. Balancing the gut microbiome with probiotics, prebiotics, fermented foods (e.g., kefir, sauerkraut), and dietary fiber can reduce gut permeability and inflammation and improve lipid metabolism.

## Additional tests

**Root cause approach to diagnosing a high LDL particle number**

A root cause approach to diagnosing an elevated LDL particle number looks beyond the number to uncover deeper imbalances like inflammation and oxidative stress, insulin resistance, liver dysfunction, or toxin exposure that may be driving the elevation.

The need for specific tests depends on your individual risk profile, clinical symptoms, family history, and the presence of other health conditions, as determined by your healthcare provider. *Here is a list of tests that may be relevant if LDL particle number levels are above range, with those available through your Function membership noted below.*

**Blood testing**

A range of blood tests can help evaluate an elevated LDL particle number through a root cause lens by giving a fuller picture of what's happening inside the body.

- **Apolipoprotein B (ApoB):** Measures atherogenic (plaque-forming) particles in the blood, which is a more accurate predictor of heart risk than LDL particle number alone.
- **LDL particle size:** Identifies whether LDL particles are small and dense (more harmful) or large and fluffy (less harmful).
- **LDL pattern** : Classifies LDL particles as pattern A (large, buoyant, and less risky LDL) or pattern B (smaller, denser, and higher risk for atherosclerosis).
- **LDL peak size** : Identifies the predominant LDL particle size, where smaller peak sizes are associated with increased cardiovascular risk.
- **Oxidized LDL (oxLDL)** : Direct measurement of oxidized LDL to evaluate the degree of oxidative stress on cholesterol.
- **Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)]:** A genetic form of LDL that increases cardiovascular risk regardless of other cholesterol levels.
- **Fasting glucose and insulin:** Elevated insulin or blood sugar can point to insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome, which is often seen alongside elevated LDL particle number.
- **Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c):** Shows average blood sugar levels over the past 2-3 months and helps reveal hidden metabolic issues.
- **Insulin resistance score:** The insulin resistance score is a valuable tool for assessing insulin resistance (IR) and identifying the risk of prediabetes and diabetes. It combines measurements of insulin and C-peptide from a single fasting blood sample, offering insights into how well the body processes glucose and insulin​. This score is especially useful for detecting IR in individuals who may have normal glucose and HbA1c levels but are still at risk due to underlying metabolic issues​.
- **Liver function:** Assesses how well the liver is processing fats and cholesterol.
- **Thyroid function:** An underactive thyroid can slow metabolism and lead to higher levels of LDL.

**Urine testing**

- **Urinalysis:** A general urine test can reveal signs of metabolic or kidney dysfunction, which are often linked to poor cholesterol profiles.
- **Microalbumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR)** : Assesses early kidney damage. A high ACR can be a marker of endothelial dysfunction, a condition where the inner lining of blood vessels is damaged, potentially increasing the risk of cardiovascular disease.
- **24-hour urine cortisol** : Elevated cortisol can contribute to impaired lipid metabolism.

**Diagnostic imaging**

Imaging can be a useful tool to see the impact of an elevated LDL particle number causing damage in the body over time.

- **Heart CT Scan with Gated CAC:** Measures calcium buildup in the coronary arteries and calculates the coronary artery calcium (CAC) score to assess long-term heart disease risk. The images are timed with your heartbeat to avoid blurriness from the heart’s normal movement and rhythm.
- **Carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT) ultrasound:** A noninvasive ultrasound scan that measures the thickness of the carotid artery's inner layers. It helps detect early signs of atherosclerosis in the carotid arteries (major blood vessels in the neck that supply blood to the brain) before symptoms appear and assess future stroke risk.
- **AI-enhanced coronary CT angiogram:** This CT scan gives a clearer picture of both calcified and soft plaques in the coronary arteries. The CT angiogram is AI-enhanced for more precise evaluation of total plaque burden, location, distribution, and rupture risk.
- **Echocardiogram (ECHO):** An ECHO can help evaluate the structure and function of the heart.
- **Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):** An MRI can be used to assess plaque in arteries and the health of the heart muscle.

**Nutrient imbalances**

- **Vitamin D (25-hydroxy):** Low vitamin D is linked to inflammation and poor cardiovascular outcomes.
- **Omega-3 index:** Measures the percentage of EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid) and DHA (docosahexaenoic acid), two types of omega-3 fatty acids, in red blood cell membranes; low levels can contribute to inflammation and cholesterol imbalance.
- **Magnesium (RBC)** : Low magnesium is often found in people with metabolic issues.
- **Homocysteine:** High levels may reflect poor methylation and can damage the lining of arteries and promote the formation of plaques, especially when LDL particle number is also elevated.
- **Vitamin B12 and folate (B9)** : These B vitamins are essential for methylation and reducing homocysteine levels.
- **Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10)** : A key antioxidant needed for heart and mitochondrial health; low levels may impair lipid metabolism and are especially important for those on statins.
- **Zinc and copper** : An imbalance may reflect oxidative stress or poor antioxidant defense, which can worsen LDL oxidation.
- **Vitamin C:** Deficiency impairs antioxidant defense, leading to oxidative stress.
- **Vitamin E:** Insufficient levels decrease protection against lipid peroxidation (oxidative damage of fats in cell membranes triggered by free radicals).

**Microbiome and gut health assessment**

The microbiome plays a key role in inflammation, metabolism, and how the body processes cholesterol. Assessing gut health in the context of an elevated LDL particle number can be beneficial.

- **Comprehensive stool analysis** : Assesses the balance between beneficial and harmful bacteria in the gut. Looks for markers of dysbiosis, yeast overgrowth, or parasitic infections. Stool testing can also evaluate gut permeability ("leaky gut") by measuring zonulin levels, which indicate whether inflammatory triggers are escaping the gut and contributing to systemic inflammation and worsening damage to blood vessels.

[Gut Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/gut-health-101) offers a comprehensive guide to understanding the importance of gut health, its impact on overall well-being, and practical steps to optimize digestive health.

**Immune system evaluation**

Evaluating the integrity of the immune system can uncover hidden inflammation or immune imbalances that may be increasing risk alongside elevated LDL particle number.

- **High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP):** An inflammatory marker that detects low-grade inflammation, which, in combination with a high LDL particle number, increases cardiovascular risk.
- **Other inflammatory markers:** Measures markers such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and pro-inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), to gauge the level of systemic inflammation that may be driving damage to blood vessels.
- **Food sensitivity testing (IgG):** Delayed immune reactions to certain foods can cause chronic inflammation.
- **Autoimmunity** : Screens for autoimmune activity that may signal the immune system is mistakenly attacking healthy tissues and increasing vascular inflammation.

**Environmental and toxin testing**

Testing for environmental toxins is not meant for everyone, but in cases of a high LDL particle number, testing for certain environmental toxins can help uncover hidden drivers of inflammation and oxidative stress.

- **PFAS:** Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances interfere with liver function and fat metabolism.
- **Heavy metals:** Evaluates for metals like lead, arsenic, and mercury, which are linked to lipid abnormalities and oxidative stress.
- **Mycotoxins (urine):** Detects mold toxins that may cause inflammation, oxidative stress, and burden the liver.

**Advanced diagnostics**

- **pCAD score:** Evaluates how effectively HDL protects against the risk of premature coronary artery disease (pCAD).
- **pCEC (percent cholesterol efflux capacity):** Measures how efficiently HDL removes cholesterol from artery walls and lowers the risk of plaque buildup (atherosclerosis) in the blood vessels of the heart.
- **Cholesterol balance testing:** Cholesterol balance testing helps uncover the source of high cholesterol, whether high cholesterol is coming from internal production (synthesis) or external absorption (from food and bile reabsorption).
- **Oxidative stress markers:** Evaluates oxidative stress through tests like urinary 8-isoprostane or plasma malondialdehyde (MDA). These tests help gauge the level of oxidative damage in the body.
- **Organic acids testing (OAT)** : Organic acid tests can detect imbalances in microbial byproducts, mitochondrial dysfunction, and nutritional deficiencies that indicate dysbiosis and systemic oxidative stress.
- **Genetic testing:** Identifies increased genetic risk for various cardiovascular diseases, such as early-onset myocardial infarction (MI or heart attacks), coronary artery disease (CAD), atrial fibrillation (AF), and strokes.

**Cognitive assessments**

A high LDL particle number may impact cognitive function over time by contributing to inflammation and reduced blood flow, which can impair how the brain gets oxygen and nutrients, much like it does in the arteries of the heart. LDL particles can linger in circulation and become oxidized, potentially affecting the small blood vessels that supply the brain, particularly when combined with insulin resistance or systemic inflammation. Over time, this can increase the risk for cognitive decline, memory issues, and even cause vascular dementia. Cognitive testing can be instrumental in detecting early signs of cognitive decline associated with an elevated LDL particle number. By assessing memory, attention, and executive function, these tests can help identify subtle changes in cognitive performance, allowing for timely interventions to manage cholesterol levels and potentially mitigate further cognitive deterioration.

For more information about maintaining optimum brain health, including cognition, memory, and ways to reduce the risk of age-related neurodegenerative disorders, visit [Brain Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/brain-health-101).

## Follow up

**Medical interventions**

Collaboration with your healthcare provider ensures a personalized approach to managing high LDL particle numbers. Consideration of all symptoms, laboratory test results, and lifestyle factors can guide a treatment plan tailored to your specific needs and health goals.

**Medications**

When LDL particle number is elevated, especially in combination with other risk factors (e.g., diabetes), clinicians may prescribe medications to help lower LDL particles and reduce the risk of conditions like atherosclerosis, heart attack, and stroke. Once the root cause, such as insulin resistance or an inherited condition like familial hypercholesterolemia, has been identified, treatment can be tailored to individual needs. For those with existing cardiovascular disease, significant soft plaque seen on an AI CT angiogram, or an abnormal carotid ultrasound (CIMT), medications are often necessary to lower their risk, even alongside lifestyle changes. Here are some medications commonly prescribed:

- **Statins (e.g., rosuvastatin, atorvastatin, simvastatin):** Statins are used for high LDL, atherosclerosis, coronary artery disease, and stroke prevention. They reduce LDL cholesterol and triglycerides while modestly increasing HDL levels. They also lower inflammation and stabilize arterial plaques. Most people on statins today fall under “primary prevention,” meaning they have high cholesterol but no history of heart disease. While there’s modest benefit for some, particularly those with multiple risk factors or a high 10-year heart disease risk, it’s important to weigh the benefit against potential side effects.
- **Ezetimibe:** Ezetimibe inhibits cholesterol absorption in the intestines and is often used in combination with statins to reduce cardiovascular risk.
- **PCSK9 inhibitors (e.g., alirocumab, evolocumab):** PCSK9 inhibitors prevent the degradation of LDL receptors, thus allowing the liver to remove more LDL from the blood.
- **Fibrates (e.g., fenofibrate, gemfibrozil):** Primarily used to treat high triglycerides and metabolic syndrome, but sometimes combined with other medications to address high LDL or low HDL.
- **Omega-3 fatty acids (e.g., icosapent ethyl):** Prescription-strength omega-3 fatty acids lower triglycerides and have added benefits in reducing cardiovascular events.
- **Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors (e.g., empagliflozin):** SGLT2 inhibitors reduce cardiovascular risk by lowering blood glucose, promoting weight loss, and reducing inflammation.
- **Thiazolidinediones (TZDs, e.g., pioglitazone):** Used to treat type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome. TZDs improve insulin sensitivity and increase HDL levels by activating PPAR-γ receptors, which influence lipid and glucose metabolism.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists (e.g., liraglutide):** GLP-1 receptor agonists improve insulin sensitivity, aid in weight loss, and may modestly raise HDL cholesterol while reducing LDL and triglycerides.

For individuals with inherited lipid disorders, medication is often essential. Statins, PCSK9 inhibitors, and cholesterol absorption blockers such as ezetimibe may be needed. That said, aggressive lifestyle changes through food, movement, and metabolic support can still play a powerful role in improving lipid levels and, in some cases, reduce or delay the need for long-term medication.

**Procedures**

When a high LDL particle number contributes to serious cardiovascular disease, and lifestyle or medications aren't enough to reduce risk, medical procedures or surgeries may be necessary to restore blood flow or prevent life-threatening complications. These interventions focus on treating blocked or damaged arteries caused by atherosclerosis—a common consequence of a persistently elevated LDL particle number.

- **Coronary angioplasty and stenting (also known as percutaneous coronary intervention or PCI):** A catheter with a balloon is inserted into narrowed heart arteries and inflated to widen them; a stent (small mesh tube) is often placed to keep the artery open. This procedure helps restore blood flow to the heart and relieve chest pain or prevent a heart attack caused by plaque buildup.
- **Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG):** CABG reroutes blood flow around blocked coronary arteries and helps prevent heart failure or further cardiovascular events.
- **Carotid endarterectomy:** Carotid endarterectomy removes plaque from carotid arteries to restore blood flow to the brain and reduce stroke risk in those with significant atherosclerosis.
- **Endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR):** EVAR places a stent graft in the aorta to prevent aneurysm rupture related to vascular disease.
- **Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR):** TAVR replaces a narrowed aortic valve to improve heart function, particularly in those with high cardiovascular risk.
- **Heart transplant:** A heart transplant may be necessary for end-stage heart failure caused by severe cardiovascular complications.

**Retesting**

Retesting LDL particle number is a key part of assessing how well your management plan is working. Pairing LDL results with inflammation markers and metabolic markers can give you the clearest picture of true progress. Here are the guidelines that can help decide when and how often to retest:

- **4-12 weeks after starting or changing a treatment plan:** This is the typical window to evaluate how effective a new strategy, like dietary changes, medications, or supplements, has been in lowering LDL particles and improving the overall lipid status.
- **Every 6-12 months for long-term monitoring:** If LDL particle number and related markers are in a healthy range and the plan is working, annual follow-up testing helps ensure things stay on track and catch early signs of change.
- **Earlier retesting (within 4-8 weeks):** May be warranted if there has been a significant change in medication or supplement dosage or new symptoms develop (e.g., fatigue, chest discomfort, brain fog).
