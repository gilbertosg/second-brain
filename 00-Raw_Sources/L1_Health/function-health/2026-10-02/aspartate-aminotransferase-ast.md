## Why it matters

Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) is an enzyme found mostly in the liver, but it’s also found in the heart, muscles, and other organs. It can also go by the name serum glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase (SGOT). Its main job is to help cells turn nutrients (like amino acids from proteins) into energy, which is essential for keeping organs running smoothly. When cells in these organs are injured or under stress, AST can leak into the bloodstream, so measuring how much AST is in the blood provides important clues about whether there might be damage or disease in the liver or other tissues.

AST isn’t evaluated alone; it is assessed along with other biomarkers, especially alanine aminotransferase (ALT), which is more specific to the liver. Comparing AST and ALT levels can help determine whether abnormal results are coming from the liver or other tissues like the heart or muscles. If AST is higher than ALT (a high AST/ALT ratio), it may point to alcoholic liver disease, cirrhosis, or muscle or heart injury. Meanwhile, if ALT is higher than AST (a low AST/ALT ratio), it often suggests fatty liver, diabetes risk, or metabolic issues. The ratio helps narrow down the possible causes and guide further testing. AST is also checked with other tests like alkaline phosphatase (ALP), bilirubin, and albumin to get a fuller picture of liver health and function.

AST results are used together with symptoms and other laboratory tests to decide if further investigation or treatment is needed.

- **Low AST** : Low AST levels aren’t typically a concern and don’t usually signal a disease, but there are situations where low AST can be clinically significant. Low AST, especially in the context of symptoms, advanced age, or chronic illness, may warrant further evaluation for vitamin deficiencies, advanced liver or kidney disease, or broader metabolic concerns.
- **High AST** : If AST levels are high, it usually means that cells in the liver, heart, or muscles are damaged. This can happen with conditions like hepatitis, fatty liver disease, cirrhosis, heart attack, or even after intense exercise or muscle injury.

**Summary**

Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) is a liver and muscle enzyme that slips into the bloodstream when those tissues are stressed or injured, so a blood test can flag early signs of trouble in the liver, muscles, or heart. AST is evaluated along with its partner liver enzyme, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and a few other markers to pinpoint whether the damage is coming from the liver, heart, or muscles and to decide if more testing and management are needed.

**Optimal ranges**

An optimal range for AST is between **10-30 U/L for women** and **10-40 U/L for men** , reflecting healthy liver and muscle turnover without creeping into deficiency on the low end or cell injury on the high end. Some suggest that the upper limit of normal for AST should be toward the lower end of the range, around 30 U/L, particularly when screening for non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Maintaining AST inside these brackets is linked to the lowest long-term risk of fatty-liver progression, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular events.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) is measured in the blood (specifically, in serum), and its levels can naturally fluctuate for several reasons. Day-to-day changes of about 5%–10% are common, and even moderate exercise can temporarily raise AST for up to 24 hours, since AST is also found in muscles as well as the liver. Medications, supplements, alcohol use, and hormonal changes (such as during the menstrual cycle or pregnancy) can all cause short-term rises or drops in AST. Factors like age, sex, body weight, race, and recent physical activity also play a role in how much AST is found in the blood at any given time.

Because AST is present in many tissues, not just the liver, its levels can go up after muscle injury, heart problems, or even after a tough workout. Chronic conditions like fatty liver disease, hepatitis, or metabolic syndrome can cause longer-lasting elevations, while some medications or vitamin deficiencies can lead to lower levels. For these reasons, a single AST result is just one piece of the puzzle, and it is helpful to look at trends over time and consider other tests and personal factors to get the most accurate picture of liver and overall health.

## Summary

An aspartate aminotransferase (AST) level above the reference range signals that cells in the liver, heart, muscles, or kidneys may be damaged. While AST isn’t specific to one organ, elevated levels often point to liver issues like hepatitis, fatty liver, or alcohol-related damage. They can also reflect muscle injury (from intense exercise or trauma), heart strain (like a heart attack), or medication side effects. For example, chronic alcohol use or metabolic conditions like obesity and diabetes often drive liver inflammation, raising AST. However, a single high result isn’t a diagnosis; it’s a clue to investigate further with tests like ALT (a more liver-specific enzyme), imaging, or a review of medications and lifestyle habits.

Symptoms of elevated AST depend on the underlying cause but may include fatigue, jaundice (yellowing of the skin or eyes), abdominal swelling, dark urine, or muscle pain. If left unaddressed, persistently high AST can be associated with complications like irreversible liver scarring (cirrhosis), liver failure, or worsening heart issues. For instance, untreated fatty liver might progress to severe liver damage, while chronic muscle inflammation could impair mobility. The key is to identify and treat the root cause—whether it’s reducing alcohol intake, managing blood sugar, or addressing nutrient deficiencies—to prevent long-term harm. Regular monitoring and a comprehensive approach (diet, stress management, toxin reduction) help restore balance and protect organ health.

**Root cause approach**

A root cause approach to elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) focuses on identifying and addressing the underlying drivers of tissue damage, whether from the liver, heart, muscles, or other organs. Greater detail on food, lifestyle, and other considerations is provided in the sections that follow, but a general overview includes:

- **History evaluation** : A thorough history and medication review identifies alcohol consumption, drugs that are toxic to the liver, supplement use, strenuous exercise, or muscle trauma, each capable of raising AST through direct cellular injury.
- **Consideration of the AST/ALT ratio** : Comparing AST with alanine aminotransferase (ALT) provides context for elevated AST levels. A ratio above two often aligns with alcohol related liver injury, while a lower ratio points toward viral, fatty, or drug-induced hepatocellular damage.
- **Follow-up testing:** A comprehensive liver panel with viral, autoimmune, and iron and copper status markers and, when indicated, ultrasound or transient elastography clarifies etiology and gauges fibrosis without relying solely on enzyme levels.
- **Nutrition:** Higher adherence to a Mediterranean dietary pattern rich in olive oil, legumes, nuts, fruits, and fish correlates with lower AST, likely through improved insulin signaling and reduced liver fat.
- **Lifestyle:** In those who are overweight, sustained weight loss of about 7-10 percent of body weight and regular moderate activity are associated with meaningful AST declines and histologic improvement in metabolic-associated fatty liver disease.
- **Botanical therapies:** Milk thistle (silymarin) and curcumin help reduce inflammation and improve liver function.
- **Pharmaceutical and procedural approaches:** Direct-acting antivirals for hepatitis C, nucleos(t)ide analogs for hepatitis B, and bariatric procedures in severe obesity show enzyme normalization by resolving the underlying driver of liver cell injury.
- **Prevention and monitoring:** Repeating liver chemistries every 3-6 months, together with periodic review of drugs, supplements, and environmental exposures, provides an ongoing check on intervention success and helps avert recurrent AST elevation.

## Causes

**Root causes of high AST**

Elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) usually signals that liver, heart, or skeletal muscle cells are leaking this enzyme because of toxic, metabolic, or mechanical stress. The downstream rise in AST, therefore, traces back to root causes such as toxicant overload, fat-driven oxidative stress, mitochondrial injury, or inherited vulnerabilities that weaken cell membranes.

**Diet and lifestyle factors**

- **Consumption of high-fructose and added processed sugars** : Excess fructose drives fat synthesis in the liver and creates uric-oxide stress that elevates AST.
- **Low physical activity** : Sedentary behavior is linked to fatty liver changes and higher AST, whereas regular movement shows the opposite trend.
- **Strenuous, unaccustomed exercise** : Overexertion or muscle injury from intense exercise or trauma can temporarily raise AST.

**Other factors**

- **Rapid weight gain or central obesity** : Fat around abdominal organs (visceral fat) promotes insulin resistance and toxic injury to liver cells, raising AST.
- **Gut-barrier dysfunction** : Increased intestinal permeability allows endotoxin to reach the liver and amplify AST-releasing inflammation.
- **Systemic inflammation** : Cytokine-driven oxidative stress weakens cell membranes and leaks AST.
- **Oxidative stress from mitochondrial overload (e.g., iron or copper excess)** : Injures liver cells and elevates AST.
- **Insulin resistance/metabolic syndrome** : Liver fat and toxic intermediates impair insulin signaling, disrupt lipid metabolism, and contribute to liver damage, ultimately releasing AST into the bloodstream.
- **Genetic variants** : Certain genetic variants and inherited conditions like hemochromatosis (iron overload) or Wilson’s disease (copper overload) can damage the liver and elevate AST.

**Medications**

- **Acetaminophen** : Overdose or chronic use can cause liver injury and raise AST.
- **Statins (simvastatin, atorvastatin)** : Can cause mild to moderate AST elevation, especially at higher doses.
- **Antibiotics (amoxicillin-clavulanate, tetracycline, ciprofloxacin)** : Linked to drug-induced liver injury and AST elevation.
- **Methotrexate (for autoimmune disease/cancer)** : Known to raise AST as a side effect.
- **Isoniazid (for tuberculosis)** : Commonly causes liver enzyme elevation, including AST.
- **Valproic acid (for seizures)** : Associated with liver injury and AST elevation.
- **Remdesivir (antiviral used for COVID-19)** : Linked to AST and ALT elevation.

**Environmental toxin exposure**

- **Heavy metals (mercury, lead, cadmium)** : These metals can directly raise AST by damaging liver cells.
- **Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs)** : Occupational and environmental exposure to these chemicals is linked to higher AST and liver enzyme levels.
- **Fine particulate matter (PM2.5)** : Air pollution exposure is associated with higher AST and liver inflammation.
- **Plastic-related chemicals (phthalates, Bisphenol A):** These chemicals can damage the liver, contributing to elevated liver enzymes
- **Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS)** : These forever chemicals can contribute to liver enzyme elevation, especially with high exposure.
- **Mold/mycotoxins:** Certain mold-related toxins found in the air, certain foods, or the environment can weaken the gut and lungs’ protective barriers, potentially allowing harmful bacteria to enter the bloodstream. They can simultaneously influence inflammatory pathways and overload cells with oxidative stress, creating a cycle of immune overactivation and tissue damage that impacts the liver.

For more information, visit [Environmental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/environmental-health-101).

**Substance use**

- **Excessive alcohol intake** : Chronic alcohol use inflames liver cells and generates oxidative stress, raising AST.
- **Anabolic steroids** : Use is associated with liver injury and significant AST elevation.
- **Methamphetamine, MDMA (ecstasy), phencyclidine (PCP)** : These drugs can cause acute liver injury with very high AST levels.

## Associated conditions

**General conditions and diseases**

Elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) is most often linked to disorders that injure liver, muscle, or heart cells.

- **Alcoholic liver disease** : Chronic heavy drinking inflames and scars liver tissue, releasing AST.
- **Viral hepatitis (hepatitis B or C)** : Ongoing viral attack on liver cells raises transaminase levels.
- **Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) or metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease** : Excess liver fat promotes oxidative stress and AST leakage.
- **Autoimmune hepatitis** : Misdirected immune attack on liver cells increases AST.
- **Hemochromatosis or Wilson's disease** : Inherited iron or copper overload harms hepatocytes, boosting AST.
- **α-1 antitrypsin deficiency** : Abnormal protein accumulation inside liver cells results in injury and raised AST.
- **Rhabdomyolysis** : Rapid skeletal-muscle breakdown floods the bloodstream with AST.
- **Heart attack or heart failure** : Damaged heart muscle releases AST proportionate to injury size.
- **Celiac disease** : Liver involvement in celiac disease can result in elevated AST levels due to liver inflammation.
- **Muscular dystrophies** : Muscle cell breakdown leads to enzyme release from damaged muscle tissue.

**Mental health conditions**

Raised AST can also appear alongside certain mental health diagnoses.

- **Alcohol use disorder** : Heavy alcohol consumption elevates AST and frequently co-occurs with depression and anxiety.
- **Major depressive disorder and anxiety in people with fatty liver disease** : Mood and anxiety symptoms are more prevalent when NAFLD is present.

## Foods to eat

Nutrition plays a key role in overall health. [Nutrition 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/nutrition-101) is a science-based review of foundational nutritional principles and eating practices that promote health and well-being. It is founded on three core principles:

- **Personalization** : Age, sex, genetics, preferences, health conditions, and individual goals should guide the optimal diet for every unique individual.

Elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) often reflects stress on liver cells; a root-cause approach considers how underlying imbalances, such as inflammation, oxidative stress, fat accumulation, or toxin exposure, interact with genetics and lifestyle. Diet can modulate many of these drivers and may support the liver’s capacity to repair and rebalance.

- **Fruits and vegetables:** Berries, citrus, and leafy greens are high in antioxidants like flavonoids and vitamin C, which help reduce oxidative stress and support liver function.
- **Cruciferous vegetables (broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cauliflower):** Sulforaphane, a bioactive compound found in cruciferous vegetables, supports the liver by helping balance oxidative stress and the inflammatory response.
- **Glycine and methionine sources** : Regeneratively raised organic poultry, wild fish, and collagen-rich broths provide amino acids required for glutathione synthesis and cellular repair within liver tissue.
- **Omega-3 fatty acids** : Fatty fish, flaxseed, and chia contain eicosapentaenoic and docosahexaenoic acids, nutrients associated with reduced liver fat and lower transaminase levels.
- **Choline-dense foods** : Eggs, salmon, and legumes furnish choline, which supports very-low-density lipoprotein assembly and may limit fat buildup that elevates AST.
- **Fermented foods and fiber** : Cultured yogurt, kefir, kimchi, and soluble fibers (oats, barley, beans, lentils, apples, pears, psyllium husk, chia, and flaxseed) foster a balanced gut microbiome, which can lower endotoxin load and systemic inflammation that reach the liver.

## Foods to limit

Some foods can make nutrient deficiencies or liver stress worse, so a root cause approach includes minimizing:

- **Refined sugars and sweetened foods:** Sugary drinks (soda, sweetened teas, fruit juices) and foods high in added sugars, as they can worsen metabolic stress and liver fat.
- **Refined grains like white bread, white rice, and pastries:** Lack fiber and can contribute to blood sugar imbalances and insulin resistance that drives fat buildup in the liver.
- **Ultra-processed foods:** Packaged snacks, fast food, and foods are low in nutrients and high in unhealthy fats, sugar, and salt which can negatively impact liver health.
- **Fried foods and foods high in trans or saturated fats:** Foods such as french fries, fried chicken, and processed baked goods can contribute to inflammatory imbalances.
- **Processed meats:** Meats like sausages, bacon, and hot dogs are very high in sodium, often making up a large part of the daily salt limit, which can increase the risk for certain chronic illnesses and impact liver and kidney function.
- **Alcohol:** The liver is responsible for breaking down alcohol. Chronic alcohol consumption leads to the buildup of toxic metabolites, resulting in increased oxidative stress, inflammation, fat accumulation, and disruption of normal liver metabolism.
- **Artificial sweeteners** : Artificial sweeteners may negatively impact gut health, potentially leading to increased liver fat accumulation and impaired nutrient absorption.

For more information, visit [Nutrition 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/nutrition-101).

## Supplements

Supplements can play an important role in supporting health by providing essential nutrients that may be lacking in a regular diet, regulating hormones and metabolic processes, and contributing to the prevention and management of various conditions. [Supplements 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/supplements-101) provides more information on supplements.

**Targeted**

Several supplements can help reduce aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels, particularly in conditions like non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) or drug-induced liver injury.

**Milk thistle (silymarin)**

- **Benefits:** Supports liver health by helping optimize liver cell integrity, detoxification pathways, and inflammatory balance; reduces AST in NAFLD and drug-induced liver injury by protecting liver cells and reducing inflammation.
- **Dosage:** 200 mg.
- **Frequency:** Twice daily.
- **Mechanism of action:** Helps modulate pathways related to antioxidative status and inflammation, and helps support liver cell repair and function.

**Curcumin**

- **Benefits:** Lowers AST in NAFLD and supports inflammatory balance by helping modulate high sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) levels.
- **Dosage:** 80-500 mg.
- **Frequency:** Twice daily.
- **Mechanism of action:** Inhibits the signaling of certain pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) and NF-κB, modulates immune cell activity to help reduce oxidative stress, and helps optimize cellular integrity.

**L-carnitine**

- **Benefits** : Lowers AST in chronic liver disease and NAFLD, especially at higher doses (≥2,000 mg/day).
- **Dosage** : 2,000-4,000 mg.
- **Frequency:** Daily,
- **Mechanism of action** : Enhances mitochondrial function and fatty acid metabolism.

**Vitamin E**

- **Benefits:** Reduces AST in NAFLD by combating oxidative stress; provides targeted support for liver cell health and antioxidative status.
- **Dosage:** 400 IU/day.
- **Frequency:** Once daily.
- **Mechanism of action:** Neutralizes reactive oxygen species (ROS) via antioxidant activity and helps modulate certain pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6).

**Garlic**

- **Benefits** : Reduces AST in NAFLD and metabolic syndrome.
- **Dosage** : 600-1,500 mg (aged extract).
- **Frequency** : Daily.
- **Mechanism of action:** Provides anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects.

**Generalized**

Generalized supplements aimed at improving liver health and helping balance inflammation and oxidative stress include:

**N-acetylcysteine (NAC)**

- **Benefits:** Helps promote glutathione production, antioxidative status, and healthy liver detoxification pathways.
- **Dosage:** 600 mg.
- **Frequency:** Twice daily.
- **Mechanism of action:** Serves as a precursor to glutathione, directly scavenges free radicals, and helps promote phase II detoxification enzyme activity.

**Probiotics (*lactobacillus/bifidobacterium*)**

- **Benefits** : Balance gut microbiota, reduce endotoxin load, and support liver health.
- **Dosage** : 10-50 billion CFUs.
- **Frequency** : Once daily.
- **Mechanism of action** : Enhance gut barrier function, lower systemic inflammation, and improve nutrient absorption.

**Alpha-lipoic acid (ALA)**

- **Benefits** : A potent antioxidant that can replenish glutathione, curb liver fat, and reduce inflammation in conditions such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.
- **Dosage** : 300-600 mg.
- **Frequency** : Once or twice daily with meals.
- **Mechanism of action** : Cycles between reduced and oxidized forms to neutralize free radicals, bind and remove pro-oxidant metals, and activate AMPK pathways that boost hepatic fat-burning and phase-II detoxification.

**Selenium (as selenomethionine)**

- **Benefits** : A trace mineral co-factor for glutathione-peroxidase that shields liver cells from oxidative damage and supports recovery in toxin or virus-related liver stress.
- **Dosage** : 100-200 mcg.
- **Frequency** : Once daily.
- **Mechanism of action** : Supplies the “spark” for antioxidant enzymes, lowers lipid peroxidation, and modulates immune signaling that can otherwise drive chronic hepatic inflammation.

## Symptoms

An above-range aspartate aminotransferase (AST) level often remains silent, yet any symptoms usually mirror the liver, muscle, or cardiac stress responsible for the elevation:

- Fatigue and low energy reflecting reduced metabolic efficiency in liver or muscle tissue
- Upper right abdominal discomfort or fullness, when inflamed liver tissue stretches its capsule
- Yellowing of skin or eyes (jaundice) indicating bile pigment buildup
- Dark urine and pale stools accompanying impaired bile flow
- Nausea, diminished appetite, and bloating related to hepatic congestion
- Itching skin resulting from circulating bile salts
- Leg or abdominal swelling when declining liver function alters fluid balance
- Easy bruising or prolonged bleeding owing to decreased clotting-factor synthesis
- Muscle soreness or weakness when the AST increase stems from skeletal muscle damage
- Chest discomfort or shortness of breath if the elevation comes from cardiac muscle injury

## Self care

**Exercise and movement**

Regular moderate exercise, such as brisk walking, swimming, or yoga, can help reduce liver inflammation and improve metabolic health, which may lower elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels. Avoiding intense or excessive exercise is important, as muscle injury can temporarily raise AST.

For more detailed information on exercise, please refer to [Exercise 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/exercise-101).

**Weight management**

Excess visceral fat around abdominal organs drives insulin resistance and fat accumulation in the liver, with sustained weight loss in those who are overweight helping to reduce AST. On the other hand, being severely underweight or having prolonged malnutrition can deplete antioxidant stores and contribute to muscle breakdown. Significant starvation can destabilize liver cell membranes and elevate AST, as shown in anorexia nervosa.

Refer to [Weight Management 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/weight-management-101) for foundational principles for healthy weight management.

**Sleep optimization**

Consistent, high-quality sleep supports liver repair and reduces inflammation, which can help bring high AST levels back toward a healthy range. Poor sleep may worsen metabolic stress and slow recovery from liver or muscle injury.

Visit [Sleep 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/sleep-101) for practical tips on improving sleep.

**Mental health and stress reduction**

Chronic stress can increase inflammation and negatively impact liver health, potentially raising AST. Practices like mindfulness, meditation, or deep breathing can help lower stress hormones and support overall metabolic balance.

For more information on mental health and stress management, visit [Mental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/mental-health-101) and [Stress Management 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/stress-management-101).

**Detoxification**

Supporting the body’s natural detoxification by staying hydrated, eating a variety of plant foods with fiber and antioxidants, and minimizing exposure to alcohol and environmental toxins can help reduce liver burden and may assist in lowering high AST.

See [Environmental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/environmental-health-101).

**Gut health support**

A healthy gut can improve nutrient absorption and reduce systemic inflammation, both of which support liver function and may help bring elevated AST levels back to normal. Naturally fermented foods, fiber-rich produce, and resistant starch (cooled potatoes, Jerusalem artichokes) can support a balanced microbiome.

See [Gut Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/gut-health-101).

## Additional tests

**Root cause approach to diagnosing high AST**

A root cause approach to diagnosing high aspartate aminotransferase (AST) involves investigating underlying triggers such as liver disease, muscle injury, heart problems, medications, or metabolic stress to pinpoint why this enzyme is elevated.

The need for specific tests depends on your individual risk profile, clinical symptoms, family history, and the presence of other health conditions, as determined by your healthcare provider. *Here is a list of tests that may be relevant if AST levels are out of range, with those available through your Function membership noted below.*

**Blood testing**

- **Alanine aminotransferase (ALT):** Helps distinguish if the elevation is liver-specific, as ALT is more liver-focused, while AST can rise from muscle or heart injury as well.
- **AST/ALT ratio** : Compares levels of these two enzymes to narrow down the cause of abnormal levels. Values above 2 often signal alcohol-related liver injury, while lower ratios suggest viral, fatty, or drug-induced causes.
- **Alkaline phosphatase (ALP)** : Assesses for bile duct or bone involvement, which can help clarify the source of AST elevation.
- **Gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT):** Indicates liver or bile duct stress, especially from alcohol or toxin exposure.
- **Total bilirubin:** Measures the liver’s ability to process waste; high levels with AST may suggest liver or bile duct disease.
- **Albumin:** Measures the liver’s ability to process waste; high levels with AST may suggest liver or bile duct disease.
- **Prothrombin time (PT/INR)** : Evaluates the liver’s ability to make clotting factors, which can be affected in severe liver disease.
- **Creatine kinase (CK)** : Checks for muscle injury, as AST can rise with muscle damage.
- **Cardiac enzymes (troponin, BNP)** : Evaluate for heart injury if AST is high and heart symptoms like chest pain or a racing heartbeat are present.
- **Blood counts and indices** : Screen for infection, inflammation, or low platelets, which can be linked to chronic liver disease.
- **Thyroid function** : Thyroid disorders can sometimes contribute to metabolic stress and liver enzyme changes.
- **Insulin, fasting glucose, hemoglobin A1c:** This analysis evaluates glucose regulation, identifying insulin resistance as a potential driver of liver fat accumulation and increased AST.
- **Advanced lipid panel** : Evaluates for metabolic syndrome and fatty liver disease.
- **Kidney function:** Kidney function may be affected in advanced liver disease or hepatorenal syndrome.
- **Lipase, amylase** : Pancreatitis can sometimes cause secondary liver enzyme elevations.

**Urine testing**

Urine tests are generally not primary for AST elevation, but may be used if kidney involvement or metabolic complications are suspected.

- **Albumin (microalbuminuria)** : Detects kidney involvement, which can co-occur with advanced liver disease
- **Creatinine:** Assesses kidney function, as kidney and liver health are closely linked.

**Diagnostic imaging (CT, MRI, ultrasound, etc.)**

- **Ultrasound:** Visualizes the liver for fat, swelling, or masses that might explain high AST.
- **Annual MRI:** Provides detailed imaging of the liver and other abdominal organs to detect inflammation, liver injury, liver masses, or other pathologies causing elevated AST.
- **Transient elastography:** Measures liver stiffness to check for scarring or fibrosis.
- **MRCP:** Focuses on bile ducts to look for blockages or issues that could affect AST.

**Nutrient imbalances**

- **Vitamin B6:** This test measures B6 levels, which support liver function; imbalances might worsen AST elevation in fatty liver conditions.
- **Magnesium:** Assesses magnesium, which is essential for liver health; a shortage could play a role in high AST when the liver is stressed.
- **Zinc:** This checks zinc, which helps the liver fight damage; an imbalance may add to high AST from inflammation.
- **Selenium** : This antioxidant cofactor helps detoxify reactive species that damage liver tissue and enzymes.
- **Copper and ceruloplasmin:** Copper is needed for liver processes. Too much or too little might increase AST by affecting liver balance. It helps assess for Wilson's disease in younger individuals with unexplained AST elevation.
- **Vitamin D:** This test looks at vitamin D, which aids liver repair; low levels could contribute to AST spikes in metabolic issues.
- **Iron studies (iron, ferritin, TIBC)** : Measures stored iron since excess iron can harm the liver and push AST higher in fatty liver disease; detects hemochromatosis or iron overload as a potential liver stressor.
- **Electrolytes (chloride, potassium, carbon dioxide, calcium, magnesium, sodium)** : Electrolyte imbalances may occur in advanced liver disease or with complications.

**Microbiome and gut health assessment**

High AST levels may indicate liver stress influenced by and affecting the gut microbiome (the bacteria in the intestines), which plays a role in the pathway connecting gut and liver health.

- **Stool microbiome analysis:** Identifies imbalances in gut bacteria (dysbiosis), which may produce toxins or inflammation that damage the liver.
- **Fecal calprotectin:** Measures gut inflammation, which can signal dysbiosis; inflammation may stress the liver.
- **Intestinal permeability:** Checks for a leaky gut lining, which may allow toxins to reach the liver, triggering damage.
- **Short-chain fatty acids (SCFA):** Assesses levels of beneficial bacterial byproducts like butyrate; low SCFAs from dysbiosis may weaken gut and liver health.

See [Gut Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/gut-health-101) for more information.

**Immune system evaluation**

- **High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP)** : Measures low-grade inflammation; high hs-CRP may signal chronic inflammation linked to liver damage.
- **Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)** : Assesses general inflammation; elevated ESR may indicate immune activity that irritates the liver.
- **Interleukin-6 (IL-6)** : Detects a specific inflammatory protein; increased IL-6 may promote liver inflammation and injury.
- **IgG food sensitivity and IgE food allergy testing** : Identifies foods triggering immune reactions; IgG and IgE-mediated responses may cause gut inflammation, which can burden the liver via the gut-liver axis.
- **Autoimmune markers (ANA, ASMA):** Screen for autoimmune hepatitis.
- **Hepatitis panel (A, B, C):** Checks for viral causes of liver inflammation.
- **Celiac disease panel** : Assesses for celiac disease, which can cause liver inflammation and raised AST.
- **IgG subclass 1-4, IgG, IgA, IgM** : Elevated immunoglobulins (hypergammaglobulinemia) are common in autoimmune hepatitis and chronic liver disease.
- **Infection testing** : Done when other infections are suspected, as some vector-borne and viral diseases can raise AST.

**Environmental and toxin testing**

- **Mycotoxins/mold** : Measures mold toxins like aflatoxins; these can damage liver cells, increasing AST through inflammation and oxidative stress.
- **Heavy metals** : Detects metals like lead, mercury, or cadmium; these can harm the liver, raising AST by causing oxidative stress and inflammation.
- **Bisphenol A (BPA)** : Measures levels of BPA, a plastic-related chemical; it can impair liver function, increasing AST through inflammation and lipid accumulation.
- **Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS)** : These forever chemicals found in a wide array of consumer and industrial products can impact liver function.

**Advanced diagnostics**

- **Fibrosis 4 (FIB-4) index:** A simple test that combines age and common lab results to estimate liver scarring risk. Calculates a fibrosis score using age, AST, ALT, and platelet count to screen for liver scarring in fatty-liver disease; a result under 1.3 suggests low risk, while a result above 2.67 indicates advanced fibrosis.
- **NAFLD fibrosis score (NFS):** A formula using age, weight/BMI, diabetes status, AST/ALT ratio, platelet count, and albumin to estimate the risk of advanced liver scarring in fatty liver disease. A score over 0.676 indicates a high risk of advanced fibrosis.
- **Liver fibrosis panel:** Uses a panel of blood markers and other factors—α2-macroglobulin, haptoglobin, apolipoprotein A1, GGT, total bilirubin plus age and gender—to estimate both fibrosis and fat accumulation in the liver, outputting a stage from F0 (none) to F4 (severe).
- **Three-marker enhanced liver fibrosis (ELF) panel (hyaluronic acid, PIIINP, TIMP-1):** A blood test that measures three markers of scar tissue activity in the liver, with a higher score signifying more scarring. Measures hyaluronic acid, PIIINP (procollagen type III N-terminal propeptide, a marker of new collagen production and tissue scarring), and TIMP-1 (tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase-1, a protein that slows collagen breakdown, linked to scar buildup) to quantify active scar formation; an ELF score above 9.8 signifies a high risk of advanced fibrosis.
- **AST-to-platelet ratio index (APRI):** A simple calculation using AST level and platelet count; values over 1.0 suggest significant liver fibrosis, and values over 2.0 indicate cirrhosis.
- **Serum PRO-C3 (pro-collagen III peptide) level:** Assesses formation of collagen III (a protein made during scar formation in the liver), an early marker of fibrotic activity; higher PRO-C3 levels correlate with more advanced liver scarring.
- **CK-18 (cytokeratin-18 fragments):** This test checks for pieces of protein released when liver cells die, helping to tell if there is active inflammation in fatty liver disease. It helps to distinguish inflammatory non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)/metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) from simple fat accumulation.
- **Oxidative stress** **markers** : Evaluates oxidative damage through tests like urinary 8-hydroxydeoxyguanosine or plasma F2-isoprostanes; these measure stress on liver cells, often raising AST due to inflammation and injury.
- **Organic acids:** Detects imbalances in urine organic acids, reflecting microbial overgrowth or mitochondrial issues; these can indicate liver strain, increasing AST through inflammation or fat buildup.
- **Genetic testing:** Analyzes genes like *PNPLA3* or *HFE* for variants linked to liver disease, as well as testing for genetic conditions that can impact the liver, like hemochromatosis (liver overload) and Wilson’s disease (copper overload).

**Cognitive assessments**

High AST, especially from liver disease, may affect brain function by allowing toxins to build up in the blood. Chronic inflammation or metabolic imbalances from liver dysfunction might also disrupt brain health, contributing to these cognitive changes. Cognitive testing, such as tasks measuring memory, attention, or problem-solving, can help by identifying these effects, offering insight into whether liver issues are affecting mental performance.

For more information about maintaining optimum brain health, including cognition, memory, and ways to reduce the risk of age-related neurodegenerative disorders, visit [Brain Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/brain-health-101).

## Follow up

**Medical interventions**

Collaboration with your healthcare provider ensures a personalized approach to managing aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels. Consideration of all symptoms, laboratory test results, and lifestyle factors can guide a treatment plan tailored to your specific needs and health goals.

**Medications**

- **Direct-acting antivirals for hepatitis C:** Short oral courses help to clear the virus and are associated with rapid AST normalisation.
- **Nucleos(t)ide analogs for hepatitis B** : Provide long-term viral suppression that lowers ongoing liver injury and AST elevations.
- **Corticosteroids or budesonide for autoimmune hepatitis** : Immune-modulating therapy that reduces inflammation and liver enzyme release.
- **Azathioprine or mycophenolate for steroid-sparing maintenance in autoimmune hepatitis** : Help with continued control of immune attack and stabilising AST.
- **Chelating agents such as penicillamine or trientine for Wilson's disease** : Help remove copper from the body to lessen oxidative stress and AST elevation.
- **Oral iron chelators for hereditary haemochromatosis** : Help to bind and remove iron to prevent further liver damage and lower AST.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists or pioglitazone for metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease** : Improve insulin sensitivity and reduce liver fat linked to AST.
- **N-acetylcysteine for acetaminophen toxicity or some acute liver injuries** : Helps replete glutathione to limit further AST rise.

**Procedures**

- **Bariatric surgery such as sleeve gastrectomy** : Achieves durable weight loss and sustained AST reduction in metabolic liver disease.
- **Liver biopsy** : Provides histologic clarity when the source of elevated AST is uncertain.
- **Liver transplant** : Replaces failing liver function and normalises AST when irreversible liver injury persists.
- **Endoscopic blood vessel (variceal) ligation** : Controls bleeding from elevated blood pressure in portal blood vessels to the liver, which can be linked to chronically high AST in conditions like alcohol-related liver disease.
- **Percutaneous coronary intervention after heart attack** : Restores blood flow to the heart and halts cardiac AST release.
- **Continuous renal replacement therapy for severe rhabdomyolysis** : Clears myoglobin and supports recovery as muscle-derived AST falls.
- **Therapeutic phlebotomy for hereditary haemochromatosis** : Removes blood to reduce iron and prevent further liver damage.

**Retesting**

Periodic laboratory testing of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and related markers offers a clear snapshot of ongoing liver, muscle, or heart cell health and guides evidence-based follow-up care.

- **Chronic liver or metabolic conditions** : AST is rechecked about 4- 6 weeks after starting therapy to confirm a downward trend.
- **Acute drug-induced liver injury or myocardial infarction** : AST measurements are repeated within 24-72 hours to track rapid changes.
- **Stable management phase** : AST is monitored every 3-6 months, tailoring the interval to the underlying condition and treatment intensity.
