## Why it matters

Eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) is an important omega-3 fatty acid known for reducing inflammation, protecting heart health, and supporting mental well-being. The body relies heavily on dietary sources like fatty fish (e.g., salmon, mackerel, sardines), algae, or supplements to obtain EPA, as its ability to convert plant-based omega-3s (alpha-linolenic acid, or ALA) into EPA is minimal.

EPA’s primary function involves regulating inflammation by producing molecules that calm the immune response and support healing. This anti-inflammatory activity helps reduce risks associated with chronic conditions like heart disease, arthritis, and depression, where excessive inflammation often plays a role.

EPA works closely with docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), another omega-3 fatty acid, to maintain a healthy balance of fats in the body. Measuring EPA levels alongside DHA and omega-6 fatty acids provides valuable insights into fatty acid balance. A higher proportion of EPA relative to omega-6 fatty acids is associated with an anti-inflammatory state, which is protective against chronic conditions like cardiovascular disease and metabolic dysfunction. Conversely, low EPA levels may reflect an imbalance in fatty acid metabolism, contributing to inflammation, elevated triglycerides, and mental health concerns.

- **Low EPA levels** often indicate increased inflammation and a greater risk of cardiovascular issues, mood disorders like depression, and elevated triglycerides. Insufficient EPA may also exacerbate conditions tied to inflammation, such as arthritis or autoimmune disorders.
- **Elevated EPA levels** , though rare, can result from excessive omega-3 supplementation, potentially interfering with blood clotting or creating imbalances with other fatty acids. Maintaining a healthy range of EPA ensures a balanced inflammatory response and supports long-term cardiovascular and mental health.

**Summary**

EPA is an important omega-3 fatty acid that helps reduce inflammation, supports heart health, and boosts mental well-being. Low levels of EPA can cause serious health problems, so it’s important to keep track of and manage this nutrient. Knowing and managing EPA levels can help to find and fix the root causes of imbalances, leading to better long-term health.

## Summary

An in-range level of EPA means the body has what it needs to keep inflammation in check, support cardiovascular health, and maintain emotional well-being. It reflects a healthy omega-3 to omega-6 balance, which protects against conditions like heart disease, arthritis, and depression. When EPA is in range, it helps stabilize mood, reduce inflammatory signals in the body, and support clear thinking and blood vessel function. Staying in range helps prevent the development of chronic diseases linked to inflammation and supports resilience under physical or emotional stress.

To keep EPA within range, a root cause approach emphasizes regular intake of omega-3-rich foods like fatty fish or algae-based supplements, especially if dietary intake is low. Ongoing testing of the omega-3 index helps ensure EPA stays in a beneficial range, allowing adjustments to diet, supplements, or lifestyle as needed. This comprehensive approach helps sustain the anti-inflammatory and cardiovascular benefits of EPA over time.

## Foods to eat

Foods rich in omega-3s, especially the EPA form, include:

Wild or sustainably-raised coldwater fish

- Herring
- Mackerel
- Salmon
- Sardines

Oils

- Flaxseed oil
- Walnut oil

Nuts and seeds

- Chia seeds
- Flaxseeds
- Walnuts

Eggs

- Pasture-raised eggs

## Foods to limit

Try to avoid refined, hydrogenated fats and oils, solid fats (like butter), and plant-based products with hydrogenated oils (like alternative milks), which are often used to create a silky and smooth texture.

Check food labels for these ingredients, as they are in many processed foods.

- Canola oil
- Fried foods
- Margarine
- Safflower oil
- Shortening
- Soybean oil
- Sunflower oil
