## Why it matters

Arachidonic acid (AA) is an omega-6 fatty acid found in the membranes of cells throughout the body, where it plays an essential role in regulating inflammation and cellular communication. It can be obtained directly through the diet, particularly from animal-based foods like meat, poultry, and eggs, or synthesized internally through the conversion of linoleic acid (LA), an omega-6 fatty acid abundant in refined oils and processed foods.

Depending on how it is processed by the body, AA can either promote inflammation, such that it helps in healing injuries, or resolve it by producing molecules that calm inflammation and support repair. Enzymes such as COX and LOX break down AA into different substances. Some of these substances, like prostaglandins and leukotrienes, trigger inflammation, as in swelling or pain after an injury, while others, like lipoxins, help resolve inflammation and promote healing once the repair process begins.

AA works closely with other fatty acids, particularly omega-3s like eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), which help balance its pro-inflammatory effects. Maintaining an appropriate balance between omega-6 and omega-3 fatty acids is important for regulating inflammation and supporting overall health. Measuring AA levels alongside EPA and DHA can help determine whether the body is tilted toward a more pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory state. For example, a high AA/EPA ratio may suggest increased inflammatory activity that could contribute to conditions like heart disease, arthritis, or diabetes. Monitoring AA levels can also help assess the effects of dietary changes or anti-inflammatory interventions.

- **High levels** : Often associated with chronic inflammation, increasing the risk of conditions like cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, and autoimmune disorders.
- **Low levels** : May impair the body’s ability to respond to injuries or infections, leading to delayed healing, reduced immune function, and difficulty managing inflammation.

Evaluating AA levels, particularly in the context of other fatty acids and symptoms, allows for personalized strategies to manage inflammation. Approaches may include dietary adjustments, like increasing omega-3 intake, lifestyle changes, and targeted therapies to restore balance and support cellular health.

## Summary

An in-range arachidonic acid (AA) level reflects balanced inflammatory activity, often associated with stable metabolic function, appropriate fat intake, and gut health. When AA stays within a healthy range, the risk of developing or worsening chronic inflammatory conditions, such as cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and autoimmune disorders, may be reduced. Maintaining AA within this optimal range supports long-term physiological resilience and lowers the likelihood of inflammation-related chronic disease.

Keeping AA in check involves nutritional strategies like omega-3-rich foods (fatty fish, flaxseeds, and walnuts), which naturally counterbalance AA-driven inflammation. Supportive therapies, including regular moderate exercise, stress management practices like mindfulness, and gut health optimization with probiotics and prebiotics, further strengthen the body's ability to regulate inflammation.

Tracking AA levels over time, alongside markers like EPA and DHA, helps track progress and fine-tune interventions, ensuring the inflammatory balance stays healthy over time.

## Foods to limit

AA is also found in refined omega-6 oils, and levels can increase if consumed in excess.
Check food labels for these ingredients, as the type of oils that can lead to elevated AA are in many processed foods:

- Canola oil
- Corn oil
- Fried foods
- Margarine
- Safflower oil
- Shortening
- Soybean oil
- Sunflower oil
