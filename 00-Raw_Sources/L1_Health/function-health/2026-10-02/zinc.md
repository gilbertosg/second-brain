## Why it matters

Zinc is a multitasking mineral essential for a healthy immune system, proper wound healing, cell growth and division, and carbohydrate metabolism. It enhances the action of insulin, plays a key role in DNA synthesis, and supports the senses of taste and smell. Like a master key, zinc activates hundreds of enzymatic reactions that help fight infections, maintain hormone balance, and support healthy skin. Because the body can't store large amounts of zinc, regular intake and good absorption are necessary to keep up with daily demands.

Zinc levels interact closely with other biomarkers, particularly copper—they balance each other like a seesaw, where too much zinc can deplete copper and vice versa. Testing zinc alongside copper, inflammatory markers like high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and other immune markers provides a fuller picture of metabolic and immune health. For example, low zinc with high hs-CRP may indicate that inflammation is diverting zinc from the blood into tissues, while zinc deficiency paired with poor wound healing could signal impaired collagen synthesis. These insights guide targeted interventions, from dietary adjustments to identifying malabsorption issues.

- **Low zinc:** Zinc deficiency has been linked to frequent infections, slow wound healing, and loss of taste or smell. It may also reflect underlying issues such as a poor diet, gut disorders like celiac disease, or chronic stress. Over the long term, inadequate zinc levels can contribute to weakened immunity and potentially cognitive decline.
- **High zinc:** Elevated zinc is rare but can occur due to excessive supplementation or industrial exposure. It may even lead to symptoms such as nausea, copper deficiency, or disruptions in cholesterol levels. In many cases, high zinc more often reflects an imbalance with other minerals rather than true toxicity.

**Optimal ranges**

The optimal range for serum zinc follows the general reference range, considered to be **60-130 mcg/dL** .

*The ranges charted alongside your results are provided by the laboratory. These are statistically derived from general populations in the United States, which some consider to be an inherently unhealthy benchmark for comparison. In contrast, the optimal ranges listed directly above are established by medical doctors based on optimal levels from the scientific literature and their clinical experience. Optimal ranges are often narrower and can identify early patterns that represent the early stages of the transition from wellness to illness. Optimal ranges are derived from both the scientific literature and the real-world experience of medical doctors. These may be different than ranges established by the labs or used in medical practice. For example, while the reference range for glucose is 100 mg/dL or less, the research reflects that any fasting glucose over 87 mg/dL directly correlates with increased cardiovascular risk. This highlights the importance of monitoring changes in your results over time for a reliable perspective.*

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Zinc levels in the blood can shift day to day based on things like stress, infection, meal timing, and even the time of day blood is drawn. For instance, inflammation and illness can cause the body to pull zinc out of the bloodstream and into cells, which can make blood levels look low even if one is not truly deficient. Zinc also follows the body’s internal clock, with levels typically higher in the morning and lower later in the day. Furthermore, eating a meal right before the test can alter zinc levels, which is why fasting tests are often more accurate. Stress, whether physical or psychological, has been shown to alter how the body uses zinc, which can cause shifts in test results day to day. Because of all this, a single zinc test gives a snapshot, but not always the full picture.

## Summary

When zinc is in the healthy range, it suggests diet, digestion, and absorption are effectively meeting the body’s needs. People with adequate zinc levels tend to have fewer infections, better mood stability, and more efficient healing from injuries. There are usually no symptoms or complications when zinc is in range—this is a sign that the body is operating in balance in one of its key systems.

To keep zinc in a healthy range, it helps to take a root-cause approach that supports the whole system, not just the mineral itself. Starting with nutrition, foods rich in zinc like oysters, beef, pumpkin seeds, and lentils can be part of your regular diet. These provide bioavailable zinc that’s easier for the body to absorb. Supporting gut health with probiotics and different types of fiber can also help maintain zinc status since poor digestion can limit zinc uptake. This whole-systems view ensures that zinc remains within an optimal—not just “normal”—range.

## Foods to eat

Foods with zinc, include the following. Note that the most easily absorbed are fish and meat sources, so if a vegetarian or vegan, it may not be as easy to get zinc from food.

**Nuts**

- Almonds
- Cashews
- Pumpkin seeds

**Meat**

- Beef
- Chicken
- Lamb

**Legumes**

- Chickpeas

**Fish**

- Crab
- Lobster
- Oyster

**Whole grains**

- Oats
