## Why it matters

Creatinine (not to be confused with the supplement creatine) is a waste product formed from the natural breakdown of muscle protein. The body produces it at a steady rate, with blood levels influenced by age, sex, and muscle mass. Measuring creatinine is a key way to assess kidney function. Because it comes from muscle tissue, creatinine also gives insight into muscle metabolism. People with more muscles tend to have higher baseline levels. However, abnormally high levels may signal muscle breakdown or other health concerns, while consistently low levels may suggest low muscle mass, malnutrition, fluid overload, pregnancy, or, in some cases, liver disease.

Monitoring creatinine is especially important for those with chronic conditions like diabetes or high blood pressure, which can increase the risk of kidney damage. Some medications can also impair kidney function. Tracking creatinine helps clinicians spot changes early and adjust treatments as needed to protect kidney health.

Since levels vary from person to person, a creatinine test isn't used in isolation. Instead, it acts as a signal for further investigation, ideally alongside other tests like blood urea nitrogen (BUN) to offer a clearer picture of overall kidney health.

- **High creatinine:** When creatinine levels rise above normal, it can indicate that the kidneys are not effectively filtering waste, which may suggest the presence of kidney dysfunction or disease.
- **Low creatinine:** When creatinine levels fall below normal, it may reflect low muscle mass, malnutrition, or muscle loss. Other potential causes include fluid overload, pregnancy, or, in some cases, liver disease, which can impair the production of creatinine. While not always a cause for concern on its own, low creatinine can serve as a clue that prompts further evaluation, especially when considered alongside other clinical findings.

**Summary**

Creatinine in the blood is an important marker of kidney function, as it is a waste product that the kidneys filter out. High levels of blood creatinine can indicate impaired kidney function, which can lead to various health problems if not managed properly. Monitoring blood creatinine levels can help in the early detection of kidney issues, allowing for dietary and lifestyle interventions to support kidney health and overall well-being.

**Optimal ranges**

The optimal range for creatinine is 0.57-1.00 mg/dL. However, a result within this range does not necessarily rule out kidney dysfunction, as blood creatinine is not sensitive to early stages of renal damage.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Creatinine can fluctuate for many reasons, even day to day. One major factor is muscle mass: because creatinine comes from muscle metabolism, people with more muscle generally have higher levels, while those with less (like older adults or people with chronic illness) often have lower levels. Physical activity can also cause temporary spikes due to increased muscle use or breakdown. Diet plays a role too—eating cooked meat, which contains creatine that is converted into creatinine in the muscles, can temporarily raise levels. Hydration matters as well: Dehydration can concentrate creatinine in the blood, while drinking lots of fluids can dilute it. That's why a single creatinine result doesn't always tell the full story—doctors often look at trends over time rather than one number.

Your creatinine is within range. You can help maintain healthy kidneys by drinking adequate amounts of water (12-15 cups per day). It also helps to have stable blood pressure, blood sugar, and weight.

## Causes

Levels may decrease due to too much protein in the diet, too much vitamin C, antibiotic use, or overhydration. Conversely, levels may increase due to high blood pressure, unhealthy exposure to an environmental toxin or heavy metal, specifically lead, cardiometabolic disease, diabetes, urinary tract infection, or insulin resistance.

## Foods to eat

You can help support healthy creatinine levels with proper hydration and healthy amounts of protein in your diet.

## Foods to limit

As kidneys try to filter the good and bad from the body, refined sugars and starches, and ultra-processed foods make that job more difficult as the additives and processes that go into making them complicate the filtering process. It's beneficial to reduce intake of those types of foods in order to keep kidneys working optimally.
