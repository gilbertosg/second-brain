TPO antibodies are immune system proteins that mistakenly attack the thyroid, indicating potential thyroid dysfunction.

## Why it matters

Thyroid peroxidase (TPO) is an enzyme essential for the production of thyroid hormones, which regulate the body's metabolism. TPO antibodies are proteins produced by the immune system that mistakenly target and attack the TPO enzyme.

High levels of TPO antibodies are commonly found in Hashimoto's thyroiditis, an autoimmune condition where the immune system attacks the thyroid gland, leading to hypothyroidism (underactive thyroid).

TPO antibodies can also be present in Graves' disease, another autoimmune disorder that causes hyperthyroidism (overactive thyroid).

The presence of TPO antibodies can indicate an increased risk of developing thyroid dysfunction, even if thyroid hormone levels are currently normal. This is important for early intervention and management.

Measuring TPO antibodies helps monitor the progression of autoimmune thyroid diseases and the effectiveness of treatments.

Tracking TPO antibodies, along with other biomarkers of inflammation and immune function, allows clinicians to intervene early, prevent complications, and tailor individualized treatments to manage symptoms and slow disease progression. An individualized treatment strategy would include an anti-inflammatory diet, regular exercise, stress management, and personalized supplements to help reduce overall inflammation and improve immune function.

### Summary

TPO antibodies are important because they help diagnose autoimmune thyroid diseases like Hashimoto's thyroiditis and Graves' disease, which can lead to thyroid dysfunction. A comprehensive root cause approach includes checking for TPO antibody levels and creating an individualized treatment plan with diet, lifestyle changes, and supplements to address the root cause and support overall immune health.

### Optimal ranges

The optimal reference range for thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibodies is below 9 IU/mL. Levels above this threshold may indicate the presence of autoimmune thyroid diseases such as Hashimoto's thyroiditis or Graves' disease. Elevated TPO antibodies suggest an ongoing autoimmune attack on the thyroid, which can lead to thyroid dysfunction over time.

The laboratory's reference range for this biomarker is considered the optimal range and is reflected accordingly. The ranges charted alongside your results are provided by the laboratory. These are statistically derived from general populations in the United States, which some consider to be an inherently unhealthy benchmark for comparison. However, for some biomarkers, the laboratory reference range and the optimal reference range are the same, such as calcium or sodium. In contrast, the optimal ranges listed directly above are established by medical doctors with their own clinical experience and the scientific literature. Optimal ranges are often narrower and meant to identify patterns of a disease before it manifests. It is important to monitor changes in your results over time for a reliable understanding of your health.

## Summary

TPO antibodies below the reference range generally indicate no significant autoimmune attack on the thyroid gland.

A below-range TPO antibody level does not rule out the diagnosis of Hashimoto's thyroiditis or Graves' disease, as individuals with these conditions may have other autoantibodies. If symptoms are present, a more comprehensive evaluation with other autoimmune biomarkers may be necessary.

## Symptoms

Possible symptoms of thyroid dysfunction, which can occur regardless of TPO antibodies, include:

- Decreased range of motion in joints
- Fatigue
- Low-grade fever
- Persistent pain, swelling, and stiffness in the joints
- Morning stiffness
- Firm nodules under the skin
- Unintentional weight loss

These symptoms may indicate an autoimmune response leading to clinical manifestations of thyroid dysfunction. If symptoms persist with low or undetectable TPO antibodies, a healthcare provider may suggest other tests, like checking for TBG, TSI antibodies, and other autoimmune biomarkers.
