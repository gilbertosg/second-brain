## Why it matters

Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) is a key enzyme mainly found in liver and muscle cells, where it helps process amino acids (the building blocks of proteins) to help create energy. Normally, ALT stays inside liver and muscle cells, and only tiny amounts are released into the blood. But when liver cell membranes become damaged due to oxidative stress, toxins, or inflammation, ALT is often released into the bloodstream, where it serves as a signal that something may be affecting liver health. Tracking ALT levels over time shows how well the liver handles challenges like imbalanced glucose or lipid metabolism, certain toxins, or potential other liver changes.

ALT is just one piece of the puzzle and is best assessed with other blood markers that collectively provide insight into liver function. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) is another enzyme that helps evaluate liver cell injury, while gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) offers clues about bile health (bile is the fluid that helps digest fats). Interpreting these markers together provides a deeper understanding of whether issues like alcohol use, certain medications, fatty liver, or infections are stressing the liver to potentially help guide tailored lifestyle changes.

Imbalanced levels of ALT can provide information about the liver.

- **Low ALT:** Low ALT, which can reflect good liver health, is not always a concern, but it may also signal liver changes or changes in vitamin B6 status that should be explored with other tests to provide a better snapshot of liver function.
- **High ALT:** ALT may signal liver stress or damage which can occur from conditions such as fatty liver, alcohol, medications (e.g. acetaminophen), bacterial dysbiosis in the gut, or viral infections like hepatitis B or C. Very high levels may require more immediate testing.

**Summary**

Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) is a key enzyme mainly found in the liver that can potentially signal whether the body is in a state of imbalance. Out-of-range levels may signal liver stress, which can be a potential clue to help uncover root causes.

**Optimal ranges**

For healthy adults, ALT levels are typically 4-36 U/L for males and 4-28 U/L for females. Higher numbers may be seen in those with alterations in liver function, exposure to certain environmental toxins, or who are overweight, obese, or have type 2 diabetes.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

ALT levels can change from day to day due to many factors. Certain dietary changes, increases in alcohol consumption, intense exercise, or taking certain medications and supplements can cause temporary increases. Because of these normal fluctuations, one slightly high result may not reflect ongoing liver concerns, but repeated elevations over time may signal the need for further evaluation. Mild or borderline ALT elevations are common, often transient, and may resolve without intervention. Up to 30% of elevated liver enzyme results normalize within three weeks.

## Summary

When alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels are higher than normal, it often suggests that liver cells are under stress and are releasing more of this enzyme into the bloodstream. The degree of elevation can offer clues: sharp spikes tend to reflect sudden liver stress, while mild increases are more often linked to chronic or temporary factors. High ALT shows that something may be affecting the liver, but it doesn’t reveal exactly what’s causing it or how serious it is, so follow-up testing or monitoring is often needed to learn more.

If present, symptoms usually relate to the underlying condition and may include fatigue, mild nausea, yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice), abdominal discomfort, darker urine, or mild swelling. Many people with elevated ALT have no symptoms at all.

Mild increases often go back to normal on their own, especially when linked to temporary or lifestyle-related causes. Still, finding the reason behind a high ALT is an important step toward protecting long-term liver health.

**Root cause approach**

A root cause approach to addressing high ALT involves identifying and managing factors contributing to elevated levels, such as chronic inflammation, autoimmune diseases, toxic exposures, and lifestyle influences. Greater detail on food, lifestyle, and other considerations is provided in the sections that follow, but a general overview includes:

- **Nutrition:** Diets rich in antioxidants, such as the Mediterranean diet, help reduce oxidative stress, which may support healthy ALT levels. In contrast, the Western diet—high in processed foods, red and processed meats, and sugary drinks, and low in fruits, vegetables, and fiber—can promote inflammatory imbalance, metabolic disorders, and changes in gut and mitochondrial health.
- **Lifestyle:** Maintaining a balanced routine that includes managing stress, getting enough sleep, and exercising moderately can help reduce inflammation in the body and support liver health.
- **Gut health:** Gut imbalances could lead to poor nutrient absorption and inflammatory imbalances. Testing to check for inflammatory bowel issues or gut microbial changes could help support the gut-liver axis.
- **Supportive therapies:** Probiotics may help improve gut barrier function and support inflammatory balance. N-acetylcysteine (NAC) helps replenish glutathione, the body’s master antioxidant.
- **Pharmaceutical adjustments:** Some medications may cause elevated ALT levels. Common examples include acetaminophen, certain antibiotics (like amoxicillin-clavulanate), NSAIDs, and others, with acetaminophen being the most frequently implicated medication.
- **Follow-up and prevention:** Repeat ALT and liver function tests every 3-6 months. A preventative focus involves maintaining a Mediterranean diet, doing regular physical activity, managing stress, minimizing toxin exposure, and optimizing gut health, which all may help support optimal liver function.

## Causes

**Root causes of high ALT**

High ALT levels usually indicate stress or injury to liver cells, but can also result from conditions affecting other organs or tissues. The underlying causes range from liver diseases and genetic disorders to heart failure, muscle injury, metabolic issues, and certain digestive or hormonal conditions.

**Diet and lifestyle factors**

- **Diet:** The Western diet—high in processed foods, red and processed meats, and sugary drinks, and low in fruits, vegetables, and fiber—is linked to increased inflammation, metabolic disorders, and poorer gut and mitochondrial health..
- **Stress:** Chronic stress has been linked in clinical studies to elevated inflammatory markers like high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α), potentially affecting liver function.
- **Lack of sleep:** Poor sleep can disrupt immune balance, alter immune cell activity, and potentially increase pro-inflammatory signals.

**Other factors**

- **Gut health issues:** Low stomach acid, SIBO, and dysbiosis can contribute to inflammatory imbalance and changes in the gut-liver axis.
- **Inflammation:** Over time, states of inflammatory imbalance can potentially contribute to liver cell damage and lead to higher ALT levels.
- **Oxidative stress:** High levels of reactive oxygen species, often from stress or toxins, can damage liver cells.
- **Metabolic Syndrome:** Metabolic syndrome can directly contribute to liver cell dysfunction through insulin resistance, inflammatory imbalance, and oxidative stress.
- **Genetics:** Genetic disorders like Wilson's disease and hereditary hemochromatosis can directly disrupt liver function by causing toxic metal buildup or protein misfolding, leading to inflammation and cirrhosis.

**Medications**

There are many medications that can be toxic to the liver, the most common of which include acetaminophen, amoxicillin-clavulanate, isoniazid, statins, methotrexate, diclofenac, erythromycin, valproate, phenytoin, and anabolic steroids.

**Environmental toxin exposure**

Certain environmental toxins can create inflammation, which can cause conditions that raise ALT levels.

- **Heavy metals:** Observational studies found metals like cadmium, lead, and arsenic connected to liver damage.
- **High-intensity traffic-related air pollution:** Most commonly found near major roads, busy intersections, congested urban centers, and transportation hubs where vehicle activity is dense and frequent.
- **Mold/mycotoxins:** Certain mold-related toxins found in the air, certain foods, or the environment can weaken the gut and lungs’ protective barriers, potentially allowing harmful bacteria to enter. They can simultaneously influence inflammatory pathways and overload cells with oxidative stress, creating a cycle of immune overactivation and tissue damage.
- **Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs):** Primarily found in legacy equipment, contaminated sites, soil, water, air, and the food chain, and persist in the environment decades after their production was halted.
- **Polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS):** PFAS are found in water, soil, air, food, and a wide array of consumer and industrial products, making them ubiquitous in both the environment and daily life.

For more information, visit [Environmental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/environmental-health-101).

**Substance use**

Alcohol is the most common substance causing liver damage, leading to fatty liver, hepatitis, cirrhosis, and potential deleterious changes through direct toxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammation. In addition, tobacco and cocaine can also injure the liver; tobacco via carcinogens and fibrosis, and cocaine through reduced blood flow and cell death.

## Associated conditions

**General conditions and diseases**

Various liver and non-liver conditions can be associated with elevated ALT levels, ranging from common metabolic issues to more specific diseases.

**Liver-related conditions**

- Viral hepatitis
- Alcohol-related liver disease
- Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)
- Autoimmune hepatitis
- Genetic disorders (hemochromatosis, Wilson’s disease)

**Non-liver conditions**

- Heart failure (reduced liver blood flow)
- Severe muscle injury
- Celiac disease or thyroid disorders
- Bile duct blockages (e.g., gallstones)
- Persistent ALT elevation may also signal metabolic issues (obesity, diabetes)

## Foods to limit

Certain foods may place extra stress on the liver and contribute to higher alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels. A root cause approach focuses on reducing foods that promote inflammation or disrupt liver and digestive function.

- **Refined sugars and sweetened foods:** Foods such as soda, desserts, and pastries are linked to fat buildup in the liver and increased risk of liver dysfunction.
- **Ultra-processed foods:** Packaged snacks, fast food, and foods with additives are associated with adverse liver outcomes.
- **Fried foods and foods high in trans or saturated fats:** Foods such as french fries, fried chicken, and processed baked goods can contribute to inflammatory imbalances.
- **Processed meats:** Meats like sausages, bacon, and hot dogs are very high in sodium, often making up a large part of the daily salt limit, which can increase the risk for certain chronic illnesses.
- **Alcohol:** The liver is responsible for breaking down alcohol. Chronic alcohol consumption leads to the buildup of toxic metabolites, resulting in increased oxidative stress, inflammation, fat accumulation, and disruption of normal liver metabolism.

For more information, visit [Nutrition 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/nutrition-101).

**Generalized**

Generalized supplements aimed at improving liver health, helping balance inflammation and oxidative stress, include:

**Milk thistle (silymarin)**

- **Benefits:** Supports liver health by helping optimize liver cell integrity, detoxification pathways, and inflammatory balance.
- **Dosage:** 200 mg.
- **Frequency:** Twice daily.
- **Mechanism of action:** Helps modulate pathways related to antioxidative status and inflammation, and helps support liver cell repair and function.

**N-acetylcysteine (NAC)**

- **Benefits:** Helps promote glutathione production, antioxidative status, and healthy liver detoxification pathways.
- **Dosage:** 600 mg.
- **Frequency:** Twice daily.
- **Mechanism of action:** Serves as a precursor to glutathione, directly scavenges free radicals, and helps promote phase II detoxification enzyme activity.

**Curcumin**

- **Benefits:** Helps support inflammatory balance by helping modulate C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) levels.
- **Dosage:** 500 mg.
- **Frequency:** Twice daily.
- **Mechanism of action:** Inhibits the signaling of certain pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) and NF-κB, modulates immune cell activity to help reduce oxidative stress, and helps optimize cellular integrity.

**Vitamin E**

- **Benefits:** Targeted support for liver cell health and antioxidative status.
- **Dosage:** 400 IU/day
- **Frequency:** Once daily.
- **Mechanism of action:** Neutralizes reactive oxygen species (ROS) via antioxidant activity and helps modulate certain pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6).

**Alpha-lipoic acid (ALA)**

- **Benefits** : Potent antioxidant that can replenish glutathione, curb liver fat, and quiet inflammation in conditions such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.
- **Dosage** : 300-600 mg.
- **Frequency** : Once or twice daily with meals.
- **Mechanism of action** : Cycles between reduced and oxidized forms to neutralize free radicals, chelate pro-oxidant metals, and activate AMPK pathways that boost hepatic fat-burning and phase-II detoxification.

**Selenium (as selenomethionine)**

- **Benefits** : Trace mineral co-factor for glutathione-peroxidase that shields liver cells from oxidative damage and supports recovery in toxin- or virus-related liver stress.
- **Dosage** : 100-200 mcg.
- **Frequency** : Once daily.
- **Mechanism of action** : Supplies the “spark” for antioxidant enzymes, lowers lipid peroxidation, and modulates immune signaling that can otherwise drive chronic hepatic inflammation.

## Symptoms

Many people with high ALT have no symptoms, especially early on, and the elevation is often discovered through routine blood work. When symptoms do occur, they’re usually related to the underlying liver condition and could potentially include fatigue, nausea, abdominal discomfort or swelling, jaundice (yellowing of the skin or eyes), dark urine, itching, and loss of appetite.

Regular moderate-intensity exercise, such as brisk walking, cycling, or swimming, helps train the immune system to respond in a more balanced way. This supports overall metabolic health and may help improve markers related to liver function and inflammation. Very intense exercise, in contrast, can temporarily raise inflammation, potentially altering liver markers.

For more detailed information on exercise, please refer to [Exercise 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/exercise-101).

**Weight management**

Excess body fat, particularly around the abdomen, can promote fat accumulation in liver cells. This process can release inflammatory signals and excess fats that overwhelm the liver, potentially leading to cellular stress, imbalanced blood sugar regulation, and liver cell damage and resulting in increased ALT levels that signify liver stress. Even moderate weight loss (around 7% of body weight) has been shown in clinical studies to significantly improve all key features of metabolic syndrome, including blood pressure, blood sugar, triglycerides, and cholesterol, while helping balance inflammation and insulin resistance.

Refer to [Weight Management 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/weight-management-101) for foundational principles for healthy weight management.

**Sleep optimization**

Adequate sleep helps maintain overall liver health by supporting stable circadian rhythms, which help regulate key liver enzymes and metabolic processes. While direct evidence linking sleep patterns to ALT levels is limited, research shows that disrupted circadian rhythms can negatively impact liver function and increase oxidative stress. Prioritizing consistent, quality sleep may help support the body’s antioxidant balance and healthy liver enzyme activity.

Visit [Sleep 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/sleep-101) for practical tips on improving sleep.

**Mental health and stress reduction**

Improving gut health and microbiome balance may help address high ALT by optimizing liver function and lowering inflammation. Key strategies include:

- **SIBO management:** Treating small intestinal bacterial overgrowth helps reduce gut-derived toxins that may burden the liver.

## Additional tests

**Root cause approach to diagnosing high ALT**

A root cause approach to diagnosing high alanine aminotransferase (ALT) involves investigating underlying triggers like substance use, inflammation, or metabolic stress to pinpoint why this liver enzyme is elevated.

The need for specific tests depends on your individual risk profile, clinical symptoms, family history, and the presence of other health conditions, as determined by your healthcare provider. *Here is a list of tests that may be relevant if ALT levels are out of range, with those available through your Function membership noted below.*

**Blood testing**

- **Aspartate transaminase (AST):** This assay measures a complementary liver enzyme to assess the extent of hepatic injury, correlating with elevated ALT.
- **Alkaline phosphatase (ALP)** : Assesses for cholestatic or biliary causes when elevated alongside ALT.
- **Gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT):** It evaluates hepatic stress from alcohol or toxic exposure, providing insight into potential causes of increased ALT.
- **Total bilirubin:** This test quantifies a hepatic byproduct, indicating whether impaired bile excretion contributes to ALT elevation.
- **Albumin:** It assesses hepatic synthetic capacity, revealing if diminished liver function is associated with higher ALT levels.
- **Prothrombin time (PT/INR)** : Assesses the liver’s synthetic function and helps gauge the severity of liver disease.
- **Blood counts and indices** : Screens for infection, inflammation, or low platelets, which can be linked to chronic liver disease.
- **Insulin, fasting glucose, hemoglobin A1c:** This analysis evaluates glucose regulation, identifying insulin resistance as a potential driver of hepatic fat accumulation and increased ALT.
- **Advanced lipid panel** : Evaluates for metabolic syndrome and fatty liver disease, which are frequent causes of high ALT.
- **Kidney function:** Kidney function may be affected in advanced liver disease or hepatorenal syndrome.
- **Lipase, amylase** : Pancreatitis can sometimes cause secondary liver enzyme elevations.
- **Thyroid function** : Thyroid dysfunction can contribute to metabolic syndrome and fatty liver.

**Urine testing**

Urine tests may be considered if there is suspicion of kidney involvement or metabolic complications.

- **Albumin:** This test detects elevated urinary protein levels, suggesting renal involvement that may reflect systemic stress, potentially associated with increased ALT.
- **Creatinine:** It assesses renal filtration efficiency, providing insight into systemic metabolic demands that could influence hepatic function and elevate ALT.

**Diagnostic imaging (CT, MRI, ultrasound, etc.)**

- **Ultrasound:** This test uses sound waves to picture the liver, showing if fat or swelling is causing ALT to climb.
- **CT (computed tomography):** Takes detailed X-ray images to spot liver damage or blockages that might explain high ALT.
- **MRI with body composition assessment:** Provides detailed imaging of the liver to detect potential inflammation, liver injury, liver masses, or other pathologies causing elevated ALT. The MRI can also be a useful tool to assess for evidence of fatty liver. The body composition assessment will measure the percentage of liver fat content.
- **MRCP (magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography):** Focuses on bile ducts, checking for clogs or issues that could boost ALT.
- **FibroScan (transient elastography):** This special ultrasound measures liver stiffness, revealing if scarring or fat is behind the ALT spike.

**Nutrient imbalances**

- **Vitamin B6:** This test measures B6 levels, which support liver function; low amounts might worsen ALT elevation in fatty liver conditions.
- **Magnesium:** It assesses magnesium, which is essential for liver health; a shortage could play a role in high ALT when the liver’s stressed.
- **Zinc:** This checks zinc, which helps the liver fight damage; an imbalance may add to high ALT from inflammation.
- **Selenium** : This antioxidant cofactor helps detoxify reactive species that damage liver enzymes.
- **Copper and ceruloplasmin:** Copper is needed for liver processes. Too much or too little might increase ALT by affecting liver balance. It also helps assess for Wilson's disease in younger patients with unexplained ALT elevation.
- **Vitamin D:** This test looks at vitamin D, which aids liver repair; low levels could contribute to ALT spikes in metabolic issues.
- **Iron studies (iron, ferritin, TIBC)** : Measures stored iron since excess iron can harm the liver and push ALT higher in fatty liver disease. Detects hemochromatosis or iron overload as a potential liver stressor.
- **Methionine and choline** : Shortfalls impair fat export from the liver, fueling ALT‑rising steatosis.
- **Electrolytes (chloride, potassium, carbon dioxide, calcium, magnesium, sodium)** : Electrolyte imbalances may occur in advanced liver disease or with complications.

**Microbiome and gut health assessment**

High ALT levels may indicate liver stress by affecting the gut microbiome (the bacteria in the intestines), which plays a role in the pathway connecting gut and liver health. Imbalances in the gut may cause inflammation or send harmful substances to the liver, raising ALT.

- **Stool microbiome analysis:** Identifies imbalances in gut bacteria (dysbiosis), which may produce toxins or inflammation that damage the liver, elevating ALT.
- **Fecal calprotectin:** Measures gut inflammation, which can signal dysbiosis; inflammation may stress the liver, contributing to higher ALT levels.
- **Intestinal permeability:** Checks for a leaky gut lining, which may allow toxins to reach the liver, triggering damage that increases ALT.
- **Short-chain fatty acids (SCFA):** Assesses levels of beneficial bacterial byproducts like butyrate; low SCFAs from dysbiosis may weaken gut and liver health, potentially raising ALT.

See [Gut Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/gut-health-101) for more information.

**Immune system evaluation**

- **High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP)** : Measures low-grade inflammation; high hs-CRP may signal chronic inflammation linked to liver damage, often raising ALT.
- **Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)** : Assesses general inflammation; elevated ESR may indicate immune activity that irritates the liver, contributing to higher ALT.
- **Interleukin-6 (IL-6)** : Detects a specific inflammatory protein; increased IL-6 may promote liver inflammation and injury, often elevating ALT.
- **IgG food sensitivity and IgE food allergy testing** : Identifies foods triggering immune reactions; IgG and IgE-mediated responses may cause gut inflammation, which can burden the liver via the gut-liver axis, possibly increasing ALT.
- **Autoimmune markers (ANA, ASMA):** Screens for autoimmune hepatitis, especially in unexplained cases.
- **Hepatitis panel (A, B, C):** Identifies viral hepatitis, a common cause of elevated ALT.
- **Infection testing** : If suspected. Some vector-borne and viral diseases can raise ALT.
- **Celiac disease panel** : Celiac disease can cause liver inflammation and elevated ALT, especially in undiagnosed or untreated cases.
- **IgG subclass 1-4, IgG, IgA, IgM** : Elevated immunoglobulins (hypergammaglobulinemia) are common in autoimmune hepatitis and chronic liver disease.

**Environmental and toxin testing**

- **Mycotoxins/mold** : Measures mold toxins like aflatoxins; these can damage liver cells, increasing ALT through inflammation and oxidative stress.
- **Heavy metals** : Detects metals like lead, mercury, or cadmium; these can harm the liver, raising ALT by causing oxidative stress and inflammation.
- **Pesticide metabolites** : Identifies residues like organochlorines or glyphosate; these can damage the liver, elevating ALT by promoting inflammation and fat buildup.
- **Bisphenol A (BPA)** : Measures levels of BPA, a plastic-related chemical; it can impair liver function, increasing ALT through inflammation and lipid accumulation.
- **Hormone disruptors** : Measures levels of chemicals like phthalates and bisphenols that cause hormone imbalances; these can disrupt liver metabolism, increasing ALT by promoting fat buildup and inflammation.
- **PFAS** : These forever chemicals found in a wide array of consumer and industrial products can impact liver function.

**Advanced diagnostics**

- **Fibrosis 4 (FIB-4) index:** A simple test that combines age and common lab results to estimate liver scarring risk. Calculates a fibrosis score using age, AST, ALT, and platelet count to screen for liver scarring in fatty-liver disease; a result under 1.3 suggests low risk, while a result above 2.67 indicates advanced fibrosis.
- **NAFLD fibrosis score (NFS):** A formula using age, weight/BMI, diabetes status, AST/ALT ratio, platelet count, and albumin to estimate the risk of advanced liver scarring in fatty liver disease. A score over 0.676 indicates a high risk of advanced fibrosis.
- **Liver fibrosis panel that combines α2-macroglobulin, haptoglobin, apolipoprotein A1, GGT, and total bilirubin with age and sex to estimate fibrosis stage:** Uses a panel of blood markers and other factors—α2-macroglobulin, haptoglobin, apolipoprotein A1, GGT, total bilirubin plus age and gender—to estimate both fibrosis and fat accumulation in the liver, outputting a stage from F0 (none) to F4 (severe).
- **Three-marker enhanced liver fibrosis (ELF) panel (hyaluronic acid, PIIINP, TIMP-1) to gauge active scar formation test:** A blood test that measures three markers of scar tissue activity in the liver, with a higher score signifying more scarring. Measures hyaluronic acid, PIIINP (Procollagen Type III N-Terminal Propeptide, a marker of new collagen production and tissue scarring), and TIMP-1 (Tissue Inhibitor of Metalloproteinase-1, a protein that slows collagen breakdown, linked to scar buildup) to quantify active scar formation; an ELF score above 9.8 signifies a high risk of advanced fibrosis.
- **AST-to-platelet ratio index (APRI):** A simple calculation using AST level and platelet count; values over 1.0 suggest significant liver fibrosis, and values over 2.0 indicate cirrhosis.
- **Serum PRO-C3 (pro-collagen III peptide) level:** Assesses formation of collagen III (a protein made during scar formation in the liver), an early marker of fibrotic activity; higher PRO-C3 levels correlate with more advanced liver scarring.
- **CK-18 (cytokeratin-18 fragments):** This test checks for pieces of protein released when liver cells die, helping to tell if there is active inflammation in fatty liver disease. It helps to distinguish inflammatory non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)/metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) from simple fat accumulation.
- **Oxidative stress** **markers** : Evaluates oxidative damage through tests like urinary 8-hydroxydeoxyguanosine or plasma F2-isoprostanes; these measure stress on liver cells, often raising ALT due to inflammation and injury.
- **Organic acids:** Detects imbalances in urine organic acids, reflecting microbial overgrowth or mitochondrial issues; these can indicate liver strain, increasing ALT through inflammation or fat buildup.
- **Genetic testing:** Analyzes genes like *PNPLA3* or *HFE* for variants linked to liver disease; these can reveal inherited risks that elevate ALT by promoting fat accumulation or inflammation.

**Cognitive assessments**

High ALT levels, which often indicate liver stress or damage, may influence cognitive function through the liver-brain connection. When the liver struggles, it can fail to clear toxins like ammonia, allowing them to reach the brain and potentially cause confusion, memory issues, or reduced focus, especially in conditions like non-alcoholic fatty liver disease or hepatitis. Chronic inflammation or metabolic imbalances from liver dysfunction might also disrupt brain health, contributing to these cognitive changes. Cognitive testing, such as tasks measuring memory, attention, or problem-solving, can help by identifying these effects, offering insight into whether liver issues are affecting mental performance.

For more information about maintaining optimum brain health, including cognition, memory, and ways to reduce the risk of age-related neurodegenerative disorders, visit [Brain Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/brain-health-101).

## Follow up

**Medical interventions**

Collaboration with your healthcare provider ensures a personalized approach to managing alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels and their potential root causes. Consideration of all symptoms, laboratory test results, and lifestyle factors can guide a treatment plan tailored to your specific needs and health goals.

**Procedures**

Medical procedures might be considered for conditions and diseases tied to high ALT levels to help determine and potentially treat root causes.

- **Liver ultrasound or other imaging (CT, MRI):** May help provide insights into how the liver is doing structurally to help rule in/out potential root causes.
- **Liver biopsy** : This procedure could be used in later evaluation in certain instances to assess the extent of liver damage in conditions like NAFLD or hepatitis, providing insight into why ALT is elevated.

**Retesting**

Retesting ALT is generally recommended about every six months to monitor changes and assess the effectiveness of a management plan, unless a healthcare provider suggests a different interval based on individual health needs.
