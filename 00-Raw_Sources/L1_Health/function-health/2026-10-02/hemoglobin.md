## Why it matters

Hemoglobin is a protein in red blood cells that acts like a delivery truck for oxygen, carrying it from the lungs to the rest of the body and then helping to remove carbon dioxide. This process is essential for keeping the cells energized and functioning properly. Measuring hemoglobin levels provides a snapshot of the body’s ability to deliver oxygen efficiently, making it an essential marker of health. When hemoglobin levels are abnormal, it can indicate conditions such as anemia, dehydration, or, if chronically abnormal, lung disease, which can significantly affect energy levels and organ function.

Hemoglobin is closely related to other biomarkers such as hematocrit, red blood cell count, and iron levels. These related markers provide a more comprehensive view of blood health and oxygen transport. For example, when low hemoglobin levels are found alongside low iron stores (ferritin), it often points to iron deficiency anemia, a condition where the body doesn’t have enough iron to produce healthy red blood cells. Conversely, high hemoglobin paired with a high hematocrit may indicate dehydration or conditions like polycythemia, where the blood becomes too thick. These connections help healthcare providers pinpoint the underlying causes of symptoms such as fatigue, weakness, or shortness of breath, leading to more personalized treatment plans.

- **Low hemoglobin:** May indicate anemia, often due to iron deficiency, chronic inflammation, or blood loss. Common symptoms include fatigue, dizziness, and difficulty concentrating because the body's tissues aren’t getting enough oxygen.
- **High hemoglobin** : Can be a sign of dehydration, chronic lung conditions, or rare disorders like polycythemia vera. This can increase the risk of blood clots and place extra strain on the heart and blood vessels.

Both high and low hemoglobin levels should be investigated to uncover their root causes—like diet, chronic conditions, or lifestyle factors—and addressed with tailored strategies such as nutritional changes, hydration, and stress management to support healthy red blood cell production.

**Optimal ranges**

Optimal hemoglobin levels vary based on factors such as age, sex, and health status, but generally the optimal range falls close to the typical reference range, between 11.10-15.90 g/dL. In some cases, optimal levels may be towards the higher end of the reference range, as this ensures adequate tissue oxygenation.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Hemoglobin levels can vary from day to day because they are influenced by many factors such as how well you are hydrated, your recent meals, your activity level, and even the time of day. For instance, if you’re dehydrated, your blood becomes more concentrated, which can temporarily increase your hemoglobin reading. Similarly, factors like living at high altitude, experiencing stress, or having an infection can also lead to fluctuations. That's why doctors often look at trends in hemoglobin over time rather than relying on a single test result.

## Summary

A hemoglobin level within the normal range indicates that the blood's capacity to carry oxygen is sufficient, suggesting adequate red blood cell production and maintenance. This typically reflects balanced nutrition, healthy bone marrow function, and an absence of conditions like anemia or polycythemia.

A root cause approach to maintaining a normal hemoglobin level involves ongoing evaluation of factors that support red blood cell production. Nutritional strategies include ensuring adequate intake of iron, vitamin B12, and folate (B9) through a balanced diet rich in lean proteins, leafy greens, and legumes.

Regular follow-up testing, including complete blood counts and nutrient panels, ensures early detection of imbalances and helps sustain healthy hemoglobin levels over time.
