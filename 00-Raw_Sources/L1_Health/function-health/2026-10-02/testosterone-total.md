## Why it matters

Testosterone is a hormone primarily produced in the testes that supports male health by influencing muscle mass, bone strength, fat distribution, red blood cell production, mood, and sexual function. It plays key roles during puberty and reproduction, with levels fluctuating throughout life and declining naturally with age. Testosterone is found in three main forms in the blood: free (unbound and immediately available), loosely bound to a protein called albumin, and tightly bound to a protein called sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG). Measuring total testosterone looks at the sum of these forms of testosterone in the bloodstream.

Testosterone does not act alone; it interacts with other biomarkers such as luteinizing hormone (LH), sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), and estradiol. For example, SHBG regulates the amount of testosterone available to the body by binding to it. Measuring total testosterone along with other hormones helps determine if symptoms like fatigue, low sex drive (libido), or mood changes are due to hormonal imbalances, and inform treatment options like nutritional support, lifestyle changes, or hormone therapy.

- **Low testosterone levels** : Low levels may contribute to fatigue, depression, reduced muscle mass, and diminished sex drive and lead to an increased risk of cardiovascular disease and metabolic syndrome, as testosterone plays a role in regulating blood sugar and fat metabolism. In aging men, a gradual decline in testosterone can contribute to frailty and cognitive decline.
- **High testosterone levels** : While less common, elevated levels may result from anabolic steroid use, testosterone replacement therapy, or certain tumors and can lead to aggressive behavior, mood swings, and sleep disturbances. Excess testosterone has also been linked to polycythemia, a condition where excessive red blood cell production raises the risk of blood clots and other cardiovascular complications.

**Summary**

Total testosterone measures all the testosterone in the blood, both bound and free, and is essential for male health, influencing muscle strength, bone density, mood, and heart function; imbalanced levels may signal deeper issues like insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, or hormonal dysregulation. Addressing the root causes of low total testosterone—such as poor sleep, chronic stress, nutritional deficiencies, or excess body fat—rather than just treating symptoms can help restore balance and prevent long-term health complications.

**Optimal ranges**

For peak vitality, total testosterone should ideally be 500-1,000 ng/dL, though most labs define "normal" as 300-1,000 ng/dL. Levels below 500 ng/dL—common in aging men—often reflect lifestyle-driven declines (poor diet, inactivity, stress) rather than natural aging and do not support optimal hormone health. Higher levels (>500 ng/dL) correlate with better metabolic health, muscle preservation, and cardiovascular function. Free testosterone and SHBG should also be checked, as they determine active hormone availability. Since optimal levels can vary from person to person, interpreting total testosterone levels should be done in consultation with a healthcare provider, considering the broader clinical context.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Levels of testosterone naturally rise and fall throughout the day, peaking in the morning and gradually decreasing by evening. This daily fluctuation, known as diurnal variation, is controlled by the body’s internal clock (circadian rhythm). Testosterone levels also tend to naturally decline with age, dropping about 1% per year after 30. Sleep is important for testosterone production since most of it occurs during deep sleep, and poor sleep can lead to lower testosterone levels. Since testosterone levels naturally fluctuate, a single test result may not provide a complete picture of hormonal health. Tracking patterns over time or doing additional tests can give a more accurate understanding of overall testosterone function.

## Summary

A normal total testosterone level suggests that the body is producing adequate amounts of testosterone to support key physiological functions such as muscle growth, mood regulation, and sexual health. Maintaining testosterone within the normal range can indicate a balanced hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, which coordinates communication between the brain and gonads to regulate hormone production. However, some men with normal testosterone levels may still experience symptoms of low testosterone if free testosterone (the biologically active form) is low, often due to increased sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) or metabolic dysfunction. Additionally, men with testosterone at the lower end of the normal range may still be at risk for conditions such as osteoporosis, muscle loss, and cognitive decline, particularly as they age.

A root cause approach to maintaining optimal testosterone levels involves lifestyle interventions, nutrient support, and addressing metabolic health. Regular resistance training and high-intensity exercise can enhance testosterone production by stimulating anabolic pathways. Key nutrients such as zinc, vitamin D, and magnesium help sustain testosterone synthesis and hormone signaling. Ensuring proper sleep and reducing exposure to hormone-disrupting chemicals found in plastics and pesticides may also help maintain hormonal balance.

## Self care

To maintain well-balanced testosterone, focus on well-balanced nutrition and weight. Ensure your cholesterol levels and metabolic functions are healthy. Regular and varied exercise is also important, like strength and resistance training, HIIT, and cardio.

Additionally, avoid toxins when possible. Chemicals found in pesticides, food, skincare, and household products may disrupt your endocrine system.
