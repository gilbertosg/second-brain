## Why it matters

Albumin is made by the liver and is the most common protein in the blood. Albumin helps maintain fluid balance by regulating pressure in the blood vessels (oncotic pressure) that prevents water from leaking out and causing swelling. It’s essential for keeping fluids stable and supporting vascular health. It acts like a carrier, moving nutrients, hormones, and binds medications and toxins, helping carry them to the liver or kidneys to help break them down and remove them from the body.

As a carrier, it also picks up things that don’t travel well on their own, like fatty acids and bilirubin (a product of the breakdown of old red blood cells), for transport, delivery, and waste clean-up. By grabbing onto waste products and free radicals, albumin helps keep the blood’s chemistry steady and helps support the liver’s detoxification processes.

Albumin works alongside other tests, such as total protein, bilirubin, ALT, AST, creatinine, BUN, C-reactive protein, and ESR, to provide a broader health picture. These markers assess liver performance, kidney function, and inflammation—factors that influence or are influenced by albumin levels. If alanine transaminase (ALT) is higher than normal, it may point to possible liver problems. If total protein levels are low, it could suggest the body isn’t getting or absorbing enough protein. These results help highlight areas that may need more attention or further testing to understand what’s happening inside the body:

- **High albumin (above normal range):** High albumin is rare but might signal dehydration, where the blood gets too concentrated, often from not enough water or heavy fluid loss like sweating or diarrhea.
- **Low albumin (below normal range):** Low albumin levels in the blood can happen when the liver isn’t making enough protein, which is often seen in liver conditions like cirrhosis or hepatitis. It can also result from not getting enough protein in the diet or from problems in the digestive system that make it hard for the body to absorb nutrients. In these cases, the body doesn’t have the building blocks it needs to keep albumin levels at an optimal range, which can lead to swelling and other health issues. Other potential causes include kidney damage, severe infections that deplete albumin, or certain chronic conditions that disrupt the liver’s ability to produce albumin. In these cases, low albumin may reflect the body’s difficulty managing underlying health challenges rather than being a standalone issue.

**Optimal ranges**

In healthy adults, an optimal serum albumin level is typically 4.0–5.0 g/dL. Levels in this range are often associated with strong liver function and overall metabolic health, while also helping maintain fluid balance and helping keep fluid within the bloodstream, rather than leaking into surrounding tissue.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Albumin can change for several reasons, sometimes shifting quickly over just a few months. A high albumin (above 5 g/dL) is rare but can happen if you’re dehydrated, such as after heavy sweating or diarrhea, because the blood becomes more concentrated. On the other hand, a low albumin level (below 3.5 g/dL) might be due to liver problems (like cirrhosis, where the liver makes less albumin), kidney issues (where albumin leaks into urine), or not getting enough nutrition. Big drops can also happen after surgery, during infections, or with inflammation from illnesses like cancer. When albumin is low, you might notice swelling in your legs or belly, which means your body’s fluid balance is off. Just like other lab results, albumin can swing up or down in response to changes in your health, stress, or diet.

## Summary

A normal albumin result is a sign that the liver is producing this protein at healthy levels and that the body is likely maintaining good fluid balance and nutrient transport. Albumin plays a key role in carrying hormones, medications, and other substances in the blood, and it helps prevent fluid from leaking into tissues. Values within the standard reference range—typically between 3.5-5.0 g/dL—are not usually associated with symptoms and suggest a lower likelihood of co-occurring conditions. However, as a standalone biomarker, normal albumin does not provide a complete picture of health.

Maintaining healthy albumin levels depends on several factors. Adequate protein intake helps supply the amino acids needed for albumin synthesis. Good gut health—supported by fiber and fermented foods—can help promote nutrient absorption, which helps support optimal liver health. Periodic lab testing allows for early detection of any shifts in albumin levels and can guide further evaluation if needed.
