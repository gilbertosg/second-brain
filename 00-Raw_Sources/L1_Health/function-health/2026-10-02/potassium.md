## Why it matters

Potassium is an electrolyte that supports several basic body functions. It helps regulate nerve signals, muscle contractions—especially in the heart, and fluid balance. Acting like a part of the body’s internal electrical system, potassium allows signals to move smoothly between nerves and muscles. It comes from foods such as leafy greens, avocados, bananas, beans, and sweet potatoes, and is removed from the body mainly by the kidneys.

Potassium works closely with other electrolytes, like sodium, chloride, and bicarbonate, to maintain stable cell function, blood pressure, and acid-base balance. It is closely tied to kidney function, as the kidneys are responsible for keeping potassium levels within a healthy range. When the kidneys don’t work properly, potassium can rise to unsafe levels. For this reason, potassium is often measured alongside markers like creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) to assess how well the kidneys are filtering waste. It also helps reveal how medications, such as diuretics, steroids, and other medications, may be influencing electrolyte levels. If potassium is not well-regulated, the risk of heart rhythm problems or muscle-related symptoms increases.

When potassium levels fall outside the normal range, they may signal an underlying issue.

- **High potassium** , known as hyperkalemia, can cause an irregular heartbeat, muscle weakness, or a heavy feeling in the limbs. It is often linked to kidney disease, dehydration, or medications like angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors and potassium-sparing diuretics.
- **Low potassium,** or hypokalemia, can lead to fatigue, muscle cramps, or a fluttery heartbeat. It may be caused by fluid loss from vomiting, diarrhea, or sweating, as well as by diuretics or other medications.

**Summary**

Measuring potassium provides insight into overall electrolyte balance, kidney function, and cardiovascular stability, particularly when chronic conditions are present or medications are being used that influence fluid or mineral levels.

**Optimal ranges**

These levels should always be interpreted in the context of your overall health and clinical situation. In addition, in some chronic health conditions, specialists may recommend maintaining potassium levels within a narrower range than the general reference interval. Ongoing monitoring and consultation with a healthcare professional can help ensure potassium stays within an optimal and safe range.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Potassium levels in the blood can vary from day to day due to a range of internal and lifestyle-related influences. Dietary intake, hydration status, sweating, medications (such as diuretics or certain blood pressure medications), and kidney function all play a role in regulating potassium levels. Conditions such as kidney problems, adrenal issues, or high stress hormone levels can affect how the body holds on to or gets rid of potassium. Since many factors can affect potassium levels, some variation is normal, and a single test might not provide the full picture. Looking at trends over time—along with other test results, symptoms, and medical history—can help determine whether an abnormal potassium level points to a real issue or simply reflects a temporary change.

## Summary

A potassium level within the normal range means the body has a healthy balance of this essential electrolyte, which plays a key role in muscle function, nerve signaling, and fluid balance. A normal result usually reflects that the kidneys, hormones (like aldosterone), and overall fluid status are working well together. In those with conditions like high blood pressure, diabetes, or heart disease, staying in the normal range can help protect against complications, especially when medications or stress could shift electrolyte levels.

Follow-up testing every 6 to 12 months, or after medication changes, can help track progress and ensure potassium stays in a healthy, stable range.
