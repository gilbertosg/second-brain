## Why it matters

Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) is a key hormone that helps assess thyroid function , which plays a major role in regulating metabolism, energy production, and hormone balance. TSH is produced by the pituitary gland—a pea-sized structure at the base of the brain—in response to signals from another brain region called the hypothalamus.

TSH tells the thyroid gland to release two important hormones: thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3). These hormones impact nearly every cell in the body, affecting processes like temperature regulation, heart rate, and digestion. TSH acts like a thermostat for thyroid function, helping regulate metabolism and energy levels. Measuring TSH is often used as a first-line test to check thyroid health.

If TSH is low but T3 or T4 levels are high, it usually means the thyroid is overactive, a condition called hyperthyroidism. On the opposite end, having high TSH levels and low T4, typically indicates hypothyroidism, meaning the thyroid isn’t producing enough hormones. TSH levels alone don’t always reveal the full picture. Testing thyroid antibodies, such as thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibodies, can help detect autoimmune thyroid conditions like Hashimoto’s disease or Graves’ disease, which may not be caught with TSH testing alone.

- **High TSH** : High TSH levels usually mean hypothyroidism, a condition where the thyroid doesn’t make enough hormones to keep up with the body’s needs. This can lead to symptoms such as fatigue, weight gain, depression, and sensitivity to cold. Hypothyroidism may result from iodine deficiency, autoimmune damage, or thyroid removal.
- **Low TSH** : Low TSH usually means the thyroid is making too much hormone (hyperthyroidism), so the brain signals it to slow down. Excessive thyroid hormones can cause symptoms like rapid heartbeat, weight loss, anxiety, and heat intolerance. This can be due to autoimmune disorders, nodules in the thyroid, or excessive iodine intake.

**Summary**

Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) controls how busy the thyroid works, affecting metabolism, energy levels, and overall health. TSH tells the thyroid gland to produce two important hormones: triiodothyronine (T3) and thyroxine (T4). When T3 and T4 levels are low, the pituitary gland releases more TSH to stimulate the thyroid. Conversely, if T3 and T4 are too high, the pituitary gland reduces TSH production. A root cause approach looks beyond just TSH levels, considering other factors like nutrition, stress, and thyroid autoantibodies to treat the root causes of thyroid problems.

**Optimal range**

The optimal range for thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) can vary slightly depending on the laboratory, but the generally accepted range for adults is:

- **Normal TSH levels:** 0.45 to 2.0 milli-international units per liter (mIU/L).
- **Possible hypothyroidism (underactive thyroid):** TSH levels above 4.0 mIU/L.
- **Possible hyperthyroidism (overactive thyroid):** TSH levels below 0.4 mIU/L.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Your thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels are not static and they can naturally fluctuate based on several factors, even day to day. TSH is released by the pituitary gland to help regulate how much thyroid hormone your body needs at any given moment. The thyroid gland is very sensitive to external factors. For example, TSH levels can vary based on the time of day (they’re typically higher at night, peaking in the early morning, and lower in the afternoon) , recent illness/infection, stress levels, medications, supplements - particularly biotin , or changes in diet or sleep. For women, hormone changes across the menstrual cycle can also play a role. Because of this built-in flexibility, a single TSH result doesn’t always tell the full story and should be interpreted alongside other related biomarkers and clinical symptoms, while looking at patterns over time.

## Summary

A normal thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) level suggests that the thyroid and pituitary glands are working in harmony to regulate metabolism, energy production, and overall endocrine balance. However, "normal" does not always mean optimal for every individual. Some individuals with a normal TSH may still notice subtle symptoms like fatigue, weight changes, or mild mood fluctuations due to poor thyroid hormone conversion, adrenal imbalances, autoimmune activity, or nutrient deficiencies that impact thyroid function. Monitoring other markers like free T3, free T4, and thyroid antibodies provides a more comprehensive picture of thyroid health.

A root cause approach focuses on maintaining thyroid balance by addressing underlying triggers such as gut health, inflammation, and nutrient deficiencies. Optimizing iodine, selenium, and zinc levels supports thyroid hormone production and conversion , while reducing inflammatory foods can lower autoimmune risk. Adaptogenic herbs like ashwagandha may help regulate stress hormones that impact thyroid function. If a normal TSH is accompanied by persistent symptoms, follow-up testing—including full thyroid panels with thyroid-specific antibodies, micronutrient assessments, and gut microbiome analysis—can provide deeper insights. Regular monitoring, lifestyle adjustments, and targeted supplementation ensure long-term balance and help prevent the progression of thyroid dysfunction.
