## Why it matters

High-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol is often called "good" cholesterol. HDL plays a critical role in removing excess cholesterol from the bloodstream and transporting it to the liver where it's processed and eliminated from the body through bile and eventually stool. This essential process, called "reverse cholesterol transport," helps regulate cholesterol levels and lowers the risk of cardiovascular diseases. This can protect arteries from cholesterol build-up, which can typically lead to atherosclerosis (hardening and narrowing of the arteries) and increase the risk of heart attacks and strokes.

- **Heart disease risk** : Measuring HDL levels helps assess the risk for heart disease. Low HDL levels are linked to a higher risk of heart disease, even if low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels are normal.
- **Anti-inflammatory effects** : HDL has anti-inflammatory properties that may help reduce inflammation that can damage blood vessels and lead to cardiovascular problems.
- **Antioxidant activity** : HDL helps prevent the oxidation of LDL cholesterol, which can cause plaque to build up in the arteries, leading to blockage.

**Optimizing HDL cholesterol**

While higher HDL is generally beneficial, cholesterol is more complex than just "good" or "bad." The quality and size of cholesterol particles provide more insight than the standard tests, which only measure the number of HDL or LDL by weight (measured in milligrams per deciliter or mg/dL of blood) and don't reveal the health of the particles themselves. The goal is to have more large, “fluffy”, and more functional HDL particles that are protective. Smaller, more dense HDL particles are often caused by insulin resistance, prediabetes, and type 2 diabetes, and increases the risk of heart disease. In addition to the size and quality of HDL particles, advanced tests like the HDLfx pCAD score and HDLfx pCEC, together can capture one of HDL’s most important protective roles and reveal cardiovascular risk factors that standard HDL cholesterol measurements can miss.

Raising HDL with medication has not proven effective in lowering risk. More effective approaches include aerobic exercise, reducing intake of refined carbohydrates and added sugars, increasing healthy fats like coconut oil, losing excess body weight, and avoiding smoking. Some people, due to genetics, may not respond well to dietary saturated fats such as coconut oil and should monitor their cholesterol closely.

**Summary**

HDL cholesterol is important because it helps remove excess cholesterol from the blood, reducing the risk of heart disease and stroke. A root cause approach looks at improving HDL naturally by focusing on diet, exercise, stress management, supplements, and addressing underlying health issues like inflammation or insulin resistance.

**Reference ranges and optimal ranges**

The reference ranges for HDL cholesterol in someone aged 20 and older are typically as follows:

- **Less than 40 mg/dL (for men)** and **less than 50 mg/dL (for women)** is considered low and associated with a higher risk of heart disease.
- **40-59 mg/dL** is generally considered acceptable, but higher levels are better.
- **60 mg/dL or higher** is considered the *optimal range* and protective against heart disease.

These ranges reflect general guidelines, but other factors like particle size and functionality are also important for cardiovascular risk assessment.

## Summary

When HDL cholesterol is within the normal range, it typically means that the body is effectively removing excess cholesterol from the bloodstream and transporting it to the liver for elimination. HDL, commonly known as the "good" cholesterol, helps reduce the buildup of plaque in the arteries, lowering the risk of heart disease and stroke.

Having HDL within the normal range doesn’t guarantee complete protection from cardiovascular disease. The quality and size of HDL particles also matter, as smaller, dysfunctional HDL particles may not be as effective at removing cholesterol. Advanced lipid biomarker assessments available with your Function membership provide a more comprehensive overview of lipid status and future risk of cardiovascular disease. A root cause approach focuses not just on the number but on the quality of HDL and underlying causes like inflammation, oxidative stress, and insulin resistance that can affect HDL function.

**Root cause approach**

From a root cause perspective, preserving healthy HDL levels involves addressing diet, lifestyle, and environmental factors that influence healthy lipid metabolism. A nutrient-dense diet, emphasizing healthy fats (e.g., olive oil, avocados, and fatty fish), fiber-rich foods, and antioxidant-rich fruits and vegetables, supports HDL’s ability to remove excess cholesterol from the blood. Regular aerobic exercise and resistance training further enhance HDL functionality. Stress management practices, such as mindfulness meditation, yoga, or breathing exercises, help regulate cortisol levels and chronic stress. Adequate restorative sleep and limiting exposure to environmental toxins help reduce chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, while improving cardiometabolic health. Routine health monitoring, including regular check-ups and comprehensive lab testing, can identify and address any changes early, ensuring HDL’s cardioprotective role.
