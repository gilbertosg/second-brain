## Why it matters

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) is an enzyme found throughout the body, with the highest levels in the liver, bones, and to a lesser extent, the intestines. ALP assists in nutrient acquisition, bone mineralization, and important biological processes located in the liver and other organs. In the liver, ALP is concentrated in the cells lining the bile ducts, where it supports bile flow, though its exact biological function is still not fully understood.

ALP is used as a marker of liver or bone activity. When bile flow is slowed or blocked—due to gallstones, scarring, or inflammation—ALP levels in the blood tend to rise. Similarly, increased ALP can occur when bones are growing, healing, or breaking down abnormally. That’s why high or low ALP levels act like a signal that something may be off in either the liver or the bones.

To narrow things down, ALP is often measured alongside other markers like ALT, AST, GGT, and bilirubin. If ALP and liver enzymes (like GGT) are both elevated, a liver issue is more likely. If only ALP is high, bone conditions might be the cause. Looking at these markers together helps guide further testing or monitoring.

- **High ALP** levels can signal problems with the liver, such as blocked bile ducts, hepatitis, or cirrhosis, since ALP rises when bile flow is disrupted or the liver is under stress. Higher ALP is also common in bone conditions like Paget’s disease, healing fractures, or bone cancers, because the enzyme is released when bone activity increases. Occasionally, infections, heart failure, or (more rarely) certain cancers can also cause high ALP; however it is not diagnostic for these conditions and should be monitored alongside other laboratory tests, with clinical evaluation recommended.
- **Low ALP** levels are less common and usually not clinically significant, but may result from nutrient deficiencies like low zinc or magnesium, which are cofactors for ALP’s biochemical reactions. When zinc and magnesium are low, ALP often can’t work efficiently. In rare cases, very low ALP can result from a genetic condition called hypophosphatasia, which affects bone and dental health. Occasionally, low ALP may also be seen in other illnesses or as a side effect of certain medications, though these associations are not always well understood.

**Summary**

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) is an enzyme found in the liver and bones, and measuring it can help reveal if there are underlying issues in these areas, such as liver disease or bone disorders. Looking at ALP levels alongside other tests helps identify the root cause of any imbalance, so the right steps can be taken to address what’s really going on inside the body.

**Optimal ranges**

The optimal range for alkaline phosphatase (ALP) is 30-120 U/L.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels naturally vary because this enzyme reflects real-time activity in the liver and bones. For example, if you’re healing from a bone injury or experiencing growth (like during pregnancy), ALP temporarily rises as part of the repair process. Nutrient levels also play a role: low zinc or magnesium intake can reduce ALP, since these minerals are essential for the enzyme to function properly. Medications, including common pain relievers or antibiotics, may also nudge ALP up or down.

While a single test provides a snapshot, ALP can shift even in stable health. These changes don’t always signal a problem; they often reflect normal bodily adjustments.

## Summary

To help keep alkaline phosphatase (ALP) in a healthy range, focus on supporting the body’s basic nutritional and metabolic needs. Zinc (found in pumpkin seeds and shellfish), magnesium (in leafy greens and nuts), and vitamin B6 (in fish, nuts, seeds, many fruits, vegetables, and whole grains) all play a role in enzyme function and should be part of a balanced diet. Some medications, like long-term antacids or certain drugs used for osteoporosis, may lower ALP, so it’s worth reviewing medications with a healthcare provider if levels change unexpectedly.

Taking small, consistent steps to support nutrition, movement, and stress levels helps maintain ALP within a healthy range.

## Foods to limit

Avoid toxic foods that add stress to your liver and the breakdown of molecules. Try to stay away from non-organic fruits and vegetables if you can. The pesticides used in non-organic agriculture can seep into your food and then into your body when you eat it.

## Self care

For liver health, it's beneficial to exercise regularly and maintain a well-balanced diet. Try to reduce toxins that can weaken your liver, such as heavy metals, microplastics, and chemicals in household and beauty products. Read directions on all medications, including over-the-counter NSAIDs, to avoid taking them in excess, hydrate properly, and if you drink alcohol, ensure you do so in moderation.
