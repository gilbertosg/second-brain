## Why it matters

Total protein is a blood test that measures the combined levels of two main types of proteins in the blood: albumin and globulin. Albumin, made by the liver, helps keep fluid in blood vessels and carries hormones, vitamins, and medicines throughout the body. Globulins, also made by the liver and immune system, play a key role in fighting infections and transporting nutrients. Together, these proteins are essential for building and repairing tissues, supporting immune health, and keeping body fluids balanced.

Total protein is often checked as part of a routine health panel, like the comprehensive metabolic panel, and is typically not interpreted alone. It’s closely related to other markers such as albumin, globulin, and the albumin/globulin (A/G) ratio. When a healthcare provider looks at total protein results, they also consider these other related values to get a clearer picture of liver and kidney function, immune health, and nutritional status. For example, if total protein is abnormal, more specific tests may be ordered to pinpoint which protein is out of balance and why. This information helps guide decisions about further testing, monitoring chronic conditions, or adjusting treatments, especially if there are symptoms like swelling, fatigue, or unexplained weight loss.

- **High total protein:** May indicate inflammation, infection, bone marrow disorders (like multiple myeloma), or dehydration.
- **Low total protein:** Could point to malnutrition, liver disease (where protein isn’t made properly), kidney disease (where protein is lost in urine), or digestive issues that prevent proper absorption.

Think of total protein like the “building supply inventory” for the body. If an individual doesn’t have enough supplies (low protein), the body can’t repair itself or fight off infections as well. If a person has too many supplies (high protein), it might mean the body is fighting something off, or there’s a problem with how the immune system is working. That’s how measuring total protein provides valuable clues about overall health and helps in deciding what steps to take next.

**Summary**

Total protein levels are important because they show how well the body is building and repairing tissues, fighting infections, and maintaining fluid balance. A root cause approach aims to identify the underlying causes of abnormal protein levels, such as poor diet or organ dysfunction, and focuses on restoring balance through personalized nutrition and lifestyle changes.

**Optimal ranges**

The optimal range for total serum protein in adults is typically **6.0-8.5 g/dL.** This range may vary slightly depending on laboratory standards and measurement techniques.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Total protein levels in blood can shift based on hydration status, diet, inflammation, or how well your liver and kidneys are working. For example, if you're dehydrated, your blood becomes more concentrated, and protein levels may appear higher than they really are, while overhydration can dilute them and make them seem low. Though total protein doesn't fluctuate dramatically from day to day in healthy individuals, significant physical stress, infection, a shift in protein intake or absorption, and even frequency of blood donation can lead to meaningful changes. Chronic conditions or nutrient malabsorption—like what occurs with gut imbalances—can subtly alter protein production over time, making this a valuable marker to track patterns in health rather than relying on a single reading.

## Summary

A total protein level in the normal range generally reflects a healthy balance between protein production, absorption, and breakdown, especially involving albumin and globulins—the two main proteins in the blood. This status suggests that liver function is not compromised, nutrient intake and digestion are adequate, and there’s no excessive protein loss through the kidneys or gut. In clinical terms, it may indicate stable immune function and a lack of chronic inflammation or severe organ dysfunction.

A root cause approach to maintaining a healthy total protein level includes optimizing digestion, reducing inflammation, and supporting liver and immune function through food and lifestyle. Nutritionally, a diet rich in complete proteins (like wild fish, pastured eggs, legumes, and fermented dairy) along with foods that support liver detoxification—such as broccoli, garlic, and leafy greens—can help sustain protein balance. Digestive enzymes and probiotics can support gut health, improving protein breakdown and absorption. Follow-up testing might include albumin, globulin, the albumin/globulin ratio, and markers of inflammation to detect subtle shifts before symptoms develop. This proactive strategy helps keep total protein in the optimal range and supports resilience against disease.

## Foods to eat

The recommended daily amount is 0.8 grams of protein per each kilogram of your body weight. So, using a conversation calculator can help. If you’re 140 pounds (63.4kg), that’s 51 grams of protein. That’s about one 5-ounce boneless, skinless chicken breast.

Animal proteins include grass-fed, regeneratively-raised meat such as beef, lamb, bison, venison, and elk; poultry like turkey and chicken; and wild or sustainably-raised coldwater fish including salmon, herring, mackerel, small halibut, and sardines, along with pasture-raised eggs.

For plant proteins, options consist of nuts and seeds like almonds, cashews, pecans, walnuts, macadamia nuts, hazelnuts, Brazil nuts, pistachios, chia seeds, hemp seeds, flaxseeds, pumpkin seeds, and sesame seeds; legumes including chickpeas, adzuki beans, black beans, navy beans, pinto beans, mung beans, black-eyed peas, and red or green lentils; grains such as quinoa, wild rice, millet, and amaranth; and non-GMO organic soy products like edamame, tempeh, natto, tofu, and miso.

Some green vegetables, such as asparagus, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, and spinach, as well as sea vegetables, including spirulina, kombu, and kelp, are also good sources of plant protein.
