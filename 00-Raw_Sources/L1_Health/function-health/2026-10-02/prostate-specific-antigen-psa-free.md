## Why it matters

Measuring free PSA is an important tool in assessing prostate health, particularly when it comes to screening for the risk of prostate cancer. PSA is a protein produced by the prostate gland that exists in two main forms in the blood: bound (attached to other proteins) and free (unattached to proteins). Free PSA is the portion that floats freely in the bloodstream without being attached to other proteins, and measuring it helps clinicians understand whether a high total PSA level is more likely due to cancer or a harmless condition like an enlarged prostate.

Unlike total PSA, free PSA does not have a standalone reference range.  **That’s because its value isn’t typically interpreted on its own. Instead, it's used in combination with total PSA to calculate the percent-free PSA using this formula:**

***Percent-free PSA = (Free PSA ÷ Total PSA) × 100***

This percentage acts like a "risk filter" to help differentiate between benign prostate conditions and a higher risk of prostate cancer. Rather than subject everyone with an elevated total PSA to an invasive biopsy, clinicians can measure free PSA in those with a total PSA between 4-10 ng/mL to calculate the percent-free PSA. The associated risk is stratified as follows:

- **Lower probability of cancer:**
  - Percent-free PSA > 25%
  - Likely benign condition (e.g., enlarged prostate, prostatitis, or advancing age)
  - Biopsy unnecessary
- **Intermediate probability of cancer:**
  - Percent-free PSA 10 - 25 %
  - MRI and urology referral recommended to discuss the need for biopsy
- **Higher probability of cancer:**
  - Percent-free PSA < 10%
  - Risk of prostate cancer is 50%
  - MRI and urology referral recommended to discuss the need for biopsy

Clinicians combine this PSA information with a review of your age, medical history, family history, symptoms, physical exam (like a digital rectal exam), and MRI findings to decide if a biopsy is needed.

An MRI of the prostate can sometimes be the next step for abnormal PSA levels. Based on your level, you may be able to get a Function scan that will also look at your prostate. This scan can help detect potentially serious prostate cancers that may be missed by PSA tests alone, and help determine whether follow up testing, including an invasive biopsy, is truly necessary. A clinician assigns the MRI findings with an E-SCORE of 1-5, calibrated to the clinical urgency and the level of follow-up care that may be required. If the MRI reveals suspicious areas, a diagnostic prostate MRI with and without IV contrast should be performed to determine whether a targeted biopsy is needed.

Please reference your total PSA and percent-free PSA biomarkers together when interpreting your results, and consult with your healthcare provider to understand how these values relate to your personal risk profile and whether follow-up is needed.

## Summary

Your healthy prostate levels go hand in hand with healthy body weight, regular exercise, a healthy microbiome in the gut, and low stress. Keep up healthy habits to maintain your healthy levels.
