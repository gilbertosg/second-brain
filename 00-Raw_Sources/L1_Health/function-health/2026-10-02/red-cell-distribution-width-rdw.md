## Why it matters

Red cell distribution width (RDW) is a measure of how much variation exists in the size of red blood cells. Ideally, red blood cells (RBC) should be fairly uniform because they need to travel smoothly through blood vessels and carry oxygen efficiently. When RDW is higher than normal, it means there’s a wider range of cell sizes—some are too big, some too small—which can signal underlying imbalances in the body. This marker is part of a standard complete blood count (CBC) test and is often used to help diagnose or monitor conditions related to blood health and overall metabolic function.

RDW is most useful when evaluated alongside other blood markers like mean corpuscular volume (MCV), hemoglobin, and ferritin. A high RDW with a low MCV often suggests iron deficiency anemia, while a high RDW with a high MCV may point to vitamin B12 or folate deficiencies. RDW can also help differentiate between different types of anemia, chronic inflammation, and even cardiovascular or metabolic conditions. Since RBC production is influenced by bone marrow health, nutrient levels, and inflammation, RDW can serve as an early warning sign for broader imbalances in the body.

- **Elevated RDW** : This can indicate various conditions, including iron, vitamin B12, or folate deficiencies, liver disease, kidney dysfunction, or chronic inflammation. A persistently high RDW has also been linked to a greater risk of cardiovascular disease, as it may reflect underlying oxidative stress and inflammation affecting red blood cell production.
- **Low RDW** : While less commonly discussed, a very low RDW generally isn’t a concern on its own. It often just means that red blood cells are uniformly sized, which is typically a sign of stability in blood cell production. Low RDW values have been associated with overall decreased mortality.

Understanding RDW alongside other blood markers can help detect nutrient deficiencies, chronic disease risks, and inflammatory states before they progress into more serious health problems.

**Summary**

RDW reflects variation in red blood cell size (anisocytosis) and can indicate underlying health conditions. A high RDW is commonly associated with iron, B12, or folate deficiencies, inflammation, oxidative stress, and some chronic diseases. A low RDW is less clinically significant, often reflecting stable red blood cell (RBC) production, and has been linked to lower overall mortality.

**Optimal ranges**

The optimal range for RDW generally falls between 11.7%-15.4%. When RDW is within this range, there is a lower risk of adverse outcomes, including all-cause mortality, compared to those with RDW in higher percentiles.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

RDW is a measure of how red blood cells vary in size. While it’s often used as part of a routine complete blood count (CBC), it’s not a fixed number; it can shift over time based on what the body is going through. For example, if a person is recovering from an illness, dealing with inflammation, deficient in certain nutrients like iron, B12, or folate, or if they have a bone marrow disorder, their RDW can temporarily rise or fall. Even hydration levels, recent infections, or starting a new supplement can cause fluctuations. That means a slightly high or low RDW on one day may look different on another, and it's more meaningful when interpreted alongside other biomarkers.

## Summary

A normal or in-range RDW result indicates that red blood cells are relatively uniform in size, which suggests stable bone marrow function and balanced iron, vitamin B12, and folate (B9) levels. This is generally a sign of healthy red blood cell production and turnover.

To maintain a healthy RDW, a root cause approach focuses on optimizing nutrient intake, reducing inflammation, and supporting red blood cell production. Ensuring sufficient levels of iron, vitamin B12, folate (B9), and copper through diet or supplementation can help sustain proper red blood cell formation. Whole, nutrient-dense foods such as leafy greens, grass-fed meats, and wild-caught fish provide bioavailable nutrients essential for red blood cell health.

If fatigue, shortness of breath, or unexplained weakness is present despite a normal RDW, additional testing may be needed to evaluate other aspects of red blood cell health.
