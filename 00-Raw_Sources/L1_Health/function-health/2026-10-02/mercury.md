## Why it matters

Mercury is a heavy metal found in the environment, and in certain forms, like methylmercury, it can build up in the body and become toxic. People are most commonly exposed through seafood (especially large fish like tuna and swordfish), dental amalgam fillings, and industrial pollution. If the body can’t remove mercury fast enough and is not addressed, it can cause symptoms like fatigue, memory loss, tremors, or numbness. Measuring mercury levels helps determine if these exposures are contributing to health problems.

Mercury comes in three main forms, each affecting the body differently:

- **Elemental mercury (liquid mercury or quicksilver):** Found in old thermometers, some electrical switches, fluorescent bulbs, and dental fillings. Dangerous if its vapor is inhaled.
- **Inorganic mercury** : Found in some batteries, older disinfectants, and chemistry labs. It can harm the kidneys and digestive system if swallowed or absorbed through the skin over time.
- **Organic mercury (especially methylmercury)** : Found in fish, coal-burning emissions, and some old antiseptics. Most likely to affect the brain and nervous system.

Mercury is often measured alongside other markers that show how well the body handles toxins. For example, creatinine is checked to ensure urine mercury testing is accurate, and levels of glutathione, selenium, and zinc can reflect how well detox systems are working.

If mercury is elevated, it might suggest a need for medically-supervised chelation therapy (a process that helps bind and remove heavy metals), support for detox pathways through specific nutrients, or lifestyle changes like avoiding high-mercury fish. Tracking mercury and related markers over time helps identify whether exposure is ongoing or something from the past, and allows for tailored strategies to reduce the toxic load on the body.

- **Elevated mercury** may point to recent or long-term exposure, and if not addressed, may potentially lead to:
- Cognitive symptoms like memory loss, difficulty concentrating, or mood shifts.
- Neurological effects such as numbness, tremors, or coordination issues.
- Kidney stress, reducing the body’s ability to clear other toxins.
- Oxidative stress, which can damage cells and speed up aging or chronic disease.

Mercury is a toxic metal that can build up in the body from sources like fish, fillings, and pollution, potentially harming the brain, kidneys, and nerves. A root cause approach looks at where the mercury is coming from and how well the body can eliminate it so deeper healing can happen.

**Optimal ranges**

The optimal level for mercury in the body is zero, as it serves no biological purpose.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Mercury levels can fluctuate because blood primarily reflects recent exposure rather than total body burden. Levels may spike after eating high-mercury fish or decline as mercury moves into deeper tissues like the brain or kidneys. Different forms of mercury clear at different rates: methylmercury (from fish) clears over weeks to months, while elemental mercury (from fillings or industrial exposure) lingers longer. Detox capacity also varies based on genetics, nutrient status, and liver function. Because of these shifts, a single blood test may not fully capture total mercury load, and results are best interpreted alongside symptoms and other tests.

## Summary

While mercury levels may appear within the laboratory reference range, this does not necessarily mean they are ideal for health. Mercury has no known beneficial role in the body, and minimizing exposure is generally recommended. Supporting the body’s natural detoxification processes through diet and lifestyle choices can help keep mercury levels as low as possible. Here are the mercury level categories based on severity:

- **Slightly elevated:** 1-5
- **Moderately elevated:** 5-10
- **High:** greater than 10

Because optimal ranges for mercury vary so widely from the lab’s reference range, it’s important to consider potential sources of exposure, possible health impacts, and next steps—even when results appear “in range.”

**Root cause approach**

A root cause approach to addressing elevated mercury involves identifying and managing factors contributing to mercury accumulation, such as dietary sources, nutrient deficiencies, toxic exposures, and lifestyle influences. This approach focuses on evaluating underlying issues and implementing strategies that may support the body’s ability to eliminate mercury safely while minimizing ongoing exposure. Greater detail on food, lifestyle, and other considerations is provided in the sections that follow, but a general overview includes:

- **Identify and reduce exposure sources** : Reviewing diet, environment, and dental history helps pinpoint sources like high-mercury fish (e.g., tuna, swordfish), occupational exposures, or silver-colored amalgam fillings.
- **Support detox pathways with nutrition** : Sulfur-rich foods like garlic, onions, and broccoli may enhance glutathione production, which helps bind and remove mercury through the liver and kidneys.
- **Replenish key nutrients** : Ensuring adequate levels of selenium, zinc, and vitamin C can support antioxidant defenses and protect against mercury-induced cellular damage.
- **Consider gentle botanicals and binders** : Cilantro and chlorella are sometimes used to support detox, and binders like modified citrus pectin or activated charcoal may assist in capturing mercury in the gut. Algae like chlorella act as natural chelators that help reduce heavy metal levels when used over time.
- **Promote elimination through lifestyle practices** : Supporting hydration, regular bowel movements, sweating (through saunas or exercise), and restful sleep may help the body clear mercury more effectively.
- **Monitor progress with follow-up testing** : Periodic blood or provoked urine testing can track changes in mercury levels and help assess the effectiveness of interventions over time.
- **Address genetic or metabolic factors** : Some individuals may have variations in detox genes (like GST or MTHFR) that slow mercury clearance, and identifying these may guide more personalized treatment strategies.
- **Evaluate pharmaceutical chelation options when needed** : Agents like DMSA or DMPS may be used ***under medical supervision*** to help remove mercury from the body, especially in moderate to severe toxicity.

## Causes

**Root causes of elevated mercury**

Elevated mercury levels are most often caused by repeated or high exposure to mercury from the environment, combined with factors that make it harder for the body to get rid of it. Common sources include eating fish high in mercury, having silver dental fillings, working in jobs like mining or dentistry, and having genetic or nutritional problems that slow down detoxification.

**Diet and lifestyle factors**

Diet and lifestyle factors that contribute to elevated mercury levels often involve both increased exposure and reduced detoxification capacity. A root cause approach looks at how these habits may be introducing mercury into the body or making it harder to clear, allowing it to build up over time.

- **Frequent consumption of high-mercury fish** : Eating fish like tuna, swordfish, king mackerel, and shark regularly can raise mercury levels.
- **Low intake of detox-supportive nutrients** : Deficiencies in selenium, zinc, sulfur-containing foods, and antioxidants can impair the body’s ability to eliminate mercury.
- **Not enough physical activity:** Lack of regular physical activity reduces circulation, lymphatic flow, and sweating, which are all essential for removing mercury and other toxins from the body.
- **Stress and poor sleep** : Chronic stress and inadequate sleep can weaken liver, kidney, and brain detox pathways, potentially slowing mercury clearance.

**Other factors**

Other underlying factors can contribute to elevated mercury levels by reducing the body’s ability to detoxify and increasing how mercury is absorbed or retained.

- **Poor gut health** : Imbalances in gut bacteria or a damaged intestinal lining may reduce mercury excretion and increase how much is absorbed into the bloodstream.
- **Chronic inflammation** : Inflammation can impair liver and kidney function, which are both essential for mercury detox.
- **High oxidative stress** : When the body has too many free radicals and not enough antioxidants, it becomes more vulnerable to mercury damage and less efficient at clearing it.
- **Insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome** : Conditions like prediabetes can reduce mitochondrial function and slow detox pathways, increasing the risk of mercury buildup.
- **Genetic variations in detox genes** : Differences in genes like GST, MTHFR, or APOE may reduce the body's ability to process and eliminate mercury.
- **Low glutathione levels** : Glutathione is a major antioxidant that binds to mercury and helps remove it; low levels may increase toxicity risk.

**Medications**

Some medications and medical products can raise mercury levels if they contain mercury as an ingredient or preservative. While mercury use in medicine has decreased, certain traditional remedies, vaccines, and antiseptics can still contribute to higher levels. Examples include:

- **Mercury-containing traditional medicines:** Some traditional Chinese, Tibetan, and Ayurvedic medicines incorporate mercury compounds like cinnabar (mercury sulfide) and calomel (mercurous chloride) that may increase mercury levels.
- **Thimerosal-containing vaccines or medications** : Thimerosal is a mercury-based preservative that was used in some vaccines, eye drops, and nasal sprays, though it has been largely phased out in most products.
- **Topical antiseptics with mercurochrome** : Older antiseptic products may still contain mercury compounds and contribute to skin absorption if used frequently.

**Environmental toxin exposure**

- **Occupational or environmental exposure** : Working in industries like mining, metal refining, dentistry, or battery manufacturing increases mercury contact.
- **Use of mercury-containing dental amalgams** : Silver-colored fillings may slowly release mercury vapor, especially when chewing or grinding teeth.
- **Use of skin-lightening creams or cosmetics containing mercury:** Some imported skin products illegally contain mercury, leading to high blood and urinary mercury levels.
- **Living near or working in artisanal gold mining areas:** Mercury is used to extract gold, releasing vapor into the air and contaminating local water/soil. Nearby residents inhale vapor or ingest contaminated water/food.

For more information, visit [Environmental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/environmental-health-101).

**Substance use**

- **Tobacco** : Tobacco plants can accumulate mercury from the soil, and smoking tobacco may lead to mercury exposure. The extent of mercury absorption from smoking and its impact on blood mercury levels require further investigation.

## Associated conditions

**General conditions and diseases**

Having mercury levels above the reference range has been linked to several health conditions that affect the nervous system, kidneys, immune response, hormonal balance, and reproductive function. This is attributed to mercury’s ability to damage cell structures, disrupt enzyme activity, increase oxidative stress (which harms cells), and interfere with the absorption or function of essential nutrients such as selenium and zinc.

*Not everyone exposed to mercury will develop these issues, but long-term or high-level exposure raises the risk, especially in people with weaker detoxification systems or nutrient deficiencies.*

- **Kidney disease** : Mercury can damage kidney tubules and impair filtration.
- **Autoimmune conditions** : Including Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, lupus, and rheumatoid arthritis.
- **Cardiovascular problems** : Such as high blood pressure and increased risk of heart disease.
- **Thyroid dysfunction** : Mercury may interfere with thyroid hormone production and conversion.
- **Infertility or reproductive issues** : Linked to hormonal disruption and poor egg or sperm quality.
- **Neurodegenerative diseases** : Such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and multiple sclerosis.

These associations do not confirm mercury as the sole cause, but they highlight how excess mercury may contribute to or worsen these health issues in susceptible individuals.

**Mental health conditions**

Mercury affects the brain and nervous system, altering how brain cells communicate and disrupting hormones that regulate mood and behavior. This can lead to mental health symptoms, especially with long-term exposure.

- **Anxiety** : Mercury disrupts neurotransmitters like GABA and glutamate, which are key players in anxiety regulation. People exposed to mercury often report nervousness, irritability, and restlessness.
- **Depression** : Mercury may disrupt serotonin and dopamine pathways, which are both involved in mood regulation, with increased depressive symptoms in some individuals with elevated mercury levels.
- “**Mad hatter’s disease”** : A neuropsychiatric syndrome historically seen in 19th-century hat makers exposed to mercury vapor during felt production. Symptoms include irritability, excessive shyness, low self-confidence, anxiety, mood swings, memory problems, and, in some cases, hallucinations or delusions. While rare today, similar symptoms have been documented in individuals with chronic mercury exposure.

## Foods to limit

If levels are very high and not addressed, this can lead to chronic toxicity and kidney damage, and heavy metals like mercury are often linked to lifestyle and dietary factors that contribute to their accumulation in the body. These factors can increase oxidative stress, inflammation, and the risk of developing kidney dysfunction and other health issues. Consuming foods grown in soil contaminated with heavy metals or diets low in essential nutrients like zinc and iron can enhance heavy metal absorption and accumulation. Avoiding foods that are high in mercury and those that contribute to metabolic dysfunction can help reduce the risk of toxicity.

- **Large predatory fish** : Fish like shark, swordfish, king mackerel, and tilefish are known to have high mercury levels due to bioaccumulation in the food chain. Limit consumption of these fish to reduce mercury exposure. Opting for lower-mercury alternatives like salmon, sardines, or scallops can reduce exposure.
- **Shellfish** : Certain shellfish can also accumulate mercury depending on the environmental conditions where they are harvested.
- **High fructose corn syrup in processed foods and beverages** : May contain trace amounts of mercury from certain manufacturing methods, and consuming it can also lead to zinc loss, which is important for detox and brain health.
- **Gut-harming foods** : Refined sugars, alcohol, and processed oils disrupt gut bacteria needed to bind and eliminate mercury
- **Foods with added processed sugars** : Processed sugar may increase inflammation, disrupt the gut, weaken immune function, and reduce the body's ability to clear toxins.
- **Non-organic produce with heavy pesticide use** : Pesticide residues may compound the toxic burden, making it harder for the body to detox mercury.
- **Alcohol** : Alcohol can impair liver function, reducing the body’s ability to detoxify effectively.
- **Contaminated water** : Water filtration systems specifically designed to remove heavy metals like mercury can help reduce exposure.

## Symptoms

If your mercury levels are above the optimal range, it can contribute to a wide range of symptoms that affect the brain, nervous system, and overall energy levels. Some of these symptoms are often overlooked and associated with other conditions.

- **Brain fog** : Difficulty concentrating, forgetfulness, and slowed thinking are common.
- **Diarrhea** : Often seen in acute ingestion or exposure.
- **Fatigue** : Persistent tiredness that doesn’t improve with rest.
- **Fever** : May occur in cases of acute toxicity or systemic response.
- **Headaches** : Ongoing or frequent headaches may be related to neurological effects.
- **Metallic taste** : A distinct and sometimes persistent taste.
- **Muscle weakness** : A sense of reduced strength or stamina.
- **Nausea and vomiting** : Common with both acute and chronic exposure.
- **Numbness or tingling** : Especially in the hands, feet, or around the mouth.
- **Excessive salivation** : Increased salivation can be a sign of mercury exposure, especially with inorganic forms.
- **Visual disturbance** : Blurred or distorted vision, especially with long-term or high-level exposure.
- **Sensitivity to light or sound** : Overreaction to sensory input.
- **Shortness of breath** : Can result from pulmonary damage or nervous system effects.
- **Tremors** : Shakiness or unsteady hands, particularly with fine motor tasks.

## Additional tests

**Root cause approach to diagnosing elevated mercury**

A root cause approach to mercury focuses on identifying sources of exposure, assessing the body’s ability to detoxify, and understanding individual factors, such as genetics, nutrition, and organ function, that may influence mercury accumulation.

**Blood testing**

- **Red blood cell counts and indices** : Red blood cell count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, MCV, and other blood indices check for anemia or immune changes, which can be affected by mercury toxicity that can disrupt bone marrow production or damage mature red blood cells.
- **Kidney function (creatinine and blood urea nitrogen/BUN)** : Evaluates kidney function, which plays a major role in mercury elimination.
- **Liver function (ALT, AST)** : Looks at liver health, since the liver helps process and detoxify mercury.

**Urine testing**

Urine tests can be helpful in certain cases to evaluate mercury levels, especially when looking at how the body is eliminating it. Without provocation (chelation), standard urine mercury tests are not reliable for detecting stored mercury and may only show recent, low-level exposures.

- **Urine mercury (post-chelation)** : Measures how much mercury is excreted after taking a chelating agent like DMSA, which pulls mercury out of storage and into the urine, helping detect hidden body burden.
- **24-hour urine mercury** : Collects all urine over 24 hours to estimate how much mercury the body is clearing in a day, offering a broader picture than a single sample.
- **Urine creatinine** : Used alongside mercury testing to make sure the sample is accurate and reflects proper kidney function.
- **Urine porphyrins profile** : Indirectly flags mercury toxicity by showing abnormal waste products from red blood cells, hinting at mercury’s interference with cellular processes

**Diagnostic imaging (CT, MRI, ultrasound, etc.)**

Imaging is not routinely used to diagnose mercury toxicity, but may be helpful in some cases to evaluate damage to organs.

- **Electrocardiography (ECG)** : Records the electrical activity of the heart and may help identify rhythm changes or abnormalities potentially caused by mercury-induced oxidative stress.
- **Electroneuromyography (EMG/ENMG)** : Measures the electrical signals in muscles and nerves to assess for mercury-related nerve damage, weakness, or numbness.
- **Kidney-ureter-bladder X-ray (KUB)** : May be used to track the movement of accidentally swallowed liquid mercury (e.g., from a broken thermometer) through the gastrointestinal tract when there is no vomiting or aspiration, helping monitor for retention or obstruction while guiding symptom management.

**Nutrient imbalances**

- **Zinc** : Zinc supports the enzymes involved in detox and helps protect the brain and immune system from mercury damage. Low zinc weakens the body’s ability to detox mercury since zinc helps bind and remove it from cells.
- **Selenium** : Selenium binds to mercury and may help reduce its harmful effects, so testing can show if there's enough to support detox.
- **Glutathione (reduced and total)** : This “master antioxidant” neutralizes mercury’s harmful effects and supports liver detox pathways.
- **Homocysteine, B12, B6, and folate (B9)** : These B vitamins help with methylation, which is a key detox pathway for mercury and other toxins.
- **Methylmalonic acid (MMA)** : High MMA suggests vitamin B12 depletion, which mercury exacerbates, requiring vitamin B12 support to protect brain/nerve health
- **Copper:** Often coexists with mercury in environmental exposures (e.g., mining, industrial pollution) and can interact synergistically to worsen toxicity, particularly in neurological and metabolic pathways.
- **Magnesium** : Magnesium is involved in hundreds of detox and repair processes; low levels can worsen mercury toxicity.
- **Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA/DHA)** : Low omega-3s can increase inflammation and worsen mercury’s effects on the brain and nerves.
- **Vitamin C** : This antioxidant helps protect cells from mercury-related damage and supports detox.
- **Amino acids:** Mercury’s binding to sulfur-rich amino acids (cysteine, methionine) and glutathione impairs detox, energy production, and neurological function. Monitoring these helps assess detox capacity and guide nutrient support (e.g., NAC, selenium).

**Microbiome and gut health assessment**

- **Comprehensive stool analysis** : Assesses the balance of gut microbes and inflammation markers, since mercury disrupts "good" bacteria like *Lactobacillus* that help neutralize toxins.
- **Gut permeability (zonulin/calprotectin):** Measures "leaky gut" severity, as mercury damages the gut lining, letting toxins enter the bloodstream.

See [Gut Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/gut-health-101) for more information.

**Immune system evaluation**

- **High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP):** Checks inflammation levels, as mercury triggers immune cells to release molecules that damage tissues over time.
- **Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)** : Another basic inflammation test that shows how active the immune system is.
- **Antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and other autoantibodies** : Mercury can trigger autoimmune reactions, where the immune system mistakenly attacks the body.
- **Food sensitivity and allergy testing:** Mercury can increase gut permeability, which may lead to immune reactions to certain foods.
- **Histamine or tryptase levels** : These can be high if mercury is triggering allergic-type responses or mast cell activation.
- **White blood cell counts and lymphocyte subset analysis:** Checks white blood cell balance, as mercury can suppress infection-fighting cells while over-activating autoimmune responses
- **Thyroid antibodies (like TPO and TgAb)** : Mercury may be a trigger for autoimmune thyroid issues like Hashimoto’s disease.
- **Total IgE or allergen panel** : Can show allergic responses that may become worse in the presence of heavy metals like mercury.

**Environmental and toxin testing**

Mercury is often found along with other toxic exposures. These tests uncover "toxin teams" (mold, metals, pesticides, and plastics) that can overwhelm the body’s defenses, guiding targeted detoxification and management strategies.

- **Heavy metals (often after a chelator like DMSA)** : Measures how much mercury and other metals (like lead or arsenic) are stored in the body.
- **Glyphosate and pesticide panel** : Checks for weed killers and farming chemicals that can overload detox pathways and make mercury harder to clear.
- **Mycotoxins/mold** : Detects toxins from mold, which can add to the body’s toxin load and overwhelm the detox system like mercury does.
- **Plastics and phthalates** : Measures exposure to chemicals from plastics that can interfere with hormones and detox pathways.
- **Toxic non-metal chemicals** : Includes solvents, gasoline additives, and other industrial toxins that often show up with mercury in people with higher exposures.

For more information, visit [Environmental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/environmental-health-101).

**Advanced diagnostics**

- **ApoE genetic testing** : Shows how well the brain can repair and detox; some versions (like ApoE4) may make mercury more harmful to the brain.
- **Detoxification gene panel (like GST, MTHFR, COMT, Nrf2)** : Looks at genes that affect how well the liver and cells can detoxify mercury and other toxins.
- **Pulmonary function test (PFT)** : Evaluates lung capacity and breathing function, which may be affected if mercury exposure has occurred through inhalation, especially in occupational settings.
- **Electroneuromyography (ENMG):** Checks nerve/muscle function if mercury causes numbness, tingling, or weakness.
- **Oxidative stress markers:** Evaluates oxidative stress through tests like urinary 8-isoprostane or plasma malondialdehyde (MDA). These tests help gauge the level of oxidative damage in the body, such as mercury’s "rusting" effect on cells, showing if antioxidants like glutathione are depleted.
- **Organic acids testing** : Organic acid tests can detect imbalances in microbial byproducts, mitochondrial dysfunction, and nutritional deficiencies that indicate dysbiosis and systemic oxidative stress.

**Cognitive assessments**

High mercury can impact cognitive function, particularly in areas like memory, processing speed, and executive function. Mercury exposure disrupts brain cell communication, reduces antioxidant defenses, and may shrink brain regions like the hippocampus (critical for memory). This can lead to symptoms like brain fog, memory issues, trouble concentrating, and mood changes. Cognitive testing helps by providing a clear, measurable picture of how the brain is working—highlighting specific areas like attention, processing speed, or memory that may be affected. This allows for targeted interventions and helps track progress as detox and healing strategies are put in place.

For more information about maintaining optimum brain health, including cognition, memory, and ways to reduce the risk of age-related neurodegenerative disorders, visit [Brain Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/brain-health-101).

## Follow up

**Medical interventions**

Collaboration with your healthcare provider ensures a personalized approach to managing mercury levels if they are elevated. Consideration of all symptoms, laboratory test results, and lifestyle factors can guide a treatment plan tailored to your specific needs and health goals.

**Medications**

Prescription medications for conditions associated with elevated mercury are typically aimed at binding and eliminating mercury, managing symptoms, or addressing complications (like autoimmunity or neurological symptoms). The use of these medications depends on the severity of mercury exposure and individual health status.

**Chelation therapy** : Chelation therapy, including the use of DMSA (dimercaptosuccinic acid), is one of the primary methods for removing mercury from the body. Chelation therapy may be used ***under medical supervision*** to remove metals in cases of significantly elevated levels, although it requires careful monitoring for potential side effects. Chelation therapy is often combined with other approaches like antioxidant support, binders, and dietary interventions to promote the detoxification of heavy metals and mitigate their harmful effects.

- **DMSA (dimercaptosuccinic acid)** is a chelating agent that binds to mercury in the bloodstream and other tissues and facilitates its excretion through urine.
  - **Administration:** It can be taken orally in capsule or tablet form.
  - **Mechanism** : DMSA binds to metals through sulfhydryl (-SH) groups, forming a stable complex that is excreted through urine. It primarily targets blood and soft tissues.
  - **Dosage** : Typically, 10-30 mg/kg/day, taken in cycles of several weeks with breaks in between. Chelation cycles often last for several weeks to months, depending on the individual’s level of exposure and toxicity.
  - **Cautions** : Potential mild gastrointestinal issues are the most common.
- **Dimercaprol (BAL):** Used less commonly now, but still considered for severe acute mercury poisoning. Given at 2.5-3 mg/kg intramuscularly every 4-6 hours for 2-7 days, depending on severity, often in combination with other chelators under medical supervision.
- **2,3-Dimercapto-1-propane sulfonic acid (DMPS):** Used in Europe and integrative settings to support mercury excretion, especially through urine. Administered orally or by IV, 3-5 mg/kg every 6-8 hours (oral) or 3-5 mg/kg IV over 2 hours daily for 5-10 days, adjusted based on mercury levels and kidney function.

These medications should always be prescribed and monitored by a licensed clinician familiar with mercury-related toxicity, especially since chelation therapy can mobilize mercury and must be done carefully to avoid redistributing it into sensitive tissues like the brain.

**Procedures**

Medical procedures for conditions associated with elevated mercury are generally supportive or corrective, depending on the severity of exposure, target organ damage, and underlying conditions triggered or worsened by mercury.

- **Dental amalgam removal:** Safely removes mercury-containing silver fillings, which may be a chronic source of exposure. This should be done by a biological or SMART-certified dentist using protective protocols to minimize mercury vapor release.
- **Dialysis** : May be used in cases of kidney failure caused by inorganic mercury toxicity.

**Retesting**

Retesting for elevated mercury is advisable to track progress, adjust the detox plan, and ensure that mercury levels are moving in the right direction without causing additional stress on the body. The timing depends on the type of mercury exposure, the intensity of the detox protocol, and individual response to treatment.

General guidelines for retesting

- **Every 3-6 months** : This is a common interval for re-checking mercury levels, especially after initiating a targeted detox plan that includes binders, nutrients, or chelation therapy.
- **After major changes in treatment** : If chelation agents like DMSA or DMPS are introduced, or if significant diet or lifestyle changes are made, it may be helpful to test again within 6 to 8 weeks to assess initial response and safety.
- **Following resolution of symptoms** : If symptoms linked to mercury improve significantly, retesting can help confirm whether levels have normalized and whether maintenance is needed.
- **If mercury-containing dental fillings were removed** : It’s best to wait 2-3 months post-removal before testing, as short-term levels may spike due to mobilization.
- **After a provoked urine test** : Retesting after a chelation challenge should be spaced at least 3 months apart to allow time for tissue clearance and prevent redistribution.
