## Why it matters

Docosapentaenoic acid (DPA) is an omega-3 fatty acid that plays a vital role in reducing inflammation, supporting brain health, and maintaining heart function. DPA serves as a "bridge" in the body’s conversion of another omega-3, eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), into docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), which is essential for brain and cellular health. While the body can make small amounts of DPA from plant-based omega-3s like alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), this process is not very efficient, especially during times of stress, illness, or high inflammation. DPA is found in fish oils and marine sources, making these dietary options important for replenishing this key nutrient.

DPA works closely with EPA and DHA to regulate inflammation and protect against chronic diseases. These three omega-3s counteract the inflammatory effects of omega-6 fatty acids, which are common in processed foods. Testing DPA alongside EPA, DHA, and the omega-6 to omega-3 ratio provides a detailed picture of inflammatory balance in the body. For example, low DPA or a high omega-6 to omega-3 ratio can indicate chronic inflammation, which raises the risk for conditions like heart disease, arthritis, and cognitive decline.

Abnormal DPA levels can offer important health clues.

- **Low DPA levels** : May signal insufficient intake of omega-3s or heightened inflammatory demands. This could worsen conditions like cardiovascular disease, arthritis, or neurodegenerative disorders.
- **High DPA levels** : Often reflect a diet rich in omega-3s or supplementation. Elevated levels are typically not a concern and suggest a strong anti-inflammatory profile.

Measuring DPA helps identify whether additional dietary support is needed to address inflammation, support brain and heart health, and promote resilience against chronic diseases. Adjusting omega-3 intake can help maintain optimal levels and support vital physiological functions. These insights can guide tailored strategies, such as dietary changes or supplements, to restore balance and manage inflammation effectively.

## Summary

An in-range level of DPA indicates the body has a steady supply of this essential omega-3 fat, which helps bridge the benefits of EPA and DHA. It reflects strong anti-inflammatory activity and supports cardiovascular function, joint health, and brain resilience. DPA also acts as a reserve, converting into EPA or DHA as needed, while directly supporting vascular flexibility and tissue repair. When DPA is in range, it quietly contributes to reduced risk of heart disease, cognitive decline, and inflammatory conditions like arthritis, even if there are no obvious symptoms.

To keep DPA within range, a root cause strategy emphasizes eating fatty fish such as salmon or mackerel, or supplementing with high-quality fish oil that contains DPA along with EPA and DHA. Antioxidants like vitamin E or alpha-lipoic acid help protect DPA from oxidation and preserve its activity in cell membranes. Botanicals like curcumin may further enhance DPA’s anti-inflammatory benefits. Maintaining a healthy gut microbiome through probiotics and prebiotic-rich foods also supports nutrient absorption. Ongoing omega-3 testing allows for personalized adjustments to keep DPA in range, helping protect brain and visual health over time.

## Foods to eat

Oily fish (salmon, mackerel, herring) and grass-fed beef can serve as the primary DPA sources.

## Foods to limit

To support omega balance, consider avoiding the following due to their content of refined, hydrogenated fats and oils, solid fats (like butter), and hydrogenated oils in plant-based products (such as in alternative milks), which are often used for texture. Always check food labels as these ingredients appear in many processed foods:

- Canola oil
- Fried foods
- Margarine
- Safflower oil
- Shortening
- Soybean oil
- Sunflower oil
