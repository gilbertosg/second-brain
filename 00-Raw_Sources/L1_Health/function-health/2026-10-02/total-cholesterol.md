## Why it matters

Total cholesterol is a measure of the total amount of cholesterol circulating in the blood. This includes high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, which helps clear excess cholesterol from the body, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and other cholesterol-containing particles like VLDL (very low-density lipoprotein) and IDL (intermediate-density lipoprotein) involved in transporting triglycerides, that have the potential to deposit into artery walls. Cholesterol is essential—it helps make hormones, build cell membranes, and support brain function. But like many things in the body, balance is key. While lower total cholesterol levels are generally beneficial, cholesterol is more complex than just "good" or "bad." Measuring total cholesterol gives a general sense of how much cholesterol is circulating, which can serve as a starting point for understanding heart and metabolic health.

Total cholesterol is most meaningful when interpreted alongside other lipid and metabolic markers including a lipoprotein fractionation which is used to analyze the different sizes and quality of lipoprotein particles in the blood, triglycerides, HDL, apolipoprotein B (ApoB) (a count of the actual number of atherogenic or plaque-forming particles), and lipoprotein(a). Together, these markers can help detect the risk of heart disease and guide clinical decisions around diet, lifestyle, and medical interventions, if needed.

For example:

- Someone who maintains a healthy lifestyle and optimal body weight may have a total cholesterol of 300 mg/dL, with an HDL of 100 mg/dL, triglycerides at 70 mg/dL, and LDL at 120 mg/dL. However, they mostly have large, fluffy, protective cholesterol particles. Despite the high total cholesterol, the particle size and pattern suggest a very low (or even no) risk of heart disease, and medical intervention may not be necessary.
- On the other hand, someone who is overweight can have a total cholesterol of just 150 mg/dL, with an LDL of 80 mg/dL, HDL of 30 mg/dL, and triglycerides at 200 mg/dL, but carry mostly small, dense LDL particles and dysfunctional HDL particles. Even with a total cholesterol in the “normal” range, this individual may be at a higher risk for heart disease and could benefit from more aggressive lifestyle and medical interventions.

This shows why context matters and that cholesterol numbers alone do not tell the full story. A deeper look at the size and quality of the cholesterol particles is needed, as well as looking for early signs of insulin resistance, an underactive thyroid, liver dysfunction, or underlying inflammation that may be driving the imbalance. These insights can guide more personalized approaches around nutrition, supplements, or medications to support long-term cardiometabolic health.

One of the best indicators of cardiovascular risk is not LDL cholesterol alone; it is the triglyceride-to-HDL ratio, ideally 1:1. A ratio above 2:1 raises risk, with a 5:1 ratio strongly linked to cardiovascular disease, insulin resistance, and prediabetes.

Optimizing HDL cholesterol can be important with an elevated total cholesterol, as large, more buoyant HDL particles are protective and can help counterbalance the associated risks. While smaller, more dense HDL particles are often caused by insulin resistance, prediabetes, and type 2 diabetes, and increase the risk of heart disease. In addition to the size and quality of HDL particles, advanced tests like the HDLfx pCAD score and HDLfx pCEC can together capture one of HDL’s most important protective roles and reveal cardiovascular risk factors that standard HDL cholesterol measurements can miss.

**Summary**

Total cholesterol represents the total amount of cholesterol in the blood, including HDL, LDL, and other cholesterol-containing particles, both protective and atherogenic (plaque-forming) types. From a root cause perspective, elevated total cholesterol can point to deeper issues like poor diet, insulin resistance, inflammation, an underactive thyroid, or liver dysfunction, all underlying causes that need to be addressed to protect overall heart health.

**Optimal ranges**

The optimal range for total cholesterol, in both men and women, is typically between 125 and 199 mg/dL, which supports healthy hormone production, brain function, and cardiovascular health without increasing the risk of plaque buildup in the arteries.

This range isn’t one-size-fits-all. A root cause approach looks at your overall cardiometabolic health by factoring in inflammation, insulin resistance, and family history alongside total cholesterol to find the most protective range for you. It highlights why personalized care matters, tailoring health goals to your unique risk factors and health status for better long-term outcomes.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Total cholesterol levels can change based on what's happening in your body and lifestyle. Factors like what you eat, how much you exercise, stress levels, blood sugar control, and even how much sleep you get can all influence it. For example, eating a meal high in refined carbs or sugars can temporarily raise triglycerides, which are part of total cholesterol. Similarly, if you're under a lot of stress or not sleeping well, your body may have more inflammation, which can also shift your total cholesterol. Because of all these influences, your total cholesterol levels can fluctuate slightly from day to day or week to week. That's why it's important to look at trends over time, not just a single test result, and to understand the bigger picture of your heart and metabolic health.

## Summary

A total cholesterol result within the normal range generally suggests that the body is maintaining a healthy balance of cholesterol-carrying particles. It suggests a balance of protective cholesterol particles like HDL and lower levels of potentially harmful LDL or other lipoproteins. This balance helps support the structural integrity of cells, hormone production, and brain health. While a normal result is encouraging, it does not completely rule out cardiovascular risk, and usually points to a lower likelihood of plaque buildup in the artery walls, leading to artery narrowing and potentially restricted blood flow.

Some people may have small, dense LDL particles or high levels of inflammation that are not reflected with total cholesterol alone, so it is important to get a broader view of cardiometabolic fitness. A comprehensive assessment of advanced lipid biomarkers, alongside inflammation markers like hs-CRP and metabolic markers like fasting glucose and insulin, is beneficial. Nutrition strategies can include eating whole, unprocessed, anti-inflammatory foods rich in healthy fats (like omega-3s from wild-caught fish and alpha-linolenic acid from flax or chia seeds), leafy greens, nuts, and seeds to help maintain healthy lipid particle size by reducing oxidative stress and stabilizing blood sugar. Regular movement, stress management, adequate restorative sleep, and maintaining a healthy weight all help regulate blood sugar and lower inflammation naturally. The goal is not just to keep the numbers in range but to keep metabolism, inflammation, and liver functioning optimally.
