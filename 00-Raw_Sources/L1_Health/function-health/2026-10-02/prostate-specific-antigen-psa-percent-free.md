## Why it matters

Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) is a protein made by the prostate, and it circulates in the blood in two forms: free PSA (unattached to other proteins) and bound PSA (stuck to blood proteins). Percent-free PSA test measures the proportion of prostate-specific antigen in the blood that isn’t attached to carrier proteins, offering a more nuanced view than total PSA alone. A low percent-free PSA (the ratio of "free" to total PSA) often happens when the prostate produces more PSA that’s bound to proteins—a pattern linked to cancer. However, age, inflammation, or an enlarged prostate can also skew this ratio, making it essential to pair this test with physical exams and imaging. By comparing the “free” PSA to the total amount of PSA, it’s possible to get clues about whether an elevated PSA is more likely due to benign conditions or something that needs closer investigation.

Percent-free PSA is calculated using this formula:

***Percent-free PSA = (Free PSA ÷ Total PSA) × 100***

This percentage acts like a "risk filter" to help differentiate between benign prostate conditions and a higher risk of prostate cancer. Rather than subject everyone with an elevated total PSA to an invasive biopsy, clinicians can measure free PSA in those with a total PSA between 4-10 ng/mL to calculate the percent-free PSA. The associated risk is stratified as follows:

- **Lower probability of cancer:**
  - Percent-free PSA > 25%
  - Likely benign condition (e.g., enlarged prostate, prostatitis, or advancing age)
  - Biopsy unnecessary
- **Intermediate probability of cancer:**
  - Percent-free PSA 10-25 %
  - MRI and urology referral recommended to discuss the need for biopsy
- **Higher probability of cancer:**
  - Percent-free PSA < 10%
  - Risk of prostate cancer is 50%
  - MRI and urology referral recommended to discuss the need for biopsy

Other clues concerning prostate health include PSA velocity (how quickly levels change over time), which can indicate whether something is evolving more aggressively. This information combined with a digital rectal exam and, if needed, imaging like a transrectal prostate ultrasound or MRI, guide decisions about whether to watch and wait, repeat testing, or consider a prostate biopsy.

Typically, a lower percent-free PSA indicates a greater risk of prostate cancer, particularly if total PSA is also elevated. An MRI of the prostate can sometimes be the next step for abnormal PSA levels. Based on your level, you may be able to get a Function MRI scan that will also look at your prostate. The Function MRI scan can help detect potentially serious prostate cancers that may be missed by PSA tests alone, and help determine whether follow-up testing, including an invasive biopsy, is truly necessary. If the MRI reveals suspicious areas, a dedicated prostate MRI with and without IV contrast should be performed to determine whether a targeted biopsy is needed.

A personalized approach to prostate health involves not only monitoring PSA levels but also addressing lifestyle factors such as diet, exercise, and stress management, which can influence overall prostate function and potentially impact PSA levels.

Please reference your total PSA and percent-free PSA biomarkers together when interpreting your results, and consult your healthcare provider to understand how these values relate to your personal risk profile and whether follow-up is needed.

**Summary**

Percent-free PSA is the proportion of prostate-specific antigen in the blood that isn’t bound to other proteins. Comparing it with total PSA gives a clearer picture of prostate health. A lower percent-free PSA (below about 10%) often suggests a higher risk of cancer, while a higher value (above 25%) usually points to non-cancerous conditions like prostate enlargement.

**Optimal ranges**

A percent-free PSA greater than 25% generally reflects a lower risk of prostate cancer, particularly in men whose total PSA falls in the 4-10 ng/mL “gray zone.” Higher percent-free PSA values are often linked to benign conditions like prostate enlargement, while lower values (typically below 10%) are associated with an increased likelihood of prostate cancer. Percent-free PSA between 10-25% warrants further evaluation by a clinician, to carefully assess the risk of prostate cancer.

**What makes this biomarker fluctuate**

Percent-free PSA can shift not only because of underlying prostate health but also due to everyday activities, temporary stresses, certain medications (e.g., corticosteroids and 5-alpha reductase inhibitors like finasteride), and recent prostate procedures. As men age, benign prostate enlargement, inflammation from infections or injury, and even minor prostate trauma can raise total PSA. Since percent-free PSA is calculated incorporating that total, the ratio can change. Vigorous exercise like cycling or heavy lifting may squeeze the prostate and cause a brief PSA bump, and ejaculation within 48 hours before a test can similarly elevate total PSA, altering the free-to-bound balance. Avoiding these types of exercise and sexual activity, including masturbation for 48 hours (2 days), before testing PSA can help ensure that percent-free PSA results reflect true prostate status rather than short-lived fluctuations.

## Summary

A percent-free prostate-specific antigen (PSA) in the normal range, typically above 25%, is generally linked with a lower risk of prostate cancer, especially when total PSA falls within the “gray zone” of 4-10 ng/mL. This result suggests that more of the PSA in the blood is unbound or “free,” which is more often seen in non-cancerous conditions like benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). While a higher percent-free PSA is reassuring, it is just one piece of the puzzle and works best when combined with total PSA, medical history, physical exam, additional testing, and other risk factors. This range does not usually lead to symptoms or complications on its own, but it provides an opportunity to focus on maintaining prostate health and staying proactive about monitoring.

A root cause approach to keeping percent-free PSA within a healthy range includes supporting the body’s natural ability to manage inflammation, hormone balance, and cellular health. Nutritionally, this can mean a diet rich in antioxidants from fruits and vegetables, healthy fats like omega-3s from fish and flaxseeds, and adequate zinc and vitamin D, which help protect prostate cells. Regular exercise helps regulate hormone levels and reduce inflammation, while stress reduction through mindfulness or yoga supports immune health. Botanical options such as saw palmetto and green tea extract may assist in maintaining balanced prostate function. Follow-up testing, including total and percent-free PSA every 6-12 months, can help track trends over time and ensure that early shifts are caught before they become concerning. This approach emphasizes that prostate health is best maintained through personalized care, thoughtful monitoring, and a supportive lifestyle.
