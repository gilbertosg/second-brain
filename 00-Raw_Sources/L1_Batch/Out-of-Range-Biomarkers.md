#### Why it matters

High-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol is often called "good" cholesterol. HDL acts like a scavenger and plays a critical role in removing excess cholesterol from the bloodstream and transporting it to the liver, where it's processed and eliminated from the body through bile and eventually stool. This essential process, called "reverse cholesterol transport," helps regulate cholesterol levels and lowers the risk of cardiovascular diseases. This can protect arteries from cholesterol build-up, which can typically lead to atherosclerosis (hardening and narrowing of the arteries) and increase the risk of heart attacks and strokes.  
While higher HDL is generally beneficial, cholesterol is more complex than just "good" or "bad." The size and quality of cholesterol particles provide more insight than standard tests, which only measure the number of HDL or LDL (low-density lipoprotein) by weight (measured in milligrams per deciliter or mg/dL of blood) and don't reveal the health of the particles themselves. Cholesterol particles can be small and dense, which increases risk, or large and fluffy, which are more protective. For example, someone with an HDL of 60 mg/dL made up mostly of large, buoyant, and protective HDL particles is likely at low risk for heart disease. But another person with an HDL of 60 mg/dL and no large HDL particles could be at much higher risk of a heart attack. The goal is to have more light, fluffy, and functional particles.  
HDL large measures the amount of large HDL particles circulating in the blood. These larger particles are the “good guys” that are better at removing excess cholesterol from the arteries, preventing plaque buildup, and reducing the risk of heart disease. Think of them like big, powerful recycling trucks that keep the arteries clean. Unlike just measuring the amount of HDL cholesterol, HDL large measures how many of those particles are actually protective. Risk decreases when the number and size of large HDL particles increase. Low levels of large HDL particles are often caused by insulin resistance, prediabetes, and type 2 diabetes.  
Comprehensive look at lipids  
It is more meaningful to consider HDL large alongside other lipid biomarkers included in your membership, as the most significant indicators of cardiovascular disease risk—aside from lipoprotein fractionation (which evaluates particle size and quality- LDL small, peak size, particle number, etc.) —are the triglyceride-to-HDL ratio and the cholesterol-to-HDL ratio.  
Elevated ratios in either case are strong markers of poor metabolic health and insulin resistance, which are often linked to small, dense HDL and LDL particles—factors that increase cardiovascular risk. Ideally, the triglyceride-to-HDL ratio should be below 2.0, and the total cholesterol-to-HDL ratio should be 2.5 or lower. These ratios give a better reflection of cardiovascular health than standard cholesterol levels alone and can indicate the presence of small, dense particles that increase plaque formation and inflammation.  
In addition to the size and quality of HDL particles, the HDLfx pCAD score and HDLfx pCEC offer valuable insight into the functionality of HDL particles. The HDLfx pCAD score evaluates how effectively HDL protects against the risk of premature coronary artery disease (pCAD), while the HDLfx pCEC (percent cholesterol efflux capacity) measures how efficiently HDL removes cholesterol from artery walls—a key defense against plaque buildup (atherosclerosis) in the blood vessels of the heart. Together, these advanced metrics capture one of HDL’s most important protective roles and reveal cardiovascular risk factors that standard HDL cholesterol measurements can miss.

* Low HDL large: May point to chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, and increased cardiovascular risk.  
* High HDL large: Typically reflects healthy cholesterol transport, good metabolic function, and lower cardiovascular risk.

The most effective approaches to increasing the levels of large HDL particles often involves reducing the consumption of refined carbohydrates and added sugars, increasing aerobic exercise, improving sleep, maintaining healthy body weight, smoking cessation, and using targeted therapies like omega-3 fatty acids, niacin, or botanicals like berberine to help shift lipid metabolism in the right direction.

What makes this biomarker fluctuate  
The levels of large HDL particles can change over time because they are influenced by several physiological and lifestyle factors that affect how the body metabolizes lipids and manages inflammation. Factors like diet, exercise, sleep quality, stress levels, and even recent illness can alter the pattern of HDL particles. For example, eating a diet high in sugar or refined carbs can lower large HDL particles, while regular exercise and healthy fats like omega-3s can help boost it. Because these factors can vary from day to day, your HDL large result isn’t always fixed. It may rise or fall slightly depending on what’s happening in your body at the moment, especially if you're healing from inflammation, losing weight, or making changes to your daily routine. It is helpful to evaluate HDL large alongside other biomarkers, instead of relying on a single number.

This pattern is often seen in individuals with metabolic dysfunction, such as insulin resistance, prediabetes, or metabolic syndrome. While total HDL cholesterol may still appear normal, having fewer large HDL particles reduces the body’s ability to clear excess cholesterol and can silently increase the risk for cardiovascular disease over time.

By addressing these underlying causes, rather than just managing symptoms, a root cause approach seeks to restore the body’s natural balance and promote long-term health and vitality. Greater detail on diet, lifestyle, and other considerations is provided in the sections that follow, but a general overview includes:

Focus on increasing healthy fats (from sources like extra virgin olive oil, avocados, fatty fish, flaxseeds, and walnuts) and fiber-rich foods (from fruits, vegetables, and whole grains) while avoiding refined carbohydrates, added sugars, trans fats, and processed foods.  
* Lifestyle modifications: Engaging in regular aerobic and strength-training exercises can help raise HDL levels and improve lipid profiles. Aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity exercise weekly, paired with stress-reduction techniques such as yoga, meditation, or mindfulness practices.  
* Weight management: If living with excess weight or obesity, gradual, sustainable weight loss can improve HDL levels and reduce insulin resistance.  
* Liver support: The liver is responsible for producing and recycling HDL cholesterol. Supporting liver function by avoiding alcohol overconsumption and processed foods, while including liver-supportive nutrients can help improve cholesterol metabolism.  
* Detoxification: If necessary, implementing protocols to reduce the body’s toxic load, including eliminating exposure to heavy metals, pesticides, and other environmental toxins, may be needed. Supporting detox pathways with nutrients (e.g., cruciferous vegetables), targeted supplements (e.g., glutathione), and saunas promotes detoxification.

* Targeted supplementation: Supplements such as omega-3 fatty acids, and botanicals like berberine can help improve HDL function and support cardiovascular health.  
* Pharmaceutical management: If lifestyle and dietary changes are not enough, pharmacological interventions with lipid-modifying medications such as statins, fibrates, niacin, or prescription omega-3 fatty acids may be beneficial, as deemed necessary by a clinician, especially for high-risk individuals.  
* Metabolic health: Insulin resistance is closely linked to low levels of HDL large. Regular monitoring of fasting glucose, insulin, and HbA1c levels, particularly for those living with insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, or metabolic syndrome, ensures appropriate glycemic control and cardiometabolic health.  
* Follow-up testing: Monitoring HDL, LDL, and triglyceride levels, with a focus on particle size and functionality, as well as inflammatory markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and homocysteine over time may help assess the effectiveness of interventions and adjust strategies as needed.

#### Causes

Root causes of low HDL large

A root cause approach to low levels of HDL large focuses on addressing core imbalances like chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and impaired lipid metabolism, which not only lower the levels of large, protective HDL particles but also have the potential to elevate the risk of cardiovascular disease.

Low levels of HDL large diminish the body’s ability to clear excess LDL cholesterol. When LDL oxidizes (oxLDL), it triggers an inflammatory response, leading to plaque buildup in the arteries. This is often fueled by an imbalance of harmful factors, such as overconsumption of refined carbohydrates and processed foods, trans fats, and chronic stress, with insufficient levels of protective elements, like healthy fats (omega-3 fatty acids), antioxidants, and fiber. These imbalances disrupt metabolic health, increase oxidative damage to lipoproteins, and impair the efficiency of HDL, hindering the body's ability to protect arteries from cholesterol buildup. A root cause approach aims to enhance HDL's role in clearing oxidized LDL from the arteries.

Diet and lifestyle factors

* Poor diet: Diets high in refined carbohydrates, added sugars, and trans fats are closely linked to lower levels of HDL large. These foods contribute to poor blood sugar regulation, higher levels of insulin and triglycerides, and may lower protective HDL particles.  
* Sedentary lifestyle: Lack of physical activity is associated with low HDL cholesterol. Regular aerobic exercise helps raise protective HDL particles and improves cholesterol transport by enhancing metabolic health.  
* Chronic stress and poor sleep: Both raise cortisol and inflammatory markers, which can interfere with lipid metabolism and reduce HDL function.

Other factors

* Chronic inflammation: Ongoing inflammation disrupts how HDL particles are formed and reduces their ability to clear cholesterol from the arteries. Inflammatory markers, such as hs-CRP and IL-6, are often elevated in individuals with low HDL.  
* Oxidative stress: Oxidative stress, caused by an imbalance between free radicals and antioxidants in the body, damages HDL particles, reducing their ability to transport cholesterol.  
* Insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction: Insulin resistance, often associated with conditions like metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes, plays a significant role in lowering HDL large. These conditions shift towards smaller, denser particles and lower the production of large HDL.  
* Gut dysbiosis: Imbalances in gut bacteria and a leaky gut can increase inflammation and disrupt lipid metabolism, lowering HDL quality.  
* Liver dysfunction: Since HDL particles are produced and processed in the liver, any stress on the liver (like fatty liver disease) can lower HDL large.  
* Thyroid dysfunction: In particular, hypothyroidism is associated with reduced HDL levels due to impaired lipid metabolism.  
* Genetic factors: Some individuals may have genetic variations, such as familial hypoalphalipoproteinemia, which cause naturally low HDL levels.

Environmental toxin exposure

Certain environmental toxins have been shown to reduce levels of large HDL particles. Here are some environmental toxins that may have an impact on HDL large:

* Air pollution: Increased exposure to fine particulate matter (PM2.5), commonly found in air pollution, has been associated with decreased HDL function, including reduced cholesterol efflux capacity and antioxidant properties.  
* Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS): PFAS, a group of synthetic chemicals, have been linked to alterations in lipid metabolism. These include changes in HDL particle distribution, notably a decrease in large HDL particles that may compromise cardiovascular protection.

For more information, visit [Environmental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/environmental-health-101).

Substance use

Certain substances have been shown to reduce levels of large HDL particles. The following substances may have an impact on HDL large:

* Tobacco: Nicotine and other harmful chemicals in cigarettes promote an oxidative environment in the body and directly lower the quality and function of HDL particles, thus impairing the body's ability to effectively clear cholesterol from arteries.  
* Alcohol: Excessive, chronic drinking leads to liver damage, which impairs the liver’s ability to process fats and cholesterol. This can result in an unfavorable lipid profile, including low levels of large, protective HDL particles. For individuals with low HDL cholesterol and/or a fatty liver, eliminating alcohol entirely is critical until cholesterol and liver function return to healthy levels.

#### Associated conditions

General conditions and diseases

Due to HDL’s role in cholesterol transport and its protective effects on the cardiovascular system, low levels of large, protective HDL particles can be linked to several conditions.

* Cardiovascular disease (CVD): Low levels of HDL large impair the body's ability to remove excess cholesterol from arteries, leading to plaque buildup and increased risk of heart attacks and strokes.  
* Metabolic syndrome: Low HDL is one of the key diagnostic criteria for metabolic syndrome. It is a cluster of risk factors, including high blood pressure, high blood sugar, and excess belly fat, that increases the risk of diabetes and heart disease.  
* Type 2 diabetes: Low HDL large is commonly seen in individuals with insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes, as poor glucose regulation negatively impacts lipid metabolism.  
* Obesity: Obesity, especially abdominal fat (or visceral fat), is strongly associated with low HDL large levels due to its role in promoting insulin resistance and chronic inflammation.  
* Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS): Women with PCOS often have low levels of HDL large due to underlying insulin resistance and impaired lipid metabolism, which increases their risk for cardiovascular disease.  
* Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD): Low HDL is frequently seen in NAFLD, a condition where fat accumulates in the liver, as both are driven by metabolic dysfunction and insulin resistance.  
* Inflammatory diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis): Chronic inflammation in conditions like rheumatoid arthritis can lower HDL large, increasing cardiovascular risk.  
* Tangier’s disease: A rare genetic disorder characterized by severely reduced or absent HDL cholesterol due to mutations in the ABCA1 gene, which impairs the body's ability to transport cholesterol out of cells for removal. It leads to the accumulation of cholesterol in tissues, causing enlarged tonsils, hepatosplenomegaly (enlarged liver and spleen), and an increased risk of cardiovascular disease.  
* Lecithin-cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT) deficiency: A rare genetic disorder that impairs the enzyme LCAT, which is essential for converting free cholesterol into cholesterol esters, a process vital for HDL formation and maturation. As a result, individuals with LCAT deficiency have abnormally low HDL cholesterol levels, which can lead to progressive kidney disease, corneal opacities, and an increased risk of cardiovascular complications due to impaired cholesterol transport.

Mental health conditions

Low HDL cholesterol has been linked to several mental health conditions, likely due to its role in regulating brain function, inflammation, and lipid metabolism. This highlights the potential link between HDL cholesterol, brain function, and emotional well-being, suggesting that lipid metabolism plays a key role not only in physical health but also in mental health outcomes.

Low levels of large, protective HDL particles may be linked to an increased risk of depression, cognitive decline, and potentially Alzheimer's disease. Additionally, low HDL levels have been linked to lower cognitive function and reduced gray matter volume in brain regions associated with neurodegenerative disease. This is because HDL also reduces inflammation and supports blood flow to the brain. When HDL function is compromised, the brain may not get the nutrients and oxygen it needs.

A gut-friendly diet

Glucose control and insulin sensitivity: Certain foods and spices, such as bitter melon, cinnamon, fenugreek, ginger, and turmeric, have been shown to support glucose control and improve insulin sensitivity. Ginger reduces fasting blood sugar by increasing insulin secretion, and turmeric's active compound, curcumin, reduces inflammation and enhances insulin sensitivity, making these foods particularly beneficial for managing insulin resistance.

  * Packaged snacks such as chips, pretzels, and salted nuts.  
  * Canned soups and vegetables.

#### Symptoms

Atherosclerosis

* Chest pain or discomfort (angina): May occur if plaque buildup in the arteries begins to restrict blood flow to the heart.  
* Shortness of breath: Particularly seen with activity and could reflect reduced oxygen delivery due to narrowed arteries or poor circulation.  
* Dizziness or lightheadedness  
* Palpitations or irregular heat rhythm

#### Self care

Exercise and movement

Exercise is one of the most effective lifestyle interventions for raising HDL cholesterol. Exercise is a key intervention in addressing underlying metabolic issues that often accompany low levels of HDL large, such as insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and oxidative stress. The emphasis is on creating a personalized exercise plan that fits health status, fitness level, and preferences.

* Aerobic exercise: Regular aerobic activities, such as brisk walking, running, swimming, and cycling, are highly effective at increasing HDL. Moderate-to-vigorous aerobic exercise improves lipid profiles, with the most significant increases in HDL seen when performed at higher intensities or longer durations. Aerobic exercise enhances fat metabolism, reduces excess body fat, and improves insulin sensitivity, which all contribute to higher HDL levels and better cholesterol transport in the body.  
* Resistance and strength training: Resistance training, such as weightlifting or bodyweight exercises (e.g., push-ups, squats), also contributes to increasing HDL, particularly when combined with aerobic exercise. Resistance training helps build muscle, reduces fat mass, and improves metabolic health, which can contribute to improved HDL size and quality.  
* High-intensity interval training (HIIT): HIIT involves short bursts of intense exercise followed by recovery periods and has been shown to be effective at improving overall cardiovascular health. This type of exercise maximizes cardiovascular benefits in shorter periods. HIIT increases fat oxidation and improves insulin sensitivity, both of which can boost HDL levels more quickly than moderate-intensity continuous exercise.

For more detailed information on exercise, please refer to [Exercise 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/exercise-101).

Weight management

Sustainable, personalized weight management strategies that target the root causes of low HDL large, such as nutrient deficiencies, insulin resistance, and lack of physical activity, are vital for achieving lasting improvements in cardiovascular health and reducing overall risk. Weight loss, particularly for those living with excess weight or obesity, can significantly improve the levels of protective HDL particles.

* Reduces insulin resistance: Excess body fat, particularly visceral fat, is strongly associated with insulin resistance and an increased risk of cardiovascular disease. Insulin resistance not only lowers HDL large but also increases triglycerides, creating an unfavorable lipid profile. Healthy weight loss improves insulin sensitivity, which in turn boosts large HDL particles.  
* Enhances fat metabolism: Weight loss, especially through a combination of dietary changes and exercise, improves the body's ability to metabolize fat. This enhanced fat metabolism leads to more effective lipid clearance, reducing LDL cholesterol and increasing HDL cholesterol.  
* Reduces systemic inflammation: Obesity is closely linked to chronic, low-grade inflammation, which impairs HDL function. Weight loss lowers inflammatory markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), improving HDL cholesterol functionality.  
* Improves hormone balance: Hormonal imbalances, particularly involving leptin and adiponectin, are common in individuals with excess body weight and are associated with reduced HDL levels. Weight loss helps restore the balance of these hormones.  
* Reduces triglycerides: Weight loss is associated with significant reductions in triglyceride levels, which is often accompanied by an increase in HDL large. Lowering triglycerides allows HDL to function more efficiently in cholesterol transport.

Low levels of HDL large can be a concern for both ends of the nutrition spectrum, that is, being overweight as well as being underweight and malnourished. Being underweight is associated with low levels of HDL due to inadequate intake of healthy fats, which are essential for HDL synthesis, and potential micronutrient deficiencies like vitamins A, B3, B12, D, and E, zinc, and calcium that support lipid metabolism. Additionally, underlying malabsorption disorders may impair HDL production further. Addressing these factors through balanced nutrition and lifestyle adjustments can help optimize HDL particle size.

Refer to [Weight Management 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/weight-management-101) for foundational principles for healthy weight management.

Sleep optimization

Chronic sleep deprivation can disrupt lipid metabolism, worsen inflammation, and increase the risk of cardiovascular diseases. By prioritizing sleep, both large HDL particle levels and overall metabolic health can potentially be improved.

* Enhances lipid metabolism: Poor sleep negatively affects lipid metabolism, leading to lower HDL levels. Adequate sleep (7-9 hours per night) supports the body's ability to process fats effectively.  
* Reduces inflammation: Chronic sleep deprivation leads to an increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 and hs-CRP, which contribute to cardiovascular risk. Improving sleep duration and quality helps regulate the immune system and reduce the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, allowing HDL to maintain its protective effects.  
* Improves insulin sensitivity: Sleep regulates glucose metabolism and insulin secretion. Improving sleep quality regulates blood sugar levels and enhances insulin sensitivity, which in turn may improve levels of HDL large.  
* Supports hormonal balance: Sleep plays a key role in regulating hormones that impact lipid metabolism, such as cortisol, leptin, and adiponectin. Adequate sleep helps balance cortisol and improves the secretion of leptin and adiponectin, which regulate fat metabolism.  
* Promotes weight management: Poor sleep contributes to weight gain, particularly visceral fat, which is closely linked to increased cardiovascular risk. Sleep also influences the production of hormones like ghrelin and leptin, which regulate hunger and satiety. Better sleep leads to improved appetite control, reducing the likelihood of overeating and excess weight gain.

Visit [Sleep 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/sleep-101) for practical tips on improving sleep.

Mental health and stress reduction

Mental health and stress reduction practices are essential for improving levels of protective HDL particles and managing inflammation, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular disease. Personalized lifestyle interventions to address both the physical and emotional aspects of health lead to long-term improvements in overall well-being.

* Reduces chronic inflammation: Stress reduction techniques, such as mindfulness, meditation, cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), and yoga, lower inflammation and regulate cortisol, allowing HDL to function optimally and reduce cardiovascular risk.  
* Enhances insulin sensitivity: Practices like mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) improve glucose regulation and insulin sensitivity by lowering cortisol levels.  
* Supports healthy weight management: Stress management helps regulate appetite hormones, such as ghrelin and leptin, which influence hunger and satiety and reduce emotional eating and weight gain, and the accumulation of visceral fat, all of which negatively impact the size of HDL particles.  
* Improves cardiovascular health: Techniques like mindfulness and yoga improve heart rate variability, lower blood pressure, and enhance overall cardiovascular function.

* Microbiome balance: Balancing the microbiome with probiotics, prebiotics, fermented foods (e.g., kefir, sauerkraut), and dietary fiber can reduce gut permeability and inflammation and improve lipid metabolism. See [Gut Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/gut-health-101).

Root cause approach to diagnosing low HDL large

Blood testing

* Apolipoprotein B (ApoB): Measures atherogenic (plaque-forming) particles in the blood, which is a more accurate predictor of heart risk.  
* LDL particle size: Identifies whether LDL particles are small and dense (more harmful) or large and fluffy (less harmful).  
* LDL particle number: LDL particle number measures the actual number of LDL cholesterol particles in the blood.  
* LDL pattern: Classifies LDL particles as pattern A (large, buoyant, and less risky LDL) or pattern B (smaller, denser, and higher risk for atherosclerosis).  
* LDL peak size: Identifies the predominant LDL particle size, where smaller peak sizes are associated with increased cardiovascular risk.  
* Oxidized LDL (oxLDL): Direct measurement of oxidized LDL to evaluate the degree of oxidative stress on cholesterol.  
* Lipoprotein(a) \[Lp(a)\]: A genetic form of LDL that increases cardiovascular risk regardless of other cholesterol levels.  
* Fasting glucose and insulin: Elevated insulin or blood sugar can point to insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome, which is often seen alongside low levels of large, protective HDL particles.  
* Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c): Shows average blood sugar levels over the past 2-3 months and helps reveal hidden metabolic issues.  
* Insulin resistance score: The insulin resistance score is a valuable tool for assessing insulin resistance (IR) and identifying the risk of prediabetes and diabetes. It combines measurements of insulin and C-peptide from a single fasting blood sample, offering insights into how well the body processes glucose and insulin​. This score is especially useful for detecting IR in individuals who may have normal glucose and HbA1c levels but are still at risk due to underlying metabolic issues​.  
* Liver function: Assesses how well the liver is processing fats and cholesterol.  
* Thyroid function: An underactive thyroid can slow metabolism and lead to lower HDL levels.

Urine testing

* Urinalysis: A general urine test can reveal signs of metabolic or kidney dysfunction, which are often linked to poor cholesterol profiles.  
* Microalbumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR): Assesses early kidney damage. A high ACR can be a marker of endothelial dysfunction, a condition where the inner lining of blood vessels is damaged, potentially increasing the risk of cardiovascular disease.  
* 24-hour urine cortisol: Elevated cortisol can contribute to impaired lipid metabolism.

Diagnostic imaging

* Heart CT Scan with Gated CAC: Measures calcium buildup in the coronary arteries and calculates the coronary artery calcium (CAC) score to assess long-term heart disease risk. The images are timed with your heartbeat to avoid blurriness from the heart’s normal movement and rhythm.  
* Carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT) ultrasound: A noninvasive ultrasound scan that measures the thickness of the carotid artery's inner layers. IT helps detect early signs of atherosclerosis in the carotid arteries (major blood vessels in the neck that supply blood to the brain) before symptoms appear and assess future stroke risk.  
* AI-enhanced coronary CT angiogram: This CT scan gives a clearer picture of both calcified and soft plaques in the coronary arteries. The CT angiogram is AI-enhanced for more precise evaluation of total plaque burden, location, distribution, and rupture risk.  
* Echocardiogram (ECHO): An ECHO can help evaluate the structure and function of the heart.  
* Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI): An MRI can be used to assess plaque in arteries and the health of the heart muscle.

Nutrient imbalances

* Vitamin D (25-hydroxy): Low vitamin D is linked to inflammation and poor cardiovascular outcomes.  
* Omega-3 index: Measures the percentage of EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid) and DHA (docosahexaenoic acid), two types of omega-3 fatty acids, in red blood cell membranes; low levels can contribute to inflammation and cholesterol imbalance.  
* Magnesium (RBC): Low magnesium is often found in people with metabolic issues.  
* Homocysteine: High levels may reflect poor methylation and can damage the lining of arteries and promote the formation of plaques, especially when large HDL particles are lower.  
* Vitamin B12 and folate (B9): These B vitamins are essential for methylation and reducing homocysteine levels.  
* Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10): A key antioxidant needed for heart and mitochondrial health; low levels may impair lipid metabolism and are especially important for those on statins.  
* Zinc and copper: An imbalance may reflect oxidative stress or poor antioxidant defenses, which can worsen heart risk.  
* Vitamin C: Deficiency impairs antioxidant defense, leading to oxidative stress.  
* Vitamin E: Insufficient levels decrease protection against lipid peroxidation (oxidative damage of fats in cell membranes triggered by free radicals).

Microbiome and gut health assessment

* Comprehensive stool analysis: Assesses the balance between beneficial and harmful bacteria in the gut. Looks for markers of dysbiosis, yeast overgrowth, or parasitic infections. #### Follow up

Medical interventions

Collaboration with your healthcare provider ensures a personalized approach to managing low levels of HDL large. Consideration of all symptoms, laboratory test results, and lifestyle factors can guide a treatment plan tailored to your specific needs and health goals.

Medications

Several medications may be prescribed to manage conditions and diseases associated with low levels of large HDL particles, primarily aiming to reduce overall cardiovascular risk by addressing lipid imbalances, insulin resistance, or inflammation.

* Statins (e.g., atorvastatin, simvastatin): Statins are used to treat cardiovascular disease (CVD) and atherosclerosis. They reduce LDL cholesterol and triglycerides while modestly increasing HDL levels. They also lower inflammation and stabilize arterial plaque.  
* Fibrates (e.g., fenofibrate, gemfibrozil): Used to treat hypertriglyceridemia and metabolic syndrome. Fibrates increase HDL cholesterol and lower triglycerides by activating peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), which regulate lipid metabolism.  
* Niacin (vitamin B3): Niacin directly increases HDL levels by inhibiting the breakdown of apolipoprotein A1, a key component of HDL particles, and reduces LDL cholesterol and triglycerides. While niacin can improve HDL levels, it may carry risks such as liver toxicity and flushing, and should be used only after discussing it with your healthcare provider.  
* PCSK9 inhibitors (e.g., alirocumab, evolocumab): PCSK9 inhibitors lower LDL cholesterol significantly and may slightly increase HDL by preventing the degradation of LDL receptors, thus enhancing cholesterol clearance from the blood.  
* Thiazolidinediones (TZDs, e.g., pioglitazone): Used to treat type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome, TZDs improve insulin sensitivity and increase HDL levels by activating PPAR-γ receptors, which influence lipid and glucose metabolism.  
* Omega-3 fatty acids: Prescription-strength omega-3 fatty acids lower triglycerides and slightly increase HDL by reducing the liver's production of triglycerides.  
* Ezetimibe: Ezetimibe lowers LDL cholesterol by inhibiting cholesterol absorption in the intestines. While its effect on HDL is modest, it is often used in combination with statins to reduce cardiovascular risk.  
* Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors (e.g., empagliflozin): SGLT2 inhibitors improve HDL levels and reduce cardiovascular risk by lowering blood glucose, promoting weight loss, and reducing inflammation.  
* GLP-1 receptor agonists (e.g., liraglutide): GLP-1 receptor agonists improve insulin sensitivity, aid in weight loss, and may modestly raise HDL cholesterol while reducing LDL and triglycerides.

Procedures

Several medical procedures, including surgeries, may be necessary to treat cardiovascular conditions associated with low levels of HDL large, particularly when lifestyle changes and medications are insufficient. These procedures primarily target underlying cardiovascular or metabolic complications.

* Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG): CABG reroutes blood flow around blocked arteries, improving heart function in individuals with severe atherosclerosis.  
* Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI): PCI, including angioplasty and stent placement, opens narrowed arteries to restore blood flow and alleviate symptoms of coronary artery disease.  
* Bariatric surgery: Bariatric surgery reduces stomach size to promote significant weight loss, improving HDL levels and reducing cardiovascular risk in individuals with obesity and metabolic syndrome.  
* Carotid endarterectomy: Carotid endarterectomy removes plaque from carotid arteries to reduce stroke risk in those with significant atherosclerosis.  
* Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR): TAVR replaces a narrowed aortic valve to improve heart function, particularly in those with high cardiovascular risk.  
* Heart transplant: A heart transplant may be necessary for end-stage heart failure caused by severe cardiovascular complications.  
* Liver transplantation: Liver transplantation restores liver function and improves lipid metabolism, especially in those with end-stage liver disease.  
* Endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR): EVAR places a stent graft in the aorta to prevent aneurysm rupture related to vascular disease.

Retesting

Retesting for HDL large is typically advised 4-12 weeks after implementing a management plan, depending on the interventions used. This period allows time for lifestyle changes, medications, or other therapies to take effect on lipid levels. If the management plan includes dietary modifications, exercise, or smoking cessation, a follow-up test after 3 months is usually appropriate to assess changes in HDL and overall lipid profiles. For those starting on medications like statins, fibrates, or niacin, retesting within 4-6 weeks may be recommended to monitor early effectiveness and adjust dosages if necessary.

Additionally, retesting may be needed more frequently for those with underlying conditions like diabetes or metabolic syndrome, which can complicate lipid management. Ongoing monitoring every 6-12 months may be advisable for long-term management to ensure levels of HDL large remain within a protective range and that the overall lipid profile is stable.

#### Why it matters

Inflammation is part of the body’s defense system. The classic signs of inflammation are heat (*calor*), pain (*dolor*), redness (*rubor*), and swelling (*tumor*). This is the body’s acute response to infection or injury. Acute inflammation is often a healthy response. Once the stressor or injury is resolved, the inflammation tends to subside. However, research over the last 30 years has found that low-grade systemic chronic inflammation (SCI), which is not related to injury or infection, is a major driver of chronic disease and aging.  
Chronic inflammation may be linked to nearly every major chronic disease, including heart disease, cancer, diabetes, high blood pressure, Alzheimer’s disease, depression, certain autoimmune diseases, allergies, asthma, and aging.  
High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) is a biomarker that measures inflammation levels in the body and can help predict the risk of cardiovascular disease and many other conditions. The liver produces CRP in response to inflammatory signals, and hs-CRP detects low levels of inflammation that may not cause obvious symptoms.  
Hs-CRP is often measured alongside other biomarkers, like cholesterol, blood sugar, and hormone levels, to provide a more complete picture of overall health. For example, elevated hs-CRP levels alongside high LDL cholesterol may indicate a higher risk for cardiovascular disease than cholesterol alone. Similarly, if hs-CRP is high while thyroid hormones are low, it could suggest that inflammation could be a factor in thyroid function changes. This biomarker helps clinicians assess the risk of chronic conditions, monitor disease progression, and determine whether lifestyle or medical interventions are effectively reducing inflammation.  
When hs-CRP levels are elevated, it can signify chronic, low-grade inflammation, which can be linked to certain chronic conditions. Elevated levels can also reflect lifestyle factors like poor diet, psychological stress, sleep hygiene, certain toxins, allergens, or imbalances in the gut microbiome.  
While many clinicians may routinely check hs-CRP, traditional pharmacological treatments may not address the underlying root cause(s) potentially contributing to imbalances in inflammation.  
Lowering hs-CRP starts with addressing the potential root causes of chronic inflammation by focusing on nutrition, lifestyle, and detoxification. A diet rich in colorful vegetables, fruits, healthy fats, and omega-3s may help support immune balance, while cutting back on processed foods, refined sugar, and trans fats may help reduce inflammatory triggers. Managing stress through meditation, movement, and deep breathing may help balance cortisol, a key driver of inflammation. Supporting normal detoxification pathways with hydration, fiber-rich foods, and liver-supportive nutrients may help maintain a balanced inflammatory response. Targeted micronutrients and vitamins may offer additional support. By taking a proactive, root-cause approach, it's possible to lower hs-CRP, restore balance, and build a foundation for long-term health.  
Summary  
High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) measures inflammation in your body, which plays a key role in the development of heart disease, diabetes, and other chronic illnesses. A root cause approach focuses on addressing inflammation through diet, managing stress, supporting detoxification, and managing potential underlying triggers.  
Optimal ranges  
The optimal range for high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) is below 1.0 mg/L, as this reflects minimal systemic inflammation and a lower risk of cardiovascular events.  
It is important to note that persistently elevated hs-CRP level above 10 mg/L is generally considered a strong indicator of acute inflammation or infection, and further investigation is warranted to determine the underlying cause and help guide preventive or therapeutic interventions.  
What makes this biomarker fluctuate  
Unlike some blood biomarkers that stay pretty stable, hs-CRP can fluctuate based on what’s going on in your life and body. For example, if you have a cold, an injury, poor sleep, are under emotional stress, or even exercised intensely the day before your blood draw, your hs-CRP may be temporarily elevated. Chronic conditions like autoimmune diseases or metabolic imbalances can cause an elevation over time. That’s why one single test isn’t always enough to tell the full story. It is best to look at trends over time and consider the context around the test—like whether you were sick, stressed, or inflamed in some other way—to understand what the number really means. Pairing hs-CRP with other markers may help uncover patterns, potentially helping determine the root cause.

#### Summary

A high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) level above the reference range indicates chronic, low-grade inflammation in the body, which can be a warning sign of increased risk for serious health conditions. Elevated hs-CRP is commonly linked to cardiovascular diseases like heart attack and stroke, as well as type 2 diabetes, obesity, autoimmune disorders, and certain cancers. Inflammation detected by hs-CRP often stems from underlying causes like poor diet, lack of exercise, chronic stress, poor sleep, environmental toxin exposure, gut microbial dysbiosis, or hidden infections. By identifying and addressing these underlying triggers, inflammation and risk of these chronic diseases can potentially be reduced.

Elevated hs-CRP levels reflect the body’s immune response to external stressors, even when there are no obvious symptoms. People with high hs-CRP may experience subtle signs of inflammation, such as fatigue, joint pain, or mood disturbances. In addition, chronic inflammation can weaken blood vessels, promote plaque buildup in arteries, and disrupt insulin sensitivity, potentially leading to long-term complications like heart disease, metabolic dysfunction, and autoimmune conditions. Identifying elevated hs-CRP early allows for proactive steps to reduce inflammation and help prevent these serious health outcomes.

Root cause approach

A root cause approach to addressing elevated hs-CRP involves identifying and managing factors contributing to inflammation, such as poor diet, insulin resistance, toxin exposure, gut imbalances, chronic infections and lifestyle influences, like lack of exercise, sleep disturbances, and chronic stress. This approach focuses on evaluating underlying issues and implementing strategies that support optimum health. These may include:

* Nutrition: Incorporating an anti-inflammatory diet rich in vegetables, fruits, whole grains, healthy fats (e.g., omega-3s), and protein for supportive micronutrients to address oxidative stress and inflammation. Diets high in ultra-processed foods, refined carbohydrates, trans fats, and artificial additives typically promote inflammation.  
* Lifestyle modification: Regular physical activity, mindfulness-based stress reduction practices like yoga or meditation, and quality sleep may help balance stress hormones and the inflammatory response.  
* Botanical therapies: Herbal supplements like curcumin (from turmeric), ginger, and green tea extract support inflammatory pathways and help modulate certain aspects of immune function.  
* Gut health: Supporting gut health through the use of prebiotics, probiotics, and high-fiber foods can help maintain microbial balance, strengthen the gut barrier, and potentially influence the inflammatory response.  
* Targeted supplementation: Incorporating supplements such as omega-3 fatty acids and vitamin D may help regulate immune and inflammatory pathways.  
* Pharmaceutical interventions: Certain medications may be recommended by a healthcare provider to help address inflammation and other potential causes.  
* Follow-up testing: Regular monitoring of hs-CRP, combined with comprehensive panels such as fasting glucose, fasting insulin, and advanced lipid biomarkers, provides valuable insights into the progress of treatment strategies.

#### Causes

Root causes of elevated hs-CRP

Elevated hs-CRP is typically driven by underlying factors that promote chronic inflammation, such as poor diet, excess fat around the abdomen, chronic stress, and oxidative stress. Identifying and addressing these root causes is essential to balancing inflammation and mitigating the risk of inflammation-related conditions like cardiovascular disease, metabolic dysfunction, and autoimmune diseases.

Diet and lifestyle factors

* Poor diet: Diets high in processed foods, sugars, refined carbohydrates, and unhealthy fats can promote inflammation by increasing oxidative stress and inflammatory cytokine production, potentially leading to elevated levels of hs-CRP.  
* Excess omega-6 fatty acids: Excessive consumption of vegetable and seed oils (e.g., soybean, corn) high in the omega-6 polyunsaturated fat (PUFA), linoleic acid (LA), can create an imbalance of omega-6 to omega-3 fats and exacerbate inflammatory pathways, leading to elevated hs-CRP levels.  
* Insufficient omega-3 fatty acids: Insufficient consumption of anti-inflammatory omega-3s from fatty fish, flaxseeds, or walnuts reduces the body’s ability to combat inflammation, leading to elevated hs-CRP.  
* Nutrient deficiencies: Deficiencies in essential nutrients such as vitamin D and magnesium can impair the body’s ability to optimally regulate inflammation.  
* Fiber intake: A lack of dietary fiber from fruits, vegetables, and whole grains may disrupt gut health and potentially contribute to imbalances in inflammation in the body.  
* Sedentary lifestyle: A lack of regular physical activity can exacerbate an inflammatory state.  
* Chronic stress: Persistent stress elevates cortisol levels, which can dysregulate the immune response and contribute to increased inflammation throughout the body.  
* Poor sleep quality: Inadequate or disrupted sleep promotes inflammation by impairing recovery processes and increasing stress hormone levels.  
* Aging: Aging can be associated with elevated hs-CRP due to a natural increase in chronic low-grade inflammation, often referred to as "inflammaging". This process can result from accumulated cellular damage and reduced immune regulation and mitochondrial function.

Other factors

* Gut microbial imbalances: Imbalances in the gut microbiome, such as dysbiosis or overgrowth of harmful bacteria, leaky gut (intestinal permeability), or infections, can trigger a systemic inflammatory response, increasing hs-CRP.  
* Oxidative stress: An imbalance between free radicals and antioxidants can lead to damaged cells and tissues, which can influence the inflammatory response.  
* Insulin resistance: Imbalances in blood sugar metabolism and insulin sensitivity can contribute to an inflammatory status..  
* Obesity: Excess body fat, particularly visceral fat, produces pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-alpha and IL-6) that elevate hs-CRP levels.  
* Genetics: Certain genetic factors may increase susceptibility to inflammation by affecting the body’s ability to regulate immune responses, potentially leading to chronically elevated hs-CRP levels.  
* Hormonal imbalances: Imbalances in hormones like insulin, cortisol, or estrogen can affect the body’s inflammatory response, potentially contributing to chronic inflammation and elevated hs-CRP levels.  
* Chronic infections: Persistent infections like periodontal disease can keep the immune system constantly activated, leading to elevated hs-CRP levels.

Medications

Certain medications can contribute to elevated hs-CRP, making it important to assess their impact when evaluating systemic inflammation.

* Oral contraceptives: The use of oral contraceptives has been shown to increase certain inflammatory markers such as CRP.

hs-CRP is also a marker of insulin resistance. Certain medications can impair glucose uptake and cause weight gain and metabolic dysfunction, leading to chronic low-grade inflammation and elevated hs-CRP levels.

* Antidepressants (e.g., SSRIs)  
* Antipsychotics (e.g., olanzapine, clozapine)

Environmental toxin exposure

Environmental toxins play a significant role in driving systemic inflammation by triggering oxidative stress. The cellular damage caused by free radicals can influence inflammatory pathways that modulate the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including hs-CRP. Chronic exposure to these toxins can overwhelm the body’s detoxification systems and antioxidant defenses, potentially leading to persistent low-grade inflammation and increased chronic disease risk.

* Heavy metals  
* Air pollution: Particulate matter (particularly, PM2.5 and PM10), nitrogen oxides, and ozone.  
* Pesticides: Chronic exposure to agricultural chemicals like organophosphates.  
* Forever chemicals (e.g., PFAS): Found in non-stick cookware and other household items, these are associated with chronic low-grade inflammation and elevated hs-CRP.  
* Endocrine-disrupting chemicals (e.g., BPA, phthalates): Found in plastics and personal care products, these disrupt hormonal balance and can promote the production of inflammatory cytokines like hs-CRP.  
* Mold and mycotoxins: Exposure to mold spores and mycotoxins (toxins produced by mold) in indoor environments can irritate the respiratory tract and stimulate the immune system, potentially leading to increased inflammation and elevated hs-CRP.

For more information, visit [Environmental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/environmental-health-101).

Substance use

Substance use can contribute to elevated hs-CRP by triggering systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune system dysregulation. Substances like alcohol, tobacco, and recreational drugs like cannabis introduce toxins into the body that stimulate the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which can raise hs-CRP levels. Chronic alcohol use, for example, damages the liver and disrupts gut health, leading to increased permeability of the intestinal lining (leaky gut) and promoting inflammation. Smoking introduces harmful chemicals that damage blood vessels, elevate oxidative stress, and drive cardiovascular inflammation, all of which can contribute to higher hs-CRP. Addressing substance use disorders and supporting detoxification pathways can help reduce inflammation and lower hs-CRP levels.

#### Associated conditions

General conditions and diseases

Clinical studies suggest that elevated hs-CRP levels are associated with a variety of conditions and diseases linked to chronic inflammation. These include:

* Non-infectious inflammation: Non-infectious inflammation is a chronic, low-grade immune activation that is caused by non-infectious triggers like oxidative stress, nutritional status, or tissue damage, and is associated with elevated hs-CRP.  
* Cardiovascular diseases: High hs-CRP is a strong marker for heart disease, atherosclerosis (plaque buildup in arteries), heart attacks, and strokes, as it likely reflects underlying vascular inflammation.  
* Metabolic disorders: Elevated hs-CRP is often seen in type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance, and metabolic syndrome, featuring disrupted blood sugar balance and increased cardiovascular risk.  
* Obesity: Chronic low-grade inflammation, reflected by high hs-CRP, is common in individuals with excess body fat, particularly visceral fat (fat around organs). Altered levels of inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-6 (IL-6) are common in this population, stimulating their liver to produce more hs-CRP.  
* Autoimmune diseases: Conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) often show elevated hs-CRP due to systemic immune activation.  
* Chronic infections: Persistent infections can cause long-term elevations in hs-CRP.  
* Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD): Elevated hs-CRP in MAFLD reflects chronic inflammation driven by insulin resistance, obesity, and metabolic dysfunction, contributing to the progression of liver damage. High hs-CRP levels also indicate an increased risk of cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes in individuals with MAFLD.  
* Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD): Increased hs-CRP reflects inflammation in the liver and may indicate disease progression to more severe conditions like non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH).  
* Cancer: Elevated hs-CRP has been associated with the incidence of certain site-specific cancers as well as overall cancer risk, likely due to the influence of an inflammatory microenvironment on cellular health.  
* Neurodegenerative diseases: High hs-CRP levels are associated with Alzheimer's disease and other cognitive disorders, as neuroinflammation may contribute to certain changes in brain composition and cognition. Elevated hs-CRP may further exacerbate the risk of neurodegeneration associated with the ApoE4 allele.

Mental health conditions

Elevated hs-CRP levels are strongly associated with several mental health conditions due to the complex interaction between systemic inflammation, the immune system, and neurotransmitters in the brain. While the pathogenesis and association are not fully clear, clinical evidence suggests these may include:

* Depression  
* Generalized anxiety disorder  
* Bipolar disorder  
* Schizophrenia  
* Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)

Nutrition plays a key role in overall health. [Nutrition 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/nutrition-101) is a science-based review of foundational nutritional principles and eating practices that promote health and well-being. It is founded on three core principles:

Using food as medicine to address elevated hs-CRP emphasizes nutrient-dense, anti-inflammatory options that support gut health, stabilize blood sugar levels, and combat oxidative stress, targeting the root causes of chronic inflammation.

Anti-inflammatory diet

An anti-inflammatory diet focuses on whole, nutrient-dense foods rich in antioxidants, healthy fats, and fiber to reduce systemic inflammation and support overall health, potentially slowing the progression of chronic diseases.

* Fruits and vegetables: Rich in antioxidants, vitamins, and minerals. Berries, leafy greens (e.g., collard greens, kale, spinach, Swiss chard), and cruciferous vegetables are particularly beneficial.  
* Healthy fats: Omega-3 fatty acids found in fatty fish (e.g., salmon, mackerel, and sardines), ground flaxseed, chia seeds, and walnuts can influence inflammatory pathways.  
* Whole grains: Foods like quinoa, brown rice, and steel-cut oats provide essential nutrients and dietary fiber, supporting overall health and helping maintain normal inflammatory pathways.  
* Nuts and seeds: Almonds, chia seeds, and sunflower seeds provide healthy fats and antioxidants.  
* Olive oil: A primary fat source, particularly in the Mediterranean diet, olive oil is rich in monounsaturated fats and compounds to support antioxidative status.  
* Curcumin: The active ingredient in turmeric, curcumin, supports healthy inflammatory and antioxidative pathways.  
* Ginger: Ginger is a key component of an anti-inflammatory diet because it contains bioactive compounds like gingerol, which help influence inflammatory status and help modulate immune responses.

Antioxidant foods

A diet rich in antioxidants like vitamin C, beta-carotene, and polyphenols helps neutralize free radicals and combat oxidative stress that drives inflammation.

* Dark berries: Blueberries, elderberries, and blackberries are particularly supportive of antioxidative status.  
* Green tea: Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), the bioactive compound in green tea, helps influence inflammatory pathways and supports overall metabolic health  
* Dark chocolate: Contains flavonoids that can influence antioxidative status.

A gut-friendly diet

A healthy gut microbiome helps maintain inflammatory pathways that contribute to elevated hs-CRP.

* Fermented foods: Yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi, skyr (Icelandic yogurt) and fermented vegetables.  
* High-fiber foods: Jerusalem artichokes, chicory root, garlic, onion, leek, asparagus, green banana, cruciferous vegetables, whole grains, beans, legumes, and dietary supplements containing prebiotic fiber help promote gut health and support beneficial gut bacteria.

Mediterranean diet

The Mediterranean diet is a way of eating inspired by the traditional dietary patterns of countries bordering the Mediterranean Sea, such as Greece, Italy, and Spain. It emphasizes whole, nutrient-dense plant-based foods, healthy fats, and lean proteins while limiting processed foods, refined sugars, and unhealthy fats to help reduce systemic inflammation associated with elevated hs-CRP.

* Fruits and vegetables: Fresh, seasonal produce like leafy greens, tomatoes, peppers, eggplants, citrus fruits, and berries.  
* Healthy fats: Extra-virgin olive oil as the primary fat source, along with avocados, nuts (e.g., almonds, walnuts), and seeds (e.g., chia, sunflower). A hallmark of the Mediterranean diet, extra-virgin olive oil is rich in monounsaturated fats and polyphenols like oleocanthal, which help balance pro-inflammatory pathways.  
* Whole grains: Quinoa, bulgur, farro, barley, brown rice, and steel-cut oats.  
* Legumes: Lentils, chickpeas, black beans, and peas.  
* Nuts and seeds: Almonds, walnuts, pistachios, flaxseeds, sesame, and sunflower seeds.  
* Dairy: Moderate amounts of high-quality dairy, such as plain Greek yogurt, cheese (e.g., feta, goat cheese), and milk.  
* Fish and seafood: Fatty fish like salmon, mackerel, and sardines, along with shellfish like shrimp and mussels.  
* Lean protein: Grass-fed, regeneratively or pasture-raised poultry and pasture-raised eggs.  
* Herbs and spices: Fresh and dried herbs like oregano, basil, parsley, rosemary, and turmeric.  
* Red wine: Rich in resveratrol, taken in moderation as part of meals.

Mediterranean ketogenic diet

The Mediterranean ketogenic diet (MKD) combines the principles of the Mediterranean and ketogenic diets, offering a dietary approach rich in healthy fats from fish, nuts, seeds, and olive oil. This diet emphasizes a higher intake of polyunsaturated and monounsaturated fats known for their anti-inflammatory properties. While the standard ketogenic diet effectively promotes ketosis and reduces carbs, it can also lead to increased intake of saturated fats from sources like red meat and dairy, potentially raising inflammation if not carefully balanced with other nutrients. Unlike the standard ketogenic diet, the MKD focuses on omega-3 fatty acids from fish and alpha-linolenic acid from plants, like those found in the Mediterranean diet. These fats are essential for reducing oxidative stress and lowering inflammation.

Nordic diet

The Nordic diet, similar to the Mediterranean diet, known for its nutrient-dense foods rich in anti-inflammatory properties and emphasis on local and seasonal foods, is particularly beneficial in supporting a healthy response to inflammation and improving cardiovascular health.

* Colorful fruits and vegetables: Emphasis on berries, root vegetables, and leafy greens to provide vitamins, minerals, and polyphenols with anti-inflammatory effects.  
* Fatty fish: Eating fish at least twice a week, particularly fatty fish like salmon, mackerel, and herring rich in omega-3 fatty acids.  
* Whole grains: Consumption of whole, non-processed grains like rye, oats, and barley helps support a healthy gut microbiome.  
* Healthy fats: Intake of unsaturated fats, primarily from sources like fish, nuts, seeds, and high-quality, cold-pressed, unrefined rapeseed oil.  
* Refined sugar and processed foods: Limit refined sugars, processed meats, and unhealthy fats (trans fats and saturated fats) that contribute to systemic inflammation and increase hs-CRP levels.

#### Foods to limit

The root cause of cardiometabolic diseases and other chronic conditions associated with elevated hs-CRP is often linked to dietary and lifestyle factors that can promote inflammation, oxidative stress, and insulin resistance. The main driver of chronic inflammation and overall disease risk is an ultra-processed diet that is high in starches, sugars, refined oils, and artificial additives.

* Refined carbohydrates and added sugars: Foods high in refined carbohydrates and sugars and poor in natural antioxidants and fiber spike blood sugar levels, can promote inflammation, and disrupt gut health.  
  * Sugary snacks and beverages: Soft drinks, candies, and desserts.  
  * Refined carbohydrates: White bread, pasta, and pastries.  
* Trans fats and hydrogenated oils: Trans fats are found in processed and fried foods and can increase the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including hs-CRP, and contribute to heart disease.  
  * Fried foods: French fries, fried chicken, and other deep-fried foods.  
  * Processed meats: Bacon, sausages, and other processed meats.  
  * Margarine and shortening: These contain partially hydrogenated oils, a source of trans fats.  
* Processed meats: Regular consumption of processed meats can promote inflammation and oxidative stress.  
  * Processed meats: Sausages, hot dogs, and deli meats are also often loaded with preservatives and sodium.  
* Advanced glycation end products (AGEs): AGEs are harmful compounds formed when proteins or fats react with sugars in a process called glycation, which happens naturally over time but is accelerated by high blood sugar levels, aging, and cooking foods at high temperatures (like grilling, frying, or roasting). AGEs promote oxidative stress and inflammation in the body, contributing to the development of chronic diseases.  
* High-sodium foods: Excessive salt intake can lead to hypertension and other health issues that may impact cardiovascular health.  
  * Packaged snacks such as chips, pretzels, and salted nuts.  
  * Canned soups and vegetables.  
* Artificial additives and preservatives: Artificial additives like artificial sweeteners (e.g., aspartame and saccharin), food dyes, preservatives, and flavor enhancers (e.g., MSG) can disrupt the gut microbiome and influence inflammatory responses.

Weight management

The amount of fat tissue in the body is directly proportional to inflammation levels and is

associated with an increased risk of chronic inflammation-related conditions, including cardiometabolic diseases. Excess weight, particularly visceral fat (fat around organs), can influence the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and adipokines, potentially driving chronic inflammation and raising hs-CRP. In those living with obesity, the buildup of immune cells within fat tissue, liver, and muscles, particularly pro-inflammatory macrophages (immune cells), may lead to a chronic inflammatory state, which can lead to insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction. Even modest weight loss has been shown to decrease hs-CRP levels by improving insulin sensitivity, reducing oxidative stress, and alleviating certain aspects of metabolic dysfunction associated with excess body fat.

Mindfulness-based stress reduction techniques like meditation, deep breathing, yoga, and heart rate variability (HRV) training can shift the body into a parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) state by balancing the autonomic nervous system, supporting healthy inflammatory and immune regulation. Community and social support

#### Additional tests

Root cause approach to diagnosing elevated hs-CRP

A root cause approach to diagnosing elevated hs-CRP focuses on identifying and addressing underlying drivers of systemic inflammation, such as poor diet, gut dysbiosis, chronic infections, toxin exposure, or metabolic dysfunction. The need for specific tests depends on your individual risk profile, clinical symptoms, family history, and the presence of other health conditions, as determined by your healthcare provider. *Here is a list of tests that may be relevant if hs-CRP levels are out of range, with those available through your Function membership noted below.*

Blood testing

* Complete blood count (CBC) with differential: Assesses immune function and detects hidden infections or anemia of inflammation (also known as anemia of chronic disease).  
* Fasting glucose, insulin, and HbA1c: Assesses long-term blood sugar control and potential insulin resistance, as metabolic dysfunction is a key driver of chronic inflammation.  
* Lipids: Evaluates cardiovascular risk, as inflammation plays a major role in heart disease and atherosclerosis. Lipoprotein(a), in particular, is an inherited cholesterol particle linked to inflammation and increases the risk of heart disease and stroke, especially when hs-CRP is high despite normal standard cholesterol levels.  
* Liver function: Assesses liver health, as liver dysfunction and fatty liver disease can drive systemic inflammation.  
* Thyroid function: Identifies thyroid dysfunction, which can be a hidden driver of inflammation, particularly undiagnosed autoimmune thyroid diseases.  
* Cortisol: Measures stress hormone balance, as chronic stress can drive inflammation.

Urine testing

* Urinalysis: A urinalysis can help detect proteinuria (excess protein in urine), microalbuminuria (small amounts of albumin in urine), or abnormal pH levels, which may indicate endothelial dysfunction or oxidative stress.  
* 24-hour urine cortisol: Measures stress hormone levels to evaluate whether chronic stress is fueling inflammation and elevated hs-CRP.  
* Urine creatinine: Assesses kidney function and filtration efficiency, as poor kidney health can contribute to systemic inflammation.  
* Urinary pH: Monitors body acid-alkaline balance, as an overly acidic state can correlate with inflammation and oxidative stress.  
* Heavy metals: Detects heavy metals in urine (e.g., mercury, lead, arsenic) that can drive chronic inflammation and oxidative stress.

Diagnostic imaging

Assessing cardiovascular health in the presence of elevated hs-CRP is essential because chronic inflammation plays a key role in the development of heart disease, arterial plaque buildup, and vascular dysfunction, even in individuals with normal cholesterol levels

* Carotid ultrasound: Assesses arterial plaque build-up, which may result from vascular inflammation.  
* AI-enhanced coronary CT angiogram : This CT scan gives a clearer picture of both calcified and soft plaques in the coronary arteries. The CT angiogram is AI-enhanced for more precise evaluation of total plaque burden, location, distribution, and rupture risk.  
* Carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT) test: A noninvasive ultrasound scan that measures the thickness of the carotid artery's inner layers; helps in the early detection of plaque buildup in the carotid artery.  
* Heart CT Scan with Gated CAC: Measures calcium buildup in the coronary arteries and calculates the coronary artery calcium (CAC) score to assess long-term heart disease risk. The images are timed with your heartbeat to avoid blurriness from the heart’s normal movement and rhythm.  
* Abdominal ultrasound: Evaluates liver health and fat accumulation, such as in metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), which is associated with high hs-CRP.  
* PET scan: If indicated, this may help detect inflammatory activity in tissues and organs, which may indicate underlying autoimmune, metabolic, or cardiovascular conditions.  
* CT scan (chest or abdomen): Helps identify hidden sources of inflammation, such as lung or gastrointestinal conditions, infections, or organ dysfunction.  
* DEXA scan: Measures bone density to rule out inflammation-related bone loss, such as in rheumatoid arthritis, which may correlate with elevated hs-CRP.

Nutrient imbalances

Testing nutrients help uncover deficiencies or imbalances that contribute to chronic inflammation.

* Vitamin D3: Low levels are linked to higher inflammation and poor immune regulation.  
* Omega-3 index: Measures the ratio of omega-3 to omega-6 fatty acids, as an imbalance can drive systemic inflammation and elevate hs-CRP.  
* Ferritin: High ferritin can indicate inflammation-driven iron storage issues (or iron overload).  
* Homocysteine: Elevated levels may indicate poor methylation, which can increase inflammation.  
* Magnesium: Magnesium deficiency contributes to oxidative stress, poor vascular health, and imbalances in inflammation.  
* Zinc and copper levels: An imbalance between zinc and copper can impact immune function and inflammatory responses  
* B-complex vitamins (B6, B12, folate): Deficiencies in these vitamins can elevate homocysteine, which has been shown clinically to contribute to inflammatory status and cardiovascular risk.  
* Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10): Low CoQ10 levels are associated with increased oxidative stress and inflammatory imbalance.  
* Selenium: Plays a critical role in antioxidant defense and immune regulation, with low levels linked to certain aspects of the inflammatory response.  
* Glutathione: Glutathione is the body’s master antioxidant, and low levels can contribute to oxidative damage and imbalanced inflammation.

Microbiome and gut health assessment

* Comprehensive stool analysis: Assesses the balance between beneficial and harmful bacteria in the gut. Looks for markers of dysbiosis, yeast overgrowth, or parasitic infections. Stool testing can also evaluate gut permeability ("leaky gut") by measuring zonulin levels, which indicate whether inflammatory triggers are escaping the gut and potentially contributing to systemic inflammation.  
* SIBO breath test: Detects small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO), a common cause of gut-driven inflammation and metabolic dysfunction.

See [Gut Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/gut-health-101) for more information.

Immune system evaluation

* Inflammatory markers: Measures other inflammatory markers such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and proinflammatory cytokines like IL-6, TNF-alpha, and IFN-gamma, to gauge the level of systemic inflammation.  
* Autoimmunity: Screens for autoimmune diseases that could be driving chronic inflammation. Testing for autoantibodies can help evaluate how autoimmune activity influences other biomarkers by identifying immune dysregulation, inflammation, or tissue-specific damage that may affect overall health.  
* Celiac disease: Screens for celiac disease by detecting autoimmune antibodies, including tissue transglutaminase IgA (tTG-IgA), deamidated gliadin peptide (DGP-IgG/IgA), and endomysial antibodies (EMA-IgA), triggered by gluten driving chronic gut inflammation and immune activation.  
* Food sensitivity and allergy testing: Identifies food sensitivities or allergies that may contribute to inflammatory immune activation.  
* Immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG, and IgM): Identifies immune deficiencies or overactivity that may contribute to inflammation.  
* Tick-borne infections: Detects antibodies against Lyme disease, *Babesia*, *Ehrlichia*, *Bartonella*, *Borrelia*, and *Anaplasma*, all pathogens that can drive chronic immune activation and elevated hs-CRP.  
* Viral infections: Identifies past or active (or reactivations) viral infections such as EBV, CMV, HHV, and hepatitis that can contribute to systemic inflammation and elevated hs-CRP.  
* Natural killer (NK) cell activity: Measures the ability of NK cells to kill infected and abnormal cells. Low NK cell activity is linked to chronic infections, particularly viral ones, and inflammation.

Environmental and toxin testing

Testing for environmental toxins—such as heavy metals (e.g., mercury, lead, arsenic), mycotoxins, pesticides/insecticides, plastics, endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), and other pollutants like BPA (bisphenol A) and PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances)—helps identify hidden drivers of chronic inflammation when hs-CRP is elevated. These toxins can trigger oxidative stress, disrupt immune function, and impair detoxification pathways, leading to chronic, low-grade inflammation. Urine, blood, and hair analyses can detect exposure levels, providing insight into toxic burdens that may be fueling systemic inflammation.

For more information, visit [Environmental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/environmental-health-101).

Advanced diagnostics

Elevated hs-CRP signals systemic inflammation, which can contribute to chronic disease. To dig deeper and pinpoint the root causes of inflammation, the following advanced diagnostic tests should be considered:

Cardiovascular and metabolic health

* Lipoprotein-associated phospholipase A2 (Lp-PLA2) and myeloperoxidase (MPO): Both biomarkers assess inflammation within blood vessels. They can signal the presence of vulnerable plaque in arteries that could lead to heart attack or stroke. When elevated, it can indicate a higher risk of cardiovascular disease.  
* Oxidized LDL (OxLDL): Measures an oxidatively modified form of LDL cholesterol that contributes to the development of atherosclerosis. It is a key biomarker for heart disease risk.  
* Apolipoprotein B (ApoB): Provides a more accurate picture of cardiovascular risk than standard cholesterol tests.  
* Insulin resistance score: Measures how well the body utilizes insulin and helps detect insulin resistance early, a root driver of inflammation.

Mitochondrial and oxidative stress

* Oxidative stress markers: Evaluates oxidative stress through biomarkers like 8-OHdG (8-Hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine), 8-isoprostane (also known as F2-isoprostane), or plasma malondialdehyde (MDA). These tests help gauge the level of oxidative damage and DNA damage in the body.  
* Organic acids: Organic acid tests can detect imbalances in microbial byproducts, mitochondrial dysfunction, and nutritional deficiencies that indicate dysbiosis and systemic oxidative stress.

Genetic testing

Genetic testing can help identify underlying predispositions to inflammation, cardiovascular disease, and metabolic dysfunction. Here are the most pertinent genetic markers to test:

* Cardiovascular and metabolic risk  
  * 9p21 (rs10757278, rs1333049): Linked to atherosclerosis and heart disease, especially in those with high hs-CRP.  
  * Apolipoprotein E (ApoE) genotype: The APOE4 variant is associated with higher inflammation and cardiovascular risk.  
  * LPA aspirin genotype: LPA 4399Met carriers may have a higher inflammatory and thrombotic burden, affecting aspirin response and hs-CRP levels.  
  * 4q25 AF risk (rs220733, rs10033464): These variants are strongly linked to atrial fibrillation (AF) and inflammation-driven heart remodeling.  
* Oxidative stress and detoxification  
  * Glutathione S-transferases (GSTT1/GSTM1): These detox enzymes help protect cells from damage. Any defects may lead to higher oxidative stress and inflammation.  
  * Superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2): Regulates antioxidant defense; mutations increase mitochondrial oxidative stress.  
  * Glutathione peroxidase 1 (GPX1): Impaired function leads to higher oxidative damage and inflammatory markers.  
* Methylation and homocysteine regulation  
  * MTHFR gene: Poor methylation can increase homocysteine and inflammation.

Cognitive assessments

Elevated hs-CRP can significantly impact cognitive function by driving systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation —key contributors to brain fog, memory loss, and neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer’s. Chronic inflammation can disrupt the blood-brain barrier, impair mitochondrial function, and fuel insulin resistance in the brain, reducing neuroplasticity and cognitive resilience. Cognitive testing can help identify early cognitive decline, attention deficits, and processing delays, even before symptoms become noticeable. By combining cognitive testing with inflammatory markers like hs-CRP, personalized interventions can be implemented to improve cognitive performance and long-term brain health.

For more information about maintaining optimum brain health, including cognition, memory, and ways to reduce the risk of age-related neurodegenerative disorders, visit [Brain Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/brain-health-101).

#### Follow up

Medical interventions

Collaboration with your healthcare provider ensures a personalized approach to managing high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) levels. Consideration of all symptoms, laboratory test results, and lifestyle factors can guide a treatment plan tailored to your specific needs and health goals.

Medications

Sometimes, certain medications may be prescribed for conditions linked to elevated hs-CRP to manage inflammation, cardiovascular risk, and immune dysfunction.

Autoimmune and inflammatory diseases

* Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (e.g., Ibuprofen, naproxen)  
* Corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone, dexamethasone)  
* Biologics (e.g., TNF inhibitors like adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab)  
* Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) (e.g., methotrexate, sulfasalazine)

Cardiovascular and metabolic conditions

* Statins (e.g., atorvastatin, rosuvastatin)  
* ACE inhibitors/ARBs (e.g., lisinopril and losartan, respectively)  
* PCSK9 inhibitors (e.g., evolocumab, alirocumab)  
* Aspirin (low-dose)  
* Metformin  
* SGLT2 inhibitors (e.g., empagliflozin, canagliflozin)  
* GLP-1 receptor agonists (e.g., semaglutide, liraglutide)

Chronic infections

* Antibiotics (e.g., doxycycline, azithromycin, rifampin)  
* Antivirals (e.g., valacyclovir, tenofovir)

Procedures

Medical procedures and surgeries are typically not indicated solely to lower hs-CRP but may be necessary in severe cases where systemic inflammation has led to cardiovascular dysfunction or chronic disease complications. When inflammation-driven damage becomes significant, the following interventions can restore blood flow, repair affected tissues, and manage disease progression.

Autoimmune and inflammatory diseases

* Joint replacement surgery (e.g., knee or hip replacement): For osteoarthritis or inflammatory joint disease worsened by chronic inflammation.  
* Endoscopic procedures (e.g., colonoscopy with biopsy): Used to detect inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) or intestinal permeability issues.  
* Plasmapheresis: Removes inflammatory immune complexes in severe autoimmune or cardiovascular conditions.

Cardiovascular and metabolic conditions

* Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG): Used for severe atherosclerosis to improve blood flow to the heart.  
* Angioplasty and stent placement: Open narrowed arteries to reduce cardiovascular risk.  
* Endarterectomy: Removes plaque buildup in arteries.  
* Bariatric surgery (e.g., gastric bypass, sleeve gastrectomy): Helps with weight loss in those living with obesity and improves metabolic health  
* Liver biopsy: Evaluates liver inflammation and fibrosis in conditions like NAFLD.

Chronic infections

* IV antibiotic therapy: Treats persistent infections contributing to chronic inflammation.

Retesting

Retesting high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) is advisable to track progress and ensure inflammation is under control. Timing depends on the underlying condition and intervention strategy.

* 4-6 weeks: If making targeted lifestyle, dietary, or medication changes to assess early response.  
* 3 months: After implementing anti-inflammatory interventions (e.g., gut healing, metabolic support, detoxification).  
* 6 months: For chronic conditions (e.g., autoimmune disease, cardiovascular disease) to monitor long-term inflammation trends.  
* Annually: As part of preventative health screening, especially if you have an underlying metabolic or cardiovascular condition.

#### Why it matters

Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol is a type of lipoprotein that carries cholesterol through the bloodstream to different parts of the body. While lower LDL is generally beneficial, cholesterol is more complex than just "good" or "bad." The size, quality, and number of cholesterol particles provide more insight than standard tests, which only measure the number of HDL (high-density lipoprotein) or LDL by weight (measured in milligrams per deciliter or mg/dL of blood) and don't reveal the health of the particles themselves. This test, which includes several biomarkers, is called lipoprotein fractionation.  
Cholesterol particles can be small and dense, which increases risk, or large and fluffy, which are more protective. Think beach ball versus golf ball. The golf ball hurts if it hits you. The beach ball does not. The goal is to have more light, fluffy, and functional particles. Two people can have the same LDL cholesterol level—such as 150 mg/dL—but very different risk profiles depending on the size and number of particles. One person might have mostly large, buoyant particles and fewer than 1,000 total LDL particles, putting them at low risk. Another person with the same LDL level might have 1,000 small, dense particles, significantly increasing their risk for a heart attack. Same number on paper, vastly different implications for treatment. The first is relatively benign, and the second requires an aggressive treatment strategy. Yet standard cholesterol tests only measure the total weight, which doesn’t fully reflect heart disease risk.  
LDL peak size is a marker that measures the size of the most common type of LDL particles in the blood. Not all LDL is the same; some particles are large and fluffy, while others are small and dense. The LDL peak size helps understand which type dominates in the body. Smaller, denser LDL particles are more dangerous because they can slip into the walls of the arteries more easily and trigger inflammation, which can lead to plaque buildup and heart disease. Larger LDL particles are less likely to do this, so knowing the LDL peak size gives a clearer picture of real cardiovascular risk.

* Low LDL peak size (typically \<217.4 Angstrom): Suggests a dominance of small, dense LDL particles, linked to higher risk of heart disease, insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, and systemic inflammation.  
* High LDL peak size (typically \>222.9 Angstrom): Indicates larger, more buoyant LDL particles, which are less likely to cause arterial damage, and is associated with a lower cardiovascular risk.

LDL peak size is best understood in the context of comprehensive lipid and metabolic markers which includes LDL particle number, LDL pattern, triglycerides (a type of fat in the blood that stores unused calories for energy), HDL (the “good” kind that typically helps clear extra cholesterol away), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), and lipoprotein(a). For example, if someone has a LDL peak size that is small and also has an elevated LDL particle number and ApoB, there is a higher concentration of harmful LDL particles circulating, which points to increased risk. It is a useful marker to help guide clinical decisions around diet, lifestyle, and medical interventions, if needed. Knowing the LDL peak size can add a layer of clarity to a standard cholesterol test.  
Optimizing HDL if there is lipid imbalance  
Optimizing HDL cholesterol is especially important with a lower LDL peak size, as large, more buoyant HDL particles are protective and can help counterbalance the associated risks. While smaller, more dense HDL particles are often caused by insulin resistance, prediabetes, and type 2 diabetes, and increases the risk of heart disease. In addition to the size and quality of HDL particles, there are other tests to consider when lipid markers point to an increased risk. Advanced tests like the pCAD score and pCEC, together can capture one of HDL’s most important protective roles and reveal cardiovascular risk factors that standard HDL cholesterol measurements can miss.  
LDL peak size looks past the standard cholesterol numbers and focuses on the *size* and *quality* of cholesterol particles, not just the quantity. It offers insight into how well metabolism, blood sugar, and inflammatory systems are working together (or not), and can guide more personalized choices around nutrition, supplements, or medications to support long-term cardiometabolic health.  
Summary  
LDL peak size shows the most common size of LDL particles. A smaller LDL peak size can point to deeper issues like poor diet, insulin resistance, or inflammation, all underlying causes that need to be addressed to protect the heart and overall health, and not just treat the symptoms.  
Optimal ranges  
The optimal range for LDL peak size is \>222.9 Angstrom. Larger LDL particles are considered less harmful, while smaller, denser LDL particles are more likely to contribute to plaque buildup in arteries, increasing cardiovascular risk.  
What makes this biomarker fluctuate  
LDL peak size can change based on what's happening in your body and lifestyle. Factors like what you eat, how much you exercise, stress levels, blood sugar control, and even how much sleep you get can all influence it. For example, eating a meal high in refined carbs or sugars can temporarily raise triglycerides, which may lead to smaller, more dense LDL particles. Similarly, if you're under a lot of stress or not sleeping well, your body may have more inflammation, which can also lower your LDL peak size. Because of all these influences, your LDL peak size can fluctuate slightly from day to day or week to week. That's why it's important to look at trends over time, not just a single test result, and to understand the bigger picture of your heart and metabolic health.  
*The laboratory's reference range for this biomarker is considered the optimal range and is reflected accordingly. The ranges charted alongside your results are provided by the laboratory. These are statistically derived from general populations in the United States, which some consider to be an inherently unhealthy benchmark for comparison. However, for some biomarkers, the laboratory reference range and the optimal reference range are the same, such as calcium or sodium. In contrast, the optimal ranges listed directly above are established by medical doctors with their own clinical experience and the scientific literature. Optimal ranges are often narrower and meant to identify patterns of a disease before it manifests. It is important to monitor changes in your results over time for a reliable understanding of your health.*

#### Summary

A below-range LDL peak size means that the most common LDL particles in the blood are small and dense. This LDL pattern is known as “pattern B” and is linked to a higher risk for heart disease. Small, dense LDL particles are more likely to slip into the lining of the blood vessels, become oxidized, and trigger inflammation, thus setting the stage for plaque buildup and atherosclerosis. This pattern is often seen in people with insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, or high triglycerides. A low LDL peak size can reveal hidden risks that typical cholesterol tests might miss.

A lower LDL peak size reflects a shift in how the body is processing and packaging cholesterol, often driven by inflammation, poor blood sugar control, or early metabolic changes. These smaller particles are more prone to damage by oxidative stress, which makes them more likely to stick to artery walls and cause inflammation. Over time, it can increase the risk for heart disease, especially if other markers like LDL particle number and ApoB are also elevated. A lower LDL peak size signals the need to address the root causes, such as poor diet (especially excess intake of refined carbs and added sugars), sedentary lifestyle, chronic stress, or hidden inflammation, to restore long-term cardiometabolic health.

When LDL peak size is low, it’s essential to dig deeper by evaluating further for the number and quality of LDL particles to get a more accurate picture of cardiovascular health.

* LDL particle number: LDL particle number measures the actual number of LDL cholesterol particles in the blood. A high LDL particle count means there are more vehicles carrying cholesterol, increasing the chances that they will penetrate artery walls and contribute to plaque buildup, even if the total LDL cholesterol looks normal. LDL particle number is often a better predictor of cardiovascular events than LDL alone.  
* LDL particle size: Identifies whether LDL particles are small and dense (more harmful) or large and fluffy (less harmful). Typically, a low LDL peak size is associated with smaller and more dense LDL particles.  
* LDL pattern: Classifies LDL particles as pattern A (large, buoyant, and less risky LDL) or pattern B (smaller, denser, and higher risk for atherosclerosis). Typically, a low LDL peak size is associated with pattern B.

Root cause approach

A root cause approach to addressing a lower LDL peak size involves identifying and managing the underlying contributors, such as chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, poor blood sugar control, insulin resistance, or genetic predispositions, while promoting protective elements like antioxidants, essential nutrients, quality sleep, and stress management. By addressing these underlying causes, rather than just managing symptoms, a root cause approach seeks to restore the body’s natural ability to regulate lipid metabolism and promote long-term health and vitality. Greater detail on diet, lifestyle, and other considerations is provided in the sections that follow, but a general overview includes:

* Nutrition: A heart-healthy, Mediterranean or anti-inflammatory diet is key to managing elevated LDL small with associated low LDL peak size. Focus on increasing healthy fats (from sources like extra virgin olive oil, avocados, fatty fish, flaxseeds, and walnuts), fiber-rich foods (from fruits, vegetables, and whole grains), while avoiding refined carbohydrates, trans fats, and ultra-processed foods.  
* Lifestyle modifications: Engaging in regular aerobic and strength-training exercises can help improve overall lipid profiles. Aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate exercise weekly, paired with stress-reduction techniques such as yoga, breathwork, meditation, or other mindfulness practices.  
* Weight management: If living with excess weight or obesity, gradual, sustainable weight loss can reduce insulin resistance and lower cardiovascular risk.  
* Liver support: The liver is responsible for cholesterol homeostasis. Supporting liver function by avoiding overconsumption of alcohol and including liver-supportive nutrients like choline and antioxidants like glutathione, alpha-lipoic acid (ALA), and milk thistle (silymarin) can help improve cholesterol metabolism.  
* Gut health support: Supporting gut health with probiotics, prebiotics, fermented foods (e.g., kefir, sauerkraut), and a diet high in fiber can reduce inflammation and improve lipid metabolism.  
* Targeted supplementation: Omega-3 fatty acids, CoQ10, and antioxidants can help reduce oxidative stress and inflammation, and support cardiovascular health.  
* Pharmaceutical interventions: If diet and lifestyle changes are not enough, pharmacological interventions with lipid-modifying medications such as statins, ezetimibe, or PCSK9 inhibitors may be beneficial, as deemed necessary by a clinician especially for high-risk individuals.  
* Metabolic health: Insulin resistance is closely linked to a low LDL peak size. Regular monitoring of fasting glucose, insulin, and HbA1c levels, particularly if living with insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes or metabolic syndrome, ensures appropriate glycemic control and cardiometabolic health.  
* Follow-up testing: Follow-up tests may include LDL particle number, LDL small, LDL pattern, triglycerides, HDL, apolipoprotein B (the main protein found on LDL particles), inflammatory markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and homocysteine, and lipoprotein(a) to better assess cardiovascular risk.

#### Causes

Root causes of a low LDL peak size

A low LDL peak size often reflects deeper imbalances in the body, like inflammation, insulin resistance, poor liver function, or hidden toxin exposures. Understanding these root causes helps address the *why* behind the number, not just lower it.

Diet and lifestyle factors

Here are common diet and lifestyle factors that can contribute to a low LDL peak size:

* Poor diet: Eating a diet high in saturated and trans fats, particularly from processed foods, can promote inflammation in blood vessels. Additionally, consuming refined carbohydrates and added sugar drives insulin resistance.  
* Physical inactivity: Lack of regular physical activity can lower HDL and raise triglycerides, which can shift LDL particles into more atherogenic (plaque-forming) forms.  
* Chronic stress: Long-term stress or hormonal shifts (like during menopause) can affect lipid levels by increasing cortisol and disrupting fat metabolism.

Other factors

* Insulin resistance: Insulin resistance is an important driver of elevated LDL peak size. The average person now consumes over 150 pounds of sugar each year, mostly through ultra-processed foods, which causes repeated spikes in blood sugar, which then forces the pancreas to release large amounts of insulin. Over time, chronically high levels of both sugar and insulin overwhelm the system, causing cells to stop responding properly. This triggers a vicious cycle: the body pumps out even more insulin, leading to increased belly fat, inflammation, and a surge in small, dense LDL particles that raises cardiovascular risk.  
* Oxidative stress: Oxidative stress plays a major role in driving a low LDL peak size and worsening its effects on the body. It happens when there’s an imbalance between free radicals (unstable molecules that can damage cells) and the body’s ability to neutralize them with antioxidants. When these particles are exposed to oxidative stress, they become oxidized, making them even more harmful. Oxidized LDL is more likely to trigger inflammation in the arteries, drive foam cell (cholesterol-filled immune cells) formation, making them more likely to stick to artery walls, resulting in plaque buildup.  
* Chronic inflammation: Inflammation from hidden sources like stress, infections, or poor sleep can disrupt cholesterol metabolism.  
* Gut dysbiosis: Poor gut health or dysbiosis (an imbalance of gut bacteria) can increase inflammation, impair bile acid metabolism, and reduce cholesterol excretion.  
* Obesity: Carrying excess abdominal fat (visceral fat) contributes to insulin resistance and increases cardiovascular risk.  
* Genetics: Inherited conditions like familial hypercholesterolemia can cause the body to produce or retain too much LDL cholesterol regardless of lifestyle.  
* Hypothyroidism: An underactive thyroid slows metabolism, which can lead to higher LDL levels.  
* Liver dysfunction: Because the liver plays a key role in cholesterol metabolism and helps clear cholesterol from the blood, dysfunction can lead to buildup.

Environmental toxin exposure

​Exposure to certain environmental toxins has been shown to directly elevate LDL cholesterol levels.

* Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS): PFAS is a “forever chemical” commonly found in consumer products, including non-stick cookware, water-repellent fabrics, and food packaging.​ Higher plasma levels of PFAS are associated with higher levels of LDL.  
* Air pollution: Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is a component of air pollution from vehicle emissions, industrial processes, and wildfires.​ Long-term exposure to PM2.5 has been linked to elevated LDL cholesterol.​

Reducing exposure to these toxins may be a valuable component of strategies aimed at maintaining healthy cholesterol levels and reducing cardiovascular risk.

For more information, visit [Environmental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/environmental-health-101).

Substance use

Substance use, particularly cigarette smoking, creates an internal environment that promotes the formation of small, dense LDL particles that are more likely to cause artery damage. Smoking directly increases oxidative stress by flooding the body with free radicals that shrink and damage LDL particles, making them more likely to trigger inflammation and stick to artery walls, ultimately resulting in plaque buildup. Even in people with normal cholesterol, smoking has been shown to worsen LDL quality and raise cardiovascular risk.​

#### Associated conditions

General conditions and diseases

Having a LDL peak size below the normal range is linked to a higher risk of several chronic conditions, especially those involving the heart and blood vessels. Over time, a low LDL peak size can contribute to plaque buildup in the arteries (atherosclerosis), which narrows them and limits healthy blood flow. Detecting and managing it early can help prevent these more serious outcomes.

Here are some conditions associated with a low LDL peak size:

* Atherogenic dyslipidemia: Characterized by elevated LDL, high triglycerides, and low HDL cholesterol. This combination increases the risk of plaque buildup in the arteries (atherosclerosis), leading to heart disease and stroke. It's commonly seen in people with insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, or type 2 diabetes.  
* Coronary artery disease (CAD): Caused by plaque buildup in the arteries that supply the heart, leading to chest pain, heart attacks, or heart failure.  
* Stroke: A result of reduced or blocked blood flow to the brain, often due to plaque or a blood clot from narrowed arteries.  
* Peripheral artery disease (PAD): A condition where narrowed arteries reduce blood flow to the legs or arms, causing pain, numbness, or even the risk of amputation.  
* Carotid artery disease: Narrowing of the arteries in the neck that supply blood to the brain, increasing stroke risk.  
* Type 2 diabetes: A low LDL peak size may appear alongside insulin resistance, eventually leading to higher blood sugar and the development of diabetes.  
* Metabolic syndrome: This is a cluster of risk factors, including high blood pressure, high blood sugar, excess belly fat, and abnormal cholesterol levels, that raise the risk for heart disease, stroke, and diabetes.  
* Chronic inflammation: Small, dense LDL particles are more prone to becoming oxidized, which can trigger a chronic, low-grade inflammatory response that damages tissues over time.

Mental health conditions

While a low LDL peak size is mainly linked to heart disease, it may also play a role in certain mental health conditions. This connection is likely due to the effects of chronic inflammation, poor circulation, and metabolic imbalances on the brain. When arteries become narrowed or stiff from plaque buildup, blood flow to the brain can be reduced, which may impact mood, memory, and cognitive performance over time.

Some mental health conditions that may be associated with a low LDL peak size include:

* Cognitive decline, dementia, and Alzheimer’s disease: Reduced blood flow and inflammation can damage brain cells, increasing the risk of memory loss and conditions like Alzheimer’s disease.  
* Depression: Imbalances in cholesterol may affect the production of brain chemicals like serotonin and disrupt hormone levels, contributing to low mood and emotional instability.

A root cause approach to diagnosing a lower LDL peak size looks beyond the number to uncover deeper imbalances like inflammation and oxidative stress, insulin resistance, liver dysfunction, or toxin exposure that may be driving the elevation.

The need for specific tests depends on your individual risk profile, clinical symptoms, family history, and the presence of other health conditions, as determined by your healthcare provider. *Here is a list of tests that may be relevant if LDL peak size levels are below range, with those available through your Function membership noted below.*

Blood testing

A range of blood tests can help evaluate a low LDL peak size through a root cause lens by giving a fuller picture of what's happening inside the body and helping uncover reasons behind these smaller, denser LDL particles.

* Apolipoprotein B (ApoB): Measures atherogenic (plaque-forming) particles in the blood, which is a more accurate predictor of heart risk than LDL peak size alone.  
* LDL particle size: Identifies whether LDL particles are small and dense (more harmful) or large and fluffy (less harmful). With a low LDL peak size, smaller and denser particles are more likely to be present.  
* LDL particle number: LDL particle number measures the actual number of LDL cholesterol particles in the blood.  
* LDL pattern: Classifies LDL particles as pattern A (large, buoyant, and less risky LDL) or pattern B (smaller, denser, and higher risk for atherosclerosis). With a low LDL peak size, pattern B is more likely.  
* Oxidized LDL (oxLDL): Direct measurement of oxidized LDL to evaluate the degree of oxidative stress on cholesterol.  
* Lipoprotein(a) \[Lp(a)\]: A genetic form of LDL that increases cardiovascular risk regardless of other cholesterol levels.  
* Fasting glucose and insulin: Elevated insulin or blood sugar can point to insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome, which is often seen alongside a low LDL peak size.  
* Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c): Shows average blood sugar levels over the past 2-3 months and helps reveal hidden metabolic issues.  
* Insulin resistance score: The insulin resistance score is a valuable tool for assessing insulin resistance (IR) and identifying the risk of prediabetes and diabetes. It combines measurements of insulin and C-peptide from a single fasting blood sample, offering insights into how well the body processes glucose and insulin​. This score is especially useful for detecting IR in individuals who may have normal glucose and HbA1c levels but are still at risk due to underlying metabolic issues​.  
* Liver function: Assesses how well the liver is processing fats and cholesterol.  
* Thyroid function: An underactive thyroid can slow metabolism and lead to higher levels of LDL.

Urine testing

* Urinalysis: A general urine test can reveal signs of metabolic or kidney dysfunction, which are often linked to poor cholesterol profiles.  
* Microalbumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR): Assesses early kidney damage. A high ACR can be a marker of endothelial dysfunction, a condition where the inner lining of blood vessels is damaged, potentially increasing the risk of cardiovascular disease.  
* 24-hour urine cortisol: Elevated cortisol can contribute to impaired lipid metabolism.  
* Urinary 8-isoprostanes: Measures a specific type of oxidative damage called lipid peroxidation, which tells how much damage free radicals are causing to LDL cholesterol particles that are small and dense. High levels of 8-isoprostanes in urine suggest that lipid peroxidation is happening at a higher rate, which may help explain why LDL peak size is lower.

Diagnostic imaging

Imaging can be a useful tool to see the impact of a low LDL peak size, causing damage in the body over time.

* Heart CT Scan with Gated CAC: Measures calcium buildup in the coronary arteries and calculates the coronary artery calcium (CAC) score to assess long-term heart disease risk. The images are timed with your heartbeat to avoid blurriness from the heart’s normal movement and rhythm.  
* Carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT) ultrasound: A noninvasive ultrasound scan that measures the thickness of the carotid artery's inner layers. Helps detect early signs of atherosclerosis in the carotid arteries (major blood vessels in the neck that supply blood to the brain), before symptoms appear and assess future stroke risk. \-AI-enhanced coronary CT angiogram: This CT scan gives a clearer picture of both calcified and soft plaques in the coronary arteries. The CT angiogram is AI-enhanced for more precise evaluation of total plaque burden, location, distribution, and rupture risk.  
* Echocardiogram (ECHO): An ECHO can help evaluate the structure and function of the heart.  
* Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI): An MRI can be used to assess plaque in arteries and the health of the heart muscle.

Nutrient imbalances

* Vitamin D (25-hydroxy): Low vitamin D is linked to inflammation and poor cardiovascular outcomes.  
* Omega-3 index: Measures the percentage of EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid) and DHA (docosahexaenoic acid), two types of omega-3 fatty acids, in red blood cell membranes; low levels can contribute to inflammation and cholesterol imbalance.  
* Magnesium (RBC): Low magnesium is often found in people with metabolic issues.  
* Homocysteine: High levels may reflect poor methylation and can damage the lining of arteries and promote the formation of plaques, especially when LDL peak size is lower.  
* Vitamin B12 and folate (B9): These B vitamins are essential for methylation and reducing homocysteine levels.  
* Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10): A key antioxidant needed for heart and mitochondrial health; low levels may impair lipid metabolism and are especially important for those on statins.  
* Zinc and copper: An imbalance may reflect oxidative stress or poor antioxidant defense, which can worsen LDL oxidation.  
* Vitamin C: Deficiency impairs antioxidant defense, leading to oxidative stress.  
* Vitamin E: Insufficient levels decrease protection against lipid peroxidation (oxidative damage of fats in cell membranes triggered by free radicals).

Microbiome and gut health assessment

The microbiome plays a key role in inflammation, metabolism, and how the body processes cholesterol. Assessing gut health in the context of a low LDL peak size can be beneficial.

* Comprehensive stool analysis: Assesses the balance between beneficial and harmful bacteria in the gut. Looks for markers of dysbiosis, yeast overgrowth, or parasitic infections. Stool testing can also evaluate gut permeability ("leaky gut") by measuring zonulin levels, which indicate whether inflammatory triggers are escaping the gut and contributing to systemic inflammation and worsening damage to blood vessels.

Immune system evaluation

Evaluating the integrity of the immune system can uncover hidden inflammation or immune imbalances that may be increasing risk alongside a lower LDL peak size.

* High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP): An inflammatory marker that detects low-grade inflammation, which, in combination with a low LDL peak size, increases cardiovascular risk.  
* Other inflammatory markers: Measures markers such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and pro-inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), to gauge the level of systemic inflammation that may be driving damage to blood vessels.  
* Food sensitivity testing (IgG): Delayed immune reactions to certain foods can cause chronic inflammation.  
* Autoimmunity: Screens for autoimmune activity that may signal the immune system is mistakenly attacking healthy tissues and increasing vascular inflammation.

Environmental and toxin testing

Testing for environmental toxins is not meant for everyone, but in cases of a low LDL peak size, testing for certain environmental toxins can help uncover hidden drivers of inflammation and oxidative stress.

* PFAS: Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances interfere with liver function and fat metabolism.  
* Heavy metals: Evaluates for metals like lead, arsenic, and mercury, which are linked to lipid abnormalities and oxidative stress.  
* Mycotoxins (urine): Detects mold toxins that may cause inflammation, oxidative stress, and burden the liver.

Advanced diagnostics

* pCAD score: Evaluates how effectively HDL protects against the risk of premature coronary artery disease (pCAD).  
* pCEC (percent cholesterol efflux capacity): Measures how efficiently HDL removes cholesterol from artery walls and lowers the risk of plaque buildup (atherosclerosis) in the blood vessels of the heart.  
* Cholesterol balance testing: Cholesterol balance testing helps uncover the source of high cholesterol, whether high cholesterol is coming from internal production (synthesis) or external absorption (from food and bile reabsorption).  
* Plasma malondialdehyde (MDA): A byproduct of lipid peroxidation, elevated MDA indicates damage to cell membranes from free radicals.  
* Organic acids testing (OAT): Organic acid tests can detect imbalances in microbial byproducts, mitochondrial dysfunction, and nutritional deficiencies that indicate dysbiosis and systemic oxidative stress.  
* Genetic testing: Identifies increased genetic risk for various cardiovascular diseases such as early-onset myocardial infarction (MI or heart attacks), coronary artery disease (CAD), atrial fibrillation (AF), and strokes.

Cognitive assessments

Low LDL peak size, with a dominance of small, dense LDL particles, may impact cognitive function because these particles are more likely to become oxidized and trigger inflammation, including in the blood vessels that supply the brain. Over time, this can reduce blood flow and impair nutrient delivery to brain cells, leading to memory issues, slower thinking, and even a higher risk for vascular dementia. Cognitive testing can be instrumental in detecting early signs of cognitive decline associated with a lower LDL peak size. By assessing memory, attention, and executive function, these tests can help identify subtle changes in cognitive performance, allowing for timely interventions to manage cholesterol levels and potentially mitigate further cognitive deterioration.

#### Follow up

Medical interventions

Collaboration with your healthcare provider ensures a personalized approach to managing a low LDL peak size. Consideration of all symptoms, laboratory test results, and lifestyle factors can guide a treatment plan tailored to your specific needs and health goals.

Medications

When LDL peak size is lower, especially in combination with other risk factors (e.g., diabetes), clinicians may prescribe medications to help lower LDL levels and reduce the risk of conditions like atherosclerosis, heart attack, and stroke. Once the root cause, such as insulin resistance or an inherited condition like familial hypercholesterolemia, has been identified, treatment can be tailored to individual needs. If you have existing cardiovascular disease, significant soft plaque seen on an AI CT angiogram, or an abnormal carotid ultrasound (CIMT), medications are often necessary to lower your risk, even alongside lifestyle changes. Here are some medications commonly prescribed:

* Statins (e.g., rosuvastatin, atorvastatin, simvastatin): Statins are used for high LDL, atherosclerosis, coronary artery disease, and stroke prevention. They reduce LDL cholesterol and triglycerides while modestly increasing HDL levels. They also lower inflammation and stabilize arterial plaques. Most people on statins today fall under “primary prevention,” meaning they have high cholesterol but no history of heart disease. While there’s modest benefit for some, particularly those with multiple risk factors or a high 10-year heart disease risk, it’s important to weigh the benefit against potential side effects.  
* Ezetimibe: Ezetimibe inhibits cholesterol absorption in the intestines and is often used in combination with statins to reduce cardiovascular risk.  
* PCSK9 inhibitors (e.g., alirocumab, evolocumab): PCSK9 inhibitors prevent the degradation of LDL receptors, thus allowing the liver to remove more LDL from the blood.  
* Fibrates (e.g., fenofibrate, gemfibrozil): Primarily used to treat high triglycerides and metabolic syndrome, but sometimes combined with other medications to address high LDL or low HDL.  
* Omega-3 fatty acids (e.g., icosapent ethyl): Prescription-strength omega-3 fatty acids lower triglycerides and have added benefits in reducing cardiovascular events.  
* Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors (e.g., empagliflozin): SGLT2 inhibitors reduce cardiovascular risk by lowering blood glucose, promoting weight loss, and reducing inflammation.  
* Thiazolidinediones (TZDs, e.g., pioglitazone): Used to treat type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome. TZDs improve insulin sensitivity and increase HDL levels by activating PPAR-γ receptors, which influence lipid and glucose metabolism.  
* GLP-1 receptor agonists (e.g., liraglutide): GLP-1 receptor agonists improve insulin sensitivity, aid in weight loss, and may modestly raise HDL cholesterol while reducing LDL and triglycerides.

For individuals with inherited lipid disorders, medication is often essential. Statins, PCSK9 inhibitors, and cholesterol absorption blockers such as ezetimibe may be needed. That said, aggressive lifestyle changes through food, movement, and metabolic support can still play a powerful role in improving lipid levels and, in some cases, reduce or delay the need for long-term medication.

Procedures

When a low LDL peak size contributes to serious cardiovascular disease, and lifestyle or medications aren't enough to reduce risk, medical procedures or surgeries may be necessary to restore blood flow or prevent life-threatening complications. These interventions focus on treating blocked or damaged arteries caused by atherosclerosis—a common consequence of persistently elevated small, dense LDL particles.

* Coronary angioplasty and stenting (also known as percutaneous coronary intervention or PCI): A catheter with a balloon is inserted into narrowed heart arteries and inflated to widen them; a stent (small mesh tube) is often placed to keep the artery open. This procedure helps restore blood flow to the heart and relieve chest pain or prevent a heart attack caused by plaque buildup.  
* Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG): CABG reroutes blood flow around blocked coronary arteries, and helps prevent heart failure or further cardiovascular events.  
* Carotid endarterectomy: Carotid endarterectomy removes plaque from carotid arteries to restore blood flow to the brain and reduce stroke risk in those with significant atherosclerosis.  
* Endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR): EVAR places a stent graft in the aorta to prevent aneurysm rupture related to vascular disease.  
* Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR): TAVR replaces a narrowed aortic valve to improve heart function, particularly in those with high cardiovascular risk.  
* Heart transplant: A heart transplant may be necessary for end-stage heart failure caused by severe cardiovascular complications.

Retesting

Retesting LDL peak size is a key part of assessing how well your management plan is working. Pairing these results with inflammation and metabolic markers can give you the clearest picture of true progress. Here are the guidelines that can help decide when and how often to retest:

* 4-12 weeks after starting or changing a treatment plan: This is the typical window to evaluate how effective a new strategy, like dietary changes, medications, or supplements, has been in increasing LDL peak size and improving the overall lipid status.  
* Every 6-12 months for long-term monitoring: If LDL peak size and related markers are in a healthy range and the plan is working, annual follow-up testing helps ensure things stay on track and catch early signs of change.  
* Earlier retesting (within 4-8 weeks): May be warranted if there has been a significant change in medication or supplement dosage or new symptoms develop (e.g., fatigue, chest discomfort, brain fog).

#### Why it matters

Glucose, often called blood sugar, is the body’s main fuel for the brain, muscles, and organs, with fat serving as a backup energy source. After eating, carbohydrates break down into glucose, which enters the bloodstream and triggers insulin release from the pancreas to help cells absorb it. If glucose levels fall, the hormone glucagon is released and helps mobilize stored glycogen from the liver and muscles. Meanwhile, excess glucose from consuming more carbs than the body needs is converted by the liver into triglycerides and stored as visceral (belly) fat. In a well‑tuned system, a person experiences steady energy and mental clarity. But when insulin response falters or when many refined carbs are consumed at once, blood sugar can swing too high (hyperglycemia) or too low (hypoglycemia), leading to fatigue, mood swings, and over time, damage to blood vessels and nerves.  
Measuring blood glucose alongside related markers like insulin, hemoglobin A1c (which shows average blood sugar over the past few months), and C-peptide (which reflects how much insulin the body produces) gives a fuller picture of how well the body manages sugar. These tests help detect early signs of insulin resistance, a condition where cells don’t respond properly to insulin. The good news is that catching this early means targeted lifestyle changes can be made—like adjusting diet, increasing movement, managing stress, and improving sleep—to prevent or manage conditions such as type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome. Together, these biomarkers guide informed decisions to maintain metabolic health and avoid complications.

* Low blood glucose (hypoglycemia) may occur with skipped meals, excessive insulin or medication doses, or hormonal imbalances, causing shakiness, sweating, confusion, and in severe cases, fainting.  
* Elevated blood glucose (hyperglycemia) often points to insulin resistance or diabetes and can lead to symptoms like excessive thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, and over time, damage to blood vessels and nerves.

Monitoring blood glucose patterns helps identify causes of imbalances such as a high-sugar diet, chronic stress, or poor sleep, enabling personalized strategies to keep energy balanced and the body healthy.

What makes this biomarker fluctuate  
Blood glucose levels naturally rise and fall throughout the day in response to factors like what and when you eat, how much you move, and even your stress and sleep patterns. For example, a meal high in refined carbohydrates can cause a rapid spike in blood sugar, while delaying or skipping meals can lead to low readings later on. Stress triggers hormones like cortisol that can raise blood sugar, and poor or irregular sleep may further disrupt insulin sensitivity. Medications, illness, and hormonal changes (for example, during the menstrual cycle) can also shift glucose up or down. Because of these day‑to‑day influences, a single blood glucose test gives only a moment‑in‑time snapshot; tracking patterns over days or weeks offers a more complete picture of how your body is handling sugar and where targeted lifestyle tweaks might help stabilize energy and metabolic health.

#### Causes

Root causes of elevated glucose levels

High blood glucose often reflects insulin resistance and impaired glucose uptake driven by factors such as excess intake of refined carbohydrates, chronic inflammation, and sedentary habits. Underlying contributors may include abdominal (visceral) fat accumulation, imbalances in the gut microbiota (dysbiosis), stress‑related hormonal disruption, and genetic predispositions that interfere with normal insulin signaling and glucose metabolism.

Diet and lifestyle factors

* Dietary choices: Consuming a diet high in refined sugars, processed foods, and refined carbohydrates can lead to increased blood sugar levels. Frequent intake of sugary drinks and snacks contributes to poor blood sugar control.  
* Chronic stress: Persistent stress can lead to hormonal imbalances, including elevated cortisol levels, which can affect blood sugar levels and insulin sensitivity.  
* Physical Inactivity: Lack of regular exercise can impair the body’s ability to regulate blood sugar effectively. Physical activity helps improve insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism.  
* Poor sleep: Poor sleep quality or insufficient sleep can disrupt glucose metabolism and contribute to insulin resistance, impacting blood sugar control.  
* Excess caloric intake: Consuming more calories than needed, especially from nutrient-poor sources, can lead to weight gain and worsen insulin resistance.  
* Low fiber intake: Diets low in fiber may result in rapid post-meal glucose spikes and poor overall blood sugar control.  
* Dehydration: Concentrates blood sugar levels and reduces insulin efficiency.

Other factors

* Gut health imbalances: Poor gut health can affect nutrient absorption and increase inflammation, contributing to elevated blood sugar levels. High-sugar diets reduce beneficial bacteria (e.g., *Akkermansia*), worsening insulin resistance.  
* Chronic inflammation: Persistent inflammation in the body can disrupt insulin signaling and potentially lead to increased blood sugar levels over time.  
* Oxidative stress: An imbalance between free radicals and antioxidants may damage cells and impair glucose metabolism.  
* Insulin resistance/metabolic syndrome: When the body becomes less sensitive to insulin, it struggles to regulate blood sugar.  
* Obesity: Being overweight significantly impacts the way that the body processes blood sugar and fatty acids, which can lead to insulin resistance. Obesity is also associated with chronic inflammation, another risk factor for high glucose levels.  
* Genetics: Family history and genetic predispositions can influence how the body handles glucose, potentially leading to elevated glucose despite similar lifestyle factors.

Medications

Some prescription medications may increase insulin resistance or alter glucose metabolism, resulting in elevated glucose levels, including:

* Glucocorticoids (steroids): Medications such as prednisone, dexamethasone, and hydrocortisone can increase insulin resistance and elevate blood sugar levels.  
* Atypical antipsychotics: Drugs like olanzapine, clozapine, quetiapine, and risperidone are associated with weight gain and impaired glucose metabolism.  
* Thiazide diuretics: These medications, like hydrochlorothiazide and chlorthalidone, often used for hypertension, can affect carbohydrate metabolism and raise blood glucose levels.  
* Protease inhibitors (HIV treatment): These medications, including lopinavir and ritonavir, are toxic to pancreatic B-cells, disrupting insulin signaling. They also release free fatty acids that impair glucose uptake.  
* Calcineurin inhibitors: Medications like tacrolimus, cyclosporine are immunosuppressants used after organ transplants and autoimmune conditions. They may reduce insulin secretion by damaging pancreatic β-cells, leading to elevated blood sugars.  
* Niacin (high‑dose): Used for managing cholesterol, it can trigger high blood sugar by releasing fatty acids that the liver turns into extra glucose and lowering adiponectin, a hormone that helps insulin work.  
* β-Blockers (non-cardioselective): Includes propranolol and metoprolol; they can raise blood sugar by blocking β₂-adrenergic receptors in the pancreas, inhibiting insulin release.

Environmental toxin exposure

Certain environmental toxins can contribute to elevated glucose levels by disrupting metabolic processes and affecting blood sugar regulation.

* Air pollution (e.g., car exhaust, industrial emissions): Particulate matter in air pollution causes inflammation and oxidative stress, which can impair how cells use glucose and reduce insulin sensitivity, contributing to elevated glucose levels.  
* Heavy metals (lead, mercury, cadmium, manganese, nickel, arsenic): Promote oxidative stress, damaging cells and worsening insulin sensitivity, leading to unstable blood sugar.  
* Plastic chemicals (BPA, phthalates): Act like hormones, interfering with insulin signaling and glucose metabolism, contributing to insulin resistance.  
* PFAS (e.g., PFOS, PFOA): These “forever chemicals" found in nonstick cookware, household products, and waterproof gear disrupt pancreatic beta-cell function, reducing insulin production and increasing glucose levels and diabetes risk.  
* Volatile organic chemicals (VOCs like benzene, toluene): These chemicals found in industrial solvents and vehicle fumes damage mitochondria (the cell’s powerhouse), impairing glucose metabolism and elevating diabetes risk.  
* Pesticides (organophosphates, glyphosate): Disrupt hormone function, reducing insulin secretion, impairing glucose regulation, and increasing diabetes risk.

For more information, visit [Environmental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/environmental-health-101).

Substance use

Substance use can elevate glucose levels through several mechanisms, impacting blood sugar control and overall metabolic health.

* Stimulants: Substances like cocaine, methamphetamines, caffeine, or prescription stimulants can first spike blood sugar by triggering stress hormones, then later lower it by cutting appetite and food intake, making blood sugar control more unpredictable.  
* Cigarette smoking: Smoking can contribute to insulin resistance, making it more difficult for the body to regulate blood sugar effectively and leading to higher average blood sugar levels. In addition, nicotine activates stress hormones (cortisol), impairing glucose uptake and elevating fasting glucose.  
* Opioids (heroin, prescription painkillers): Long‑term use may promote insulin resistance, making it harder for cells to absorb glucose. These can also damage the pancreas’ insulin‑producing cells, reducing hormone release and destabilizing blood sugar control.

#### Associated conditions

General conditions and diseases

Elevated glucose levels can harm blood vessels and nerves and lead to serious health issues. These high levels are associated with an increased risk for several conditions, including:

* Insulin resistance: Having insulin resistance is a risk factor for developing high levels of blood glucose because it is associated with blood sugar imbalance.  
* Type 2 diabetes: High glucose levels are a primary indicator of type 2 diabetes, a condition where the body becomes resistant to insulin or does not produce enough insulin to maintain normal blood sugar levels.  
* Metabolic syndrome: This is a cluster of conditions including high blood pressure, high blood sugar, excess abdominal fat, and abnormal cholesterol levels, which together increase the risk of heart disease and type 2 diabetes.  
* Cardiovascular disease: Chronic high blood sugar can damage blood vessels and nerves, increasing the risk of cardiovascular diseases such as heart attacks and strokes.  
* Chronic kidney disease: Over time, high blood glucose can damage the kidneys, potentially leading to diabetic nephropathy and chronic kidney disease.  
* Eye problems: Uncontrolled blood sugar can lead to conditions such as diabetic retinopathy and vision loss.  
* Hormonal imbalances: Conditions like polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) are often linked to blood sugar imbalances, affecting overall hormonal health.  
* Cognitive decline: Chronic high blood sugar may damage brain vessels, contributing to memory problems and a higher risk of dementia. Long term imbalances in blood glucose may impair how the brain uses energy, contribute to inflammation, and promote the buildup of abnormal proteins associated with dementia, increasing the risk of cognitive decline and dementia, including Alzheimer’s disease.  
* Impaired wound healing: High levels of blood glucose and associated uncontrolled blood sugar levels or diabetes can contribute to impaired wound healing. Impaired wound healing, especially for wounds to the feet or lower legs, can eventually lead to infection and possible amputation.  
* Nerve damage: Unstable blood sugar can cause peripheral neuropathy, leading to symptoms such as pain, tingling, or numbness in the legs. Some people with peripheral neuropathy develop sores on their legs or feet.  
* Non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD): Insulin resistance and chronic high blood sugar can contribute to fat buildup in the liver, which may progress to liver inflammation or scarring.  
* Inflammatory conditions: Elevated blood sugar is associated with increased inflammation, which can contribute to a range of chronic conditions, including certain autoimmune disorders and, in some cases, a higher risk of cancer.  
* Sleep apnea: Insulin resistance worsens sleep apnea, which further disrupts glucose metabolism.

Mental health conditions

Elevated glucose levels are associated with several mental health conditions due to the impact of chronic high blood sugar on brain function and overall well-being. Some of these conditions include:

* Anxiety: Elevated glucose, particularly in type 2 diabetes, is linked with a higher risk of anxiety.  
* Depression: High glucose levels are associated with an increased risk of depression, which can further affect blood sugar control.  
* Mental health disorders: Some medications for conditions like schizophrenia or bipolar disorder may raise glucose levels.  
* Mood swings: Fluctuating blood sugar levels can lead to mood swings and irritability.

#### Foods to eat

Nutrition plays a key role in overall health. [Nutrition 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/nutrition-101) is a science-based guide to key nutritional principles and eating practices that promote health and well-being. It is founded on three core principles:

* Food as medicine: Beyond the benefits of balanced protein, fat, and carbohydrates, fiber, phytonutrients, vitamins, and minerals also support the functioning of every cellular process in the body.  
* Personalization: Food choices should be optimized based on age, sex, genetics, preferences, health conditions, and individual goals.  
* Whole real food: A foundation of real, whole foods, or minimally processed foods and avoiding ultra-processed foods is the true recipe to health.

Using food as medicine may help address high blood glucose by targeting underlying insulin resistance, inflammation, and gut health to support more stable sugar metabolism.

* Fiber-rich, low-glycemic foods: Consuming soluble fiber from sources like oats, beans, apples, and psyllium may slow sugar absorption and help reduce blood sugar spikes, with low-glycemic foods such as quinoa, lentils, and non-starchy vegetables supporting steadier glucose levels.  
* Anti-inflammatory eating patterns: Adopting a Mediterranean-style diet that features omega-3-rich foods like salmon and walnuts, olive oil as a primary fat, and antioxidant-packed fruits and leafy greens may improve blood sugar control by reducing inflammation.  
* Gut health support: Including probiotics from yogurt or kefir and prebiotic fibers from garlic, onions, and bananas may improve insulin sensitivity and promote a balanced microbiome, contributing to better glucose metabolism.  
* Meal composition: Meal composition and the order in which foods are eaten can greatly affect blood sugar after meals. Using a plate method that includes half non-starchy vegetables, one quarter regeneratively-raised organic protein, and one quarter whole food unrefined carbohydrates like whole grains, beans, seeds and and starchy veggies like sweet potatoes and butternut squash may help balance glucose levels, and beginning meals with vegetables and protein before carbohydrates can further reduce blood sugar spikes.  
* Time-restricted eating: Limiting food intake to an 8- to 10-hour window each day can help reduce average glucose by 0.1%-0.2% over three months, likely by aligning eating patterns with the body's natural circadian rhythms.

#### Foods to limit

Processed and unhealthy foods are a leading cause of many common chronic diseases. Certain foods can contribute to elevated glucose levels and should be limited or avoided.

* Added sugars: Consuming large amounts of sugar can increase blood sugar levels and contribute to insulin resistance over time. Foods high in added sugars include sodas, sweet teas, cookies, and candy.  
* High glycemic index foods: These foods are more likely to cause unwanted spikes in blood sugar after consumption. Examples include white rice, white bread, and potatoes.  
* Refined carbohydrates: Foods like white bread and pasta can quickly raise blood sugar levels, potentially contributing to insulin resistance over time.  
* Processed foods: These are often high in refined carbs, sugars, and unhealthy fats. They can cause blood sugar spikes and contribute to overall poor dietary habits.  
* Fried foods: These foods are often cooked in unhealthy fats and coated in carbohydrates, making them energy-dense and contributing to weight gain, inflammation, and dysregulated blood sugar control.

[Nutrition 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/nutrition-101) provides more information and insight into the benefits of using food as medicine.

#### Symptoms

High blood sugar levels can be associated with certain symptoms such as:

* Blurred vision: Difficulty seeing clearly.  
* Fatigue: Feeling unusually tired or weak.

#### Self care

Regular exercise, weight management, stress reduction, supportive mental health practices, and quality sleep may work together to enhance insulin sensitivity and stabilize blood sugar levels, ultimately contributing to more balanced glucose levels.

Exercise and movement

Moderate aerobic exercise, such as brisk walking or cycling, for about 150 minutes per week, may help lower elevated glucose by improving insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake. Resistance training two to three times per week can build muscle mass, further enhancing blood sugar regulation and contributing to healthier glucose levels.

High intensity interval training (HIT), which involves short bursts of intense exercise followed by recovery periods, can improve insulin sensitivity and boost muscle glucose uptake, thereby lowering elevated glucose levels. Overall, a combination of these various exercise approaches can play a key role in managing blood sugar levels and supporting metabolic health.

For more detailed information on exercise, please refer to [Exercise 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/exercise-101).

Weight management

For individuals with diabetes, being overweight is commonly linked to an elevated glucose because excess body fat, particularly around the abdomen, increases insulin resistance and leads to consistently high blood sugar levels. Maintaining a healthy weight reduces the amount of fat stored in the liver and muscles. This can help the body process sugar more efficiently and reduce the amount of sugar in the blood, leading to lower glucose levels. In individuals who are overweight, losing weight can help improve insulin sensitivity, making it easier for the cells to use glucose effectively.

Although being underweight is less frequently associated with high glucose, it may reflect poor nutrition or reduced muscle mass that impairs glucose uptake, which in some cases could contribute to elevated blood sugar levels.

Refer to [Weight Management 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/weight-management-101) for foundational principles for healthy weight management.

Sleep

Quality sleep plays an important role in glucose metabolism and blood sugar control. Sleep enhances insulin sensitivity and helps regulate hormones involved in glucose control. Conversely, sleep deprivation can lead to insulin resistance and impaired glucose tolerance.

Visit [Sleep 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/sleep-101) for practical tips on improving sleep.

Mental health and stress reduction

Stress significantly impacts metabolic health and can contribute to insulin resistance and higher blood sugars over time. Effective mental health and stress reduction practices may help lower cortisol levels, which can improve insulin sensitivity and stabilize blood sugar, ultimately contributing to healthier glucose levels. Additionally, techniques like mindfulness and cognitive-behavioral therapy can enhance emotional regulation and reduce systemic inflammation, supporting better metabolic balance.

For more information on mental health and stress management, visit [Mental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/mental-health-101) and [Stress Management 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/stress-management-101).

Detoxification

Lowering the body's toxic burden by minimizing contact with environmental toxins such as pesticides, plastics, and endocrine (hormone) disrupting chemicals (EDCs) can decrease chronic inflammation, which can impair insulin sensitivity. Detoxification practices may help lower blood sugar by easing the liver’s workload and reducing inflammation that drives insulin resistance. Supporting pathways that clear toxins—through botanicals like milk thistle, sulfur‑rich vegetables (such as broccoli or Brussels sprouts), and gentle sweating via sauna or dry brushing—can decrease oxidative stress and improve how the cells respond to insulin, making it easier to keep blood glucose in a healthy range. High-protein diets can reduce fat in the liver and improve insulin sensitivity. In addition, reducing free radical damage with antioxidant support—for instance, by including vitamin C-rich fruits like oranges or green tea in the diet—may help maintain stable metabolic processes and improve glucose control.

See [Environmental Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/environmental-health-101).

Gut health support

Imbalances in gut bacteria and a “leaky” intestinal lining can drive chronic inflammation and insulin resistance, making blood sugar harder to control. Supporting the microbiome with fiber‑rich prebiotic foods from sources like garlic, onions, and bananas, probiotic-rich foods such as yogurt, kefir, or fermented vegetables, targeted probiotics with strains like *Lactobacillus acidophilus, Bifidobacterium lactis, Lactobacillus casei,* and *Akkermansia muciniphila,* an anti-inflammatory diet, and practices that strengthen the gut barrier (like avoiding processed foods, alcohol, and other irritants, choosing whole, unprocessed foods, and minimizing chemicals) may improve metabolic signaling and help stabilize glucose levels.

See [Gut Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/gut-health-101).

Community and social support

Family, friends, and support groups help manage related to high blood glucose levels by reducing stress and promoting well-being. Individuals managing diabetes without social support are twice as likely to experience depression, so the empathy, encouragement, and shared experiences provided by close ones and support groups can ease the burden of diabetes management and foster a sense of community and empowerment.

#### Additional tests

Root cause approach to diagnosing elevated glucose

A root‑cause diagnostic approach to high blood glucose focuses on key tests—such as fasting insulin and C‑peptide, insulin resistance score, oral glucose tolerance, liver enzymes, and inflammatory markers—to reveal whether insulin resistance, insulin underproduction, liver dysfunction, or chronic inflammation is driving the elevated sugar levels.

The need for specific tests depends on your individual risk profile, clinical symptoms, family history, and the presence of other health conditions, as determined by your healthcare provider. *Here is a list of tests that may be relevant if glucose is out of range, with those available through your Function membership noted below.*

Blood testing

* Hemoglobin A1c: Shows average blood sugar over the past two to three months, providing a long‑term view of glucose control.  
* Fasting insulin: Checks circulating insulin to see if the body is overproducing insulin in response to high blood sugar.  
* C‑peptide: Measures the molecule released alongside insulin, indicating how much natural insulin the pancreas is making.  
* Insulin resistance score: Evaluates the risk of insulin resistance, helping assess how effectively cells respond to insulin.  
* LDL cholesterol: Elevated glucose is often associated with insulin resistance, and measuring LDL helps assess the risk of plaque buildup in arteries.  
* HDL cholesterol: Since high blood sugar can lower HDL levels, checking HDL gives insight into the body's capacity to remove excess cholesterol and protect against heart disease.  
* LDL particle size and number: Elevated glucose is linked to small, dense LDL particles that are more likely to cause cardiovascular issues, so assessing their size and number helps gauge risk.  
* Apolipoprotein B (ApoB): ApoB measures the number of cholesterol-carrying particles, and higher levels in the context of high glucose indicate an increased risk of heart disease.  
* Lipoprotein(a): Elevated glucose may coincide with dyslipidemia, and measuring lipoprotein(a) helps identify additional genetic risks for cardiovascular events.  
* Triglycerides: High glucose often correlates with elevated triglycerides, reflecting poor metabolic control and a greater likelihood of cardiovascular complications.  
* Uric acid: Checks for gout risk that can be associated with metabolic imbalances.  
* Leptin: Chronic high blood sugar levels may impair leptin signaling, which can exacerbate metabolic dysfunction and weight regulation challenges.  
* Liver function (AST, ALT, GGT): Evaluates liver health and fatty liver disease that can correspond with metabolic dysfunction; elevated levels signal liver stress.  
* Kidney function (BUN and creatinine): Assesses kidney function.  
* Thyroid panel (TSH, T3, T4, TPO antibodies): Evaluates thyroid function, which affects metabolism and overall health.  
* Sex hormones (DHEA-S, estradiol, progesterone, free testosterone, SHBG): Assesses hormonal balance that can influence metabolic processes.  
* Fibrinogen: Measures blood clotting risk; higher levels in individuals with elevated glucose suggest an increased risk of cardiovascular complications.  
* Adiponectin: Adiponectin testing helps assess metabolic dysfunction when glucose is elevated because low levels strongly correlate with insulin resistance. Adiponectin enhances insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake while suppressing liver glucose production, making it a key protective hormone against diabetes progression.

Urine testing

* Urine albumin: Measures protein leakage from the kidneys; elevated levels often occur in individuals with high glucose, indicating potential diabetic kidney damage.

Diagnostic imaging (CT, MRI, ultrasound, etc.)

* Liver ultrasound: Evaluates fatty liver disease, which is common in individuals with elevated glucose and insulin resistance.  
* Abdominal ultrasound: Assesses kidney structure to detect abnormalities that may be linked to high blood sugar-related kidney damage.  
* Heart CT Scan with Gated CAC: Measures calcium buildup in the coronary arteries and calculates the coronary artery calcium (CAC) score to assess long-term heart disease risk. The images are timed with your heartbeat to avoid blurriness from the heart’s normal movement and rhythm.  
* MRI Scan with Skeletal & Neurological Assessment: An MRI can be a useful tool to help assess overall fat distribution and visceral fat (abdominal fat stored around internal organs), particularly to look for evidence of fatty liver. The body composition assessment will measure the percentage of liver fat content.

Nutrient imbalances

* Vitamin D, 25-Hydroxy: Evaluates vitamin D levels, as deficiencies are common in metabolic syndrome and can contribute to poor glycemic control and elevated glucose.  
* Homocysteine: Measures homocysteine levels, with elevated values often indicating B vitamin deficiencies and increased inflammation, both of which are linked to metabolic syndrome and high glucose.  
* Ferritin: Assesses iron storage, where imbalances may signal underlying inflammation or iron dysregulation frequently associated with metabolic syndrome and elevated glucose.

Microbiome and gut health assessment

* Comprehensive stool analysis: Examines the balance of good and bad bacteria, digestive markers, and inflammation in the gut to help identify underlying issues that might affect nutrient absorption and overall metabolic function. Detects gut bacteria imbalances that may increase inflammation and weaken the gut lining, allowing toxins to enter the bloodstream and trigger insulin resistance, leading to higher glucose levels over time.  
* SIBO breath test: Detects bacterial overgrowth in the small intestine, which can disrupt digestion and nutrient uptake, providing important insights into gut health.  
* Celiac disease panel: This screening checks for gluten sensitivity or celiac disease, conditions that can damage the gut lining and impair nutrient absorption, indirectly influencing metabolic balance.

See [Gut Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/gut-health-101) for more information.

Immune system evaluation

* Inflammatory markers: Measures markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) to gauge the level of systemic inflammation and evaluate cardiovascular risks associated with metabolic imbalances.  
* Food sensitivity and allergy testing (IgG, IgE panels): Identifies food sensitivities or allergies that may contribute to inflammation or impact immune function that can impact metabolic balance.  
* Autoimmunity testing (ANA, rheumatoid factor, TPO antibodies): These tests screen for autoimmune conditions that may influence immune function and overall metabolic health, potentially affecting glucose levels.

Environmental and toxin testing

* Mycotoxins/mold: Measures levels of mold toxins, which may disrupt metabolic balance and impact health.  
* Heavy metals: Assesses exposure to heavy metals like lead, cadmium, and arsenic that can interfere with hormonal and metabolic functions.  
* Pesticide residues: Identifies the presence of pesticides in the body, which may affect metabolic processes and hormone health.  
* Endocrine (hormone) disruptor screening (BPA, phthalates, PFAS): Detects chemicals from plastics that can disturb hormone regulation and influence metabolism.

Advanced diagnostics

* Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM): Provides detailed, real-time data on blood sugar fluctuations.  
* Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT): Tracks how quickly blood sugar returns to normal after drinking a sweet solution, revealing impaired glucose tolerance.  
* HFE gene mutation analysis: Screens for mutations related to iron overload, which can complicate metabolic regulation in diabetes.  
* Fructosamine: Reflects average blood sugar over the past two to three weeks, useful for tracking more immediate changes in glucose levels.  
* Glycated albumin: Assesses short-term glucose levels over the past 2-3 weeks, helping to validate high glucose results and detect any discrepancies in blood sugar monitoring.  
* Oxidative stress markers: Evaluates oxidative stress through tests like urinary 8-isoprostane or plasma malondialdehyde (MDA). These tests help gauge the level of oxidative damage in the body.  
* Organic acids testing: Organic acid tests can detect imbalances in microbial byproducts, mitochondrial dysfunction, and nutritional deficiencies that indicate dysbiosis and systemic oxidative stress.

Cognitive assessments

High blood glucose can damage brain blood vessels, promote inflammation, and create insulin resistance in neurons, leading to memory loss, slowed thinking, and attention problems. Cognitive testing—through brief tools like memory recall tasks, attention quizzes, or problem‑solving exercises—can catch these subtle changes early, track how well the brain responds to better blood sugar control, and guide personalized steps to protect long‑term mental sharpness.

For more information about maintaining optimum brain health, including cognition, memory, and ways to reduce the risk of age-related neurodegenerative disorders, visit [Brain Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/brain-health-101).

* Female health: Estradiol (E2), testosterone (free), testosterone (total), DHEA-sulfate, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), prolactin, sex hormone binding globulin (SHBG)  
* Male health: Testosterone (free), testosterone (total), estradiol (E2), DHEA-sulfate, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), prolactin, sex hormone binding globulin (SHBG)

#### Follow up

Medical interventions

Collaboration with your healthcare provider ensures a personalized approach to managing glucose levels. Consideration of all symptoms, laboratory test results, and lifestyle factors can guide a treatment plan tailored to your specific needs and health goals.

Medications

Medications for elevated glucose target improved insulin sensitivity, reduced hepatic glucose production, and enhanced glucose uptake to manage blood sugar levels and decrease the risk of complications.

* Metformin: Often the first-line treatment for elevated glucose in type 2 diabetes, metformin improves insulin sensitivity and reduces hepatic glucose production.  
* Sulfonylureas: These medications stimulate the pancreas to produce more insulin, helping to lower blood sugar levels in individuals with elevated glucose.  
* GLP‑1 receptor agonists: By enhancing insulin secretion, suppressing glucagon, and slowing gastric emptying, these drugs help reduce post-meal glucose spikes and lower glucose.  
* SGLT‑2 inhibitors: These agents work by promoting the excretion of glucose through urine, thereby reducing blood sugar levels.  
* Insulin therapy: In cases where other medications are insufficient, exogenous insulin may be prescribed to help control blood sugar.

Procedures

In certain cases where lifestyle modifications and medications are insufficient to control elevated glucose levels, specific medical procedures may be considered to address the underlying metabolic dysfunction and its complications.

* Bariatric surgery: Procedures such as gastric bypass or sleeve gastrectomy may be recommended for individuals with obesity-induced type 2 diabetes to enhance insulin sensitivity and improve glycemic control.  
* Endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty: This less invasive option may help achieve weight loss and metabolic improvement in individuals struggling with severe insulin resistance.

Retesting

Retesting blood sugar at key intervals helps reveal whether changes, such as new medications or diet tweaks, are working as intended.

* HbA1c: Retesting every 3 months when starting or changing treatment, then every 6 months once levels are stable, offers a clear view of long‑term blood sugar trends.  
* Fasting glucose: Checking weekly to monthly during medication adjustments (for example, when adding metformin or an SGLT2 inhibitor) helps fine‑tune dosages and meal timing.  
* Post‑meal blood sugar: Using continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) or a finger‑stick 1-2 hours after eating shines light on how specific foods affect sugar levels, guiding refinements to meal plans or insulin doses.  
* Time in range: For those using CGM, aiming for 70-180 mg/dL and reviewing data every 1-2 weeks during active therapy changes help identify patterns and support timely tweaks.  
* Symptom‑driven checks: Any time symptoms like thirst, fatigue, or dizziness return, an immediate glucose check can catch unexpected highs or lows before they become more serious.

By combining these retesting strategies (focused on both short‑term glucose checks and longer‑term markers)—you and your care teams can monitor progress, uncover hidden patterns, and adapt the plan to keep blood sugar within a healthy range.

#### Why it matters

OmegaCheck is a blood test that measures the percentage of three key omega-3 fatty acids, EPA, DHA, and DPA, out of the total circulating phospholipid fatty acids (PLFAs) in the blood, providing a comprehensive view of the body’s fatty acid profile. These omega-3s are important building blocks for cells and help regulate inflammation, support cardiovascular and brain function, and maintain overall metabolic health. Because the body can't produce enough of them on its own, they must come from food or supplements—most commonly from oily fish like salmon, sardines, or mackerel. OmegaCheck provides a direct look at how well the body is stocked with these essential fats, offering a more accurate picture than dietary tracking alone.  
OmegaCheck is often used alongside other cardiovascular and metabolic tests to personalize health decisions. The test can also be useful for people with a family history of heart disease, high inflammation markers like hs-CRP, or those taking omega-3 supplements who want to know if they're getting enough. Since fatty acid metabolism varies significantly between individuals, OmegaCheck helps bridge the gap between what someone eats and what their body actually uses. This test is distinct from tests that measure EPA and DHA in red blood cells using a different method and scale, so results are not directly comparable.  
Further breakdown of omega-3 fatty acids measured in OmegaCheck  
Eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA)

* Source: Found in fatty fish, fish oil, and algae oil. The body can also convert alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), a plant-based omega-3, into EPA, but the process is inefficient, so direct sources are typically needed.  
* Role: Known for its anti-inflammatory effects, EPA helps lower triglycerides and supports both heart and mental health.

Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA)

* Source: Found in fatty fish, algae, and fish oil supplements. A small amount can be made from ALA, but conversion is limited, making dietary intake important.  
* Role: A structural fat in the brain and retina, DHA is essential for cognition, vision, and nervous system development.

Docosapentaenoic acid (DPA)

* Source: Found in cold-water fatty fish such as salmon, mackerel, and sardines. The body can produce DPA from EPA, and under certain conditions, convert DPA back to EPA or forward to DHA. Small amounts of DPA may also be synthesized from ALA, a plant-based omega-3 found in flaxseeds, chia seeds, and walnuts, though this conversion is limited.  
* Role: Acts as an intermediary between EPA and DHA in the omega-3 pathway, but also has distinct biological functions. DPA contributes to cardiovascular and cognitive health, supports anti-inflammatory activity, and may serve as a reserve form of EPA or DHA, depending on the body’s needs.

By identifying how much omega-3 is truly circulating in the body, OmegaCheck provides a foundation for targeted nutrition, inflammation balance, cardiovascular protection, and personalized strategies to support long-term metabolic health.

#### Summary

A below-range OmegaCheck results indicate insufficient levels of key omega-3 fatty acids—EPA, DHA, and DPA—relative to other circulating fats in the blood. This often stems from low intake of omega-3-rich foods like fatty fish, flaxseeds, or algae, or from an imbalance caused by excess omega-6 intake from processed foods and seed oils ([Dempsey, 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2023.1072653/full); [DiNicolantonio, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8504498/)). When omega-3s are low, the body struggles to regulate inflammation effectively, which may increase the risk of chronic conditions such as cardiovascular disease, cognitive decline, and mood disorders like depression or anxiety.

Root cause approach

A root cause approach to addressing a below-range OmegaCheck involves identifying and correcting factors contributing to the imbalance while supporting the body. Greater detail on food, lifestyle, and other considerations is provided in the sections that follow, but a general overview includes:

* Nutrition: Increasing intake of omega-3-rich foods like fatty fish (e.g., salmon, mackerel, sardines) and algae-based sources can help restore omega-3 levels by directly providing this essential fatty acid. Reducing consumption of omega-6-rich processed foods and refined seed and vegetable oils may help restore the omega-3 to omega-6 ratio, supporting anti-inflammatory pathways.  
* Lifestyle: Regular physical activity and stress management techniques can reduce chronic stress and inflammation that can impact omega-3 absorption and metabolism.  
* Gut health: Supporting gut health with probiotics and prebiotics can enhance nutrient absorption and improve fatty acid metabolism.  
* Supplementation: High-quality omega-3 supplements containing EPA and DHA can support fatty acid balance and reduce inflammation. Dosage should be tailored to individual needs and monitored by a healthcare provider.  
* Detoxification support: Addressing toxin exposure and promoting liver detoxification can improve fatty acid metabolism and cellular health while reducing oxidative stress.  
* Follow-up testing: Regular monitoring of omega-3 levels—especially EPA, DHA, and DPA—through repeat OmegaCheck testing can assess short-to-mid-term progress and guide adjustments to the intervention plan.

This comprehensive strategy helps address the root causes of low omega-3 levels in the blood and supports the body’s ability to manage inflammation, cardiovascular health, and cognitive function.

#### Causes

Root causes of a low OmegaCheck

Low OmegaCheck levels often result from inadequate dietary intake of omega-3 fatty acids, inefficient conversion of alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) to EPA, or imbalances in the omega-6 to omega-3 ratio ([Dempsey, 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2023.1072653/full); [DiNicolantonio, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8504498/)). Chronic inflammation, stress, and metabolic conditions can also impair the body's ability to maintain optimal omega-3 levels, contributing to deficiencies.

Diet and lifestyle factors

* Inadequate diet: Consuming insufficient amounts of fatty fish, seafood, and plant-based sources like algae can lead to deficiencies in omega-3 levels, leading to a low OmegaCheck.  
* High omega-6 intake: Eating a diet high in omega-6 fatty acids, commonly found in processed foods and refined vegetable and seed oils, can interfere with the balance of omega-3s in the body.  
* Increased stress: Chronic stress may affect metabolism and utilization of fatty acids.  
* Sedentary lifestyle: Limited physical activity may impair the body’s ability to regulate inflammation and fatty acid balance.

Other factors

* Poor gut health: An unhealthy gut microbiome can impair the absorption and metabolism of nutrients, including fatty acids.  
* Hormonal imbalances: Conditions like insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome can increase systemic inflammation and influence fatty acid balance.  
* Chronic inflammation: High levels of inflammation increase the body's demand for omega-3 fatty acids, depleting stores.  
* Oxidative stress: Excessive free radicals can damage fatty acids, reducing their availability and effectiveness in the body.  
* Genetic factors: Variations in genes involved in fatty acid metabolism, such as *FADS1* and *FADS2*, can reduce the conversion of ALA to EPA and reduce total omega-3 fatty acid levels.  
* Aging: Age-related metabolic changes can reduce the efficiency of omega-3 fatty acid absorption and conversion.

Medications

Certain medications can contribute to low OmegaCheck results by affecting fatty acid metabolism or absorption.

* Statins: Commonly prescribed to lower cholesterol, statins such as atorvastatin have been shown to significantly reduce circulating levels of omega-3 fatty acids, including EPA and DHA. This interaction may occur through changes in lipid metabolism or interference with fatty acid transport and absorption, potentially reducing the effectiveness of omega-3 supplementation.

Substance use

Substance use can contribute to a low OmegaCheck through several mechanisms.

* Alcohol: Chronic alcohol use can impair the liver’s ability to metabolize and convert essential fatty acids, leading to reduced levels. Alcohol also affects nutrient absorption and may displace dietary intake of omega-3-rich foods.  
* Smoking: Smoking is associated with increased oxidative stress and inflammation, which can disrupt fatty acid metabolism. Additionally, smoking can negatively affect nutritional status, including the intake of omega-3 fatty acids.

#### Associated conditions

A below-range OmegaCheck result reflects a broader imbalance in the body’s fatty acid profile. This imbalance can reduce the body’s ability to control inflammation, maintain cardiovascular and metabolic stability, and support long-term cellular health, making it a key upstream factor in the development of chronic conditions over time.

General conditions and diseases

Low levels of EPA are linked to several health issues, including:

* Cardiovascular disease: Low circulating omega-3 levels are associated with increased risk of heart disease, elevated triglycerides, and high blood pressure. EPA and DHA support arterial flexibility, reduce blood clotting and inflammation, and help lower triglycerides, all of which contribute to cardiovascular protection.  
* Inflammatory conditions: A deficiency in omega-3 fatty acids can contribute to a pro-inflammatory state, raising the likelihood of chronic inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and asthma. Omega-3s play a central role in producing specialized lipid mediators that help resolve inflammation and restore immune balance ([Droulin, 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0300908419300306); [Marton, 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6801729/)).  
* Poor skin health: Low omega-3 levels may impair skin hydration and barrier function, contributing to dryness, sensitivity, and inflammatory skin conditions.  
* Vision problems: DHA is an important structural fat in the retina. Inadequate levels of DHA and EPA may contribute to poor visual acuity and increased risk of age-related macular degeneration by weakening the eye’s ability to counter oxidative stress and inflammation.  
* Metabolic Imbalances: Insufficient omega-3 fatty acids may impair glucose metabolism and contribute to insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes.  
* Thyroid dysfunction: Impaired thyroid function may affect lipid metabolism, reducing the body’s ability to synthesize and utilize omega-3 fatty acids efficiently, potentially leading to lower levels.  
* Neurodegenerative diseases: Lower omega-3 fatty acid status has been associated with an increased risk of cognitive decline and dementia. Adequate omega-3 levels support cognitive functions such as memory and learning, and deficiencies are linked to neurodegenerative diseases.

Mental health conditions

Below-range OmegaCheck levels can also impact mental health, contributing to:

* Depression: Low omega-3 levels have been linked to an increased risk of depressive symptoms and mood disorders.  
* Anxiety: Imbalances in fatty acid composition, including insufficient omega-3s, may contribute to greater susceptibility to anxiety and stress-related symptoms.  
* Schizophrenia: Low omega-3 levels may increase the risk of schizophrenia or worsen its symptoms.

#### Foods to limit

Reducing inflammatory and nutrient-depleting foods is important while supporting the body's ability to restore balanced fatty acid levels.

* Excessive red meat and processed meats (sausages, bacon, hot dogs): These foods are significant sources of arachidonic acid (AA), another omega-6 fatty acid derived from animal products. High AA intake can increase the production of pro-inflammatory molecules, potentially worsening omega-3 deficiency and contributing to chronic inflammatory states.

#### Additional tests

Root cause approach to diagnosing low OmegaCheck levels

A root cause approach to diagnosing low OmegaCheck levels focuses on identifying and addressing underlying factors such as dietary insufficiencies, disrupted fat absorption, chronic inflammation, impaired fatty acid metabolism, or exposure to environmental toxins that can interfere with healthy omega-3 status. These imbalances may be influenced by poor intake of marine-sourced omega-3s, excessive omega-6 consumption, gut dysfunction, metabolic stress, or medication effects.

The need for specific testing depends on your personal health history, risk factors, symptoms, and current conditions, as assessed by your healthcare provider. *Below is a list of potentially relevant tests when OmegaCheck results are below range, with those available through your Function membership noted below.*

Blood testing

* Advanced lipid assessment: Assesses cholesterol and triglyceride levels, in addition to advanced biomarkers like apolipoprotein B, which can reflect disruptions in fatty acid metabolism.  
* Liver function: Evaluates liver enzymes and protein levels to assess the liver’s role in processing and metabolizing fatty acids.  
* Kidney function: Measures creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) to ensure proper filtration and elimination of toxins that may interfere with fatty acid metabolism.  
* Thyroid panel: Assesses thyroid function, as imbalances can affect lipid metabolism.

Diagnostic imaging (CT, MRI, ultrasound, etc.)

* Abdominal ultrasound: Assesses liver and gallbladder health to identify issues such as fatty liver or bile flow obstruction, which can impair fatty acid metabolism and absorption.  
* MRI of the abdomen: Provides detailed imaging of the liver and pancreas to detect structural or functional abnormalities affecting fatty acid processing.  
* DEXA scan (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry): Evaluates body fat composition to understand potential imbalances in lipid metabolism that could influence fatty acid levels.  
* CT scan of the abdomen: Offers a comprehensive view of abdominal organs to identify potential malabsorption-related conditions impacting fatty acid levels.

Nutrient imbalances

* Omega-6 to omega-3 ratio: Determines the balance between these fatty acids, which is important for maintaining optimal fatty acid function.  
* Vitamin E: Evaluates antioxidant levels, as deficiencies can increase oxidative stress and deplete fatty acids.  
* Zinc: Screens for zinc deficiency, which may impair fatty acid metabolism and utilization.

Microbiome and gut health assessment

* Comprehensive stool analysis: Assesses the balance between beneficial and harmful bacteria in the gut. Looks for markers of dysbiosis, yeast overgrowth, or parasitic infections. Stool testing can also evaluate gut permeability ("leaky gut") by measuring zonulin levels, which indicate whether inflammatory triggers are escaping the gut and contributing to systemic inflammation.

See [Gut Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/gut-health-101).

Immune system evaluation

* Inflammatory markers: Measures markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) to gauge the level of systemic inflammation.  
* Food sensitivity and allergy testing: Identifies food sensitivities or allergies that may contribute to inflammation or impact immune function.  
* Autoimmune antibody panels: Screens for conditions like celiac disease or lupus, which can impair nutrient absorption and fatty acid metabolism ([Islam, 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/immunology/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2020.01477/full); [Mędza, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10419423/)).  
* Cytokine profile: Evaluates pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines that influence fatty acid utilization and balance, like adiponectin.

Advanced diagnostics

* Oxidative stress markers: Evaluates oxidative stress through tests like urinary 8-isoprostane or plasma malondialdehyde (MDA). These tests help gauge the level of oxidative damage in the body.  
* Organic acids: Organic acid tests can detect imbalances in microbial byproducts, mitochondrial dysfunction, and nutritional deficiencies that indicate dysbiosis and systemic oxidative stress.  
* Genetic testing: Identifies variations in genes related to fatty acid metabolism or absorption.  
* Bile acids: Evaluates bile flow, which is needed for the digestion and absorption of fatty acids.

Cognitive assessments

Omega-3s play a critical role in reducing inflammation and supporting neuronal communication. Low levels are linked to impaired cognitive functions such as memory, attention, and executive function. Mood disorders like depression and anxiety can further exacerbate cognitive decline by disrupting normal brain function and increasing inflammatory markers. Cognitive testing can help identify specific areas of cognitive impairment, track changes over time, and evaluate the effectiveness of targeted interventions aimed at restoring omega-3 levels and improving brain health.

For more information about maintaining optimum brain health, including cognition, memory, and ways to reduce the risk of age-related neurodegenerative disorders, visit [Brain Health 101](https://www.functionhealth.com/guide/brain-health-101).

#### Follow up

Medical interventions

Collaboration with your healthcare provider ensures a personalized approach to managing OmegaCheck levels. Consideration of all symptoms, laboratory test results, and lifestyle factors can guide a treatment plan tailored to your specific needs and health goals.

Medications

Medications prescribed for conditions associated with low OmegaCheck levels often aim to reduce systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, or metabolic dysfunction—factors that can impact omega-3 status. These may include:

* Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs): These medications inhibit COX enzymes that convert arachidonic acid (AA) into pro-inflammatory compounds, potentially reducing the inflammatory burden that competes with omega-3 pathways.  
* Anti-diabetic medications (e.g., metformin): By improving insulin sensitivity and reducing low-grade inflammation, these medications may support healthier lipid metabolism and indirectly help maintain or improve circulating omega-3 levels

Retesting

Retesting OmegaCheck is typically recommended 8-12 weeks after starting a management plan, which includes dietary changes, omega-3 supplementation, or lifestyle interventions, to allow time for measurable changes in circulating fatty acid levels. Individuals with conditions that affect fat absorption, metabolism, or inflammation may benefit from more frequent monitoring to guide personalized adjustments. Regular follow-up testing helps track progress, confirm improvement in omega-3 status, and evaluate the effectiveness of your overall approach to supporting long-term cardiovascular, cognitive, and metabolic health.
