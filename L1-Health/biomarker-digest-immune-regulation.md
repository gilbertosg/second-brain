## Basophils — in range

**Measures:** the absolute count of basophils, the rarest white cell (well under 1% of the total).

**Why it matters:** basophil granules hold histamine and other messengers. On contact with an allergen or parasite they release them, widening vessels and causing swelling, itch, and redness, and they signal other immune cells to make antibodies. A high count with raised IgE or eosinophils strengthens a case for allergy, asthma, or parasites; a high count with very high total white cells can point to a bone marrow disorder such as chronic myeloid leukemia.

**High / low:** high basophils link to allergic inflammation, chronic sinus or skin allergy, asthma, parasites, myeloproliferative disease, and mild transient rises after infection, surgery recovery, or hypothyroidism. Low basophils are uncommon and follow low white cell production, acute infection, hyperthyroidism, lupus, chronic hives, steroids, or stress hormones. The usual range is 0–200 cells/uL. Colds, pollen, medications, stress, exercise, and time of day move it.

**Your result:** 33 cells/uL (May 19) → 37 cells/uL (Oct 2), in range. Quest and Function optimal 0–200.

## Eosinophils — in range

**Measures:** the absolute count of eosinophils, white cells that target parasitic worms and shape allergic reactions.

**Why it matters:** eosinophils release enzymes and signals that protect or, when overactive, irritate lung, skin, and gut. Function reads them with total white cells, IgE, and hs-CRP, and sometimes with stool tests or biopsies, to separate allergy or asthma from drug reactions, parasites, or rarer immune disorders.

**High / low:** high eosinophils signal allergies (asthma, eczema, hay fever), drug reactions, worm infections, or eosinophilic esophagitis; mild rises after infection often fade on recheck, and persistent high levels warrant a work-up. Low eosinophils usually do not matter unless other white cells are suppressed, and they fall with acute bacterial infection, stress hormones, and steroids. Counts rise at night and dip in the early morning.

**Your result:** 92 cells/uL (May 19) → 118 cells/uL (Oct 2), in range. Quest and Function optimal 15–500.

**Source:** [raw file](../00-Raw_Sources/L1_Health/function-health/2026-10-02/eosinophils.md)

**Measures:** eosinophils as a share of all white blood cells.

**Why it matters:** eosinophils go to inflamed areas to trap material and kill cells, parasites, and bacteria, and they tame allergic responses, though they can overreact to harmless triggers such as pollen. They make up no more than about 5% of white cells. The raw file gives no range for the percentage.

## High-Sensitivity C-Reactive Protein (hs-CRP) — 🔴 above range

**Measures:** a liver-made inflammation protein, read at the low levels that show chronic, low-grade inflammation without symptoms.

**Why it matters:** Function describes chronic systemic inflammation, unrelated to injury or infection, as a major driver of chronic disease and aging, and hs-CRP as a measure that predicts cardiovascular risk. It reads hs-CRP with cholesterol, blood sugar, and hormones: high hs-CRP with high LDL implies more cardiovascular risk than LDL alone, and high hs-CRP with low thyroid hormone suggests inflammation may be affecting the thyroid. Poor diet, stress, sleep, toxins, allergens, and gut imbalance raise it.

**High / low:** the optimal level is below 1.0 mg/L, which reflects minimal inflammation and lower cardiovascular risk. Above range means chronic low-grade inflammation, linked to heart attack, stroke, type 2 diabetes, obesity, autoimmune disorders, and certain cancers, sometimes with subtle fatigue, joint pain, or mood change. A level persistently above 10 mg/L points to acute inflammation or infection and calls for investigation. A cold, injury, poor sleep, emotional stress, or intense exercise the day before the draw can raise it temporarily.

**Your result:** 1.2 mg/L (May 19) → **1.2 mg/L** (Oct 2), above range on both draws. Quest 0–1; Function optimal ≤ 1. The vault protocol's action trigger (> 1.0 mg/L) is breached on both.

**Causes (Function):** a diet of processed food, sugar, and refined carbohydrate; excess omega-6 from seed oils with too little omega-3; vitamin D and magnesium shortfalls; low fiber; a sedentary life; chronic stress; poor sleep; aging; gut dysbiosis and leaky gut; oxidative stress; insulin resistance; obesity, particularly visceral fat; and genetics.

**Follow-up (Function):** physician-guided plan; medication classes it lists for linked conditions are NSAIDs, corticosteroids, biologics, DMARDs, statins, ACE inhibitors or ARBs, PCSK9 inhibitors, low-dose aspirin, metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 agonists, and antibiotics or antivirals for chronic infection; procedures are not used to lower hs-CRP alone. It advises regular hs-CRP monitoring with fasting glucose, fasting insulin, and advanced lipid markers.

**Levers Function lists** *(its text, for physician discussion)*: an anti-inflammatory diet of colorful produce, healthy fats, and omega-3s; regular aerobic exercise (overtraining or very high intensity can temporarily raise hs-CRP); weight loss, since even modest loss lowers hs-CRP by improving insulin sensitivity; sleep; stress reduction; gut support with fiber, prebiotics, and probiotics. Foods to limit: refined carbohydrates and added sugar, trans fats, fried food, processed meat, and advanced glycation end products. Supplements it names: omega-3 (1–3 g), curcumin (500–1,000 mg of standardized extract), CoQ10 (300–400 mg), quercetin. Function advises a clinician review before any supplement.

**Source:** [raw file](../00-Raw_Sources/L1_Health/function-health/2026-10-02/high-sensitivity-c-reactive-protein-hs-crp.md)

## Lymphocytes — in range

**Measures:** the absolute count of lymphocytes: B cells (antibodies), T cells (kill infected cells and coordinate), and natural killer cells.

**Why it matters:** the count shows how many specialist immune cells are on duty. Function reads it with neutrophils and monocytes, and subtype shifts such as the CD4/CD8 ratio help identify infection, autoimmune conditions, and immune deficiency.

**High / low:** low lymphocytes (lymphopenia) can follow chemotherapy, long-term corticosteroids, liver disease, intense physical stress, advanced HIV, or malnutrition and bring recurrent colds and slow wound healing. High lymphocytes (lymphocytosis) suggest a viral illness, an autoimmune flare, chronic inflammation, or a blood cancer such as chronic lymphocytic leukemia. Function treats the reference range as optimal, and values near the low end have associated with higher immune risk in some groups.

**Your result:** 2,119 cells/uL (May 19) → 2,264 cells/uL (Oct 2), in range. Quest and Function optimal 850–3,900.

**Source:** [raw file](../00-Raw_Sources/L1_Health/function-health/2026-10-02/lymphocytes.md)

## Monocytes — in range

**Measures:** the absolute count of monocytes, white cells that clear germs and debris and coach T cells.

**Why it matters:** monocytes patrol the blood and, when signaled, enter tissue as macrophages or dendritic cells, which bridge defense and healing. A rise with total white cells can point to smoldering infection, an autoimmune flare, or certain blood cancers; low monocytes with low red or white cells can point to bone marrow problems. Falling counts after treatment can show therapy working.

**High / low:** low monocytes can reflect chemotherapy, stress hormones, heavy alcohol, benzene exposure, B12 or folate deficiency, or rare genetic defects. High monocytes often mean chronic infection (tuberculosis), autoimmune disease (rheumatoid arthritis), recovery from injury, or rarely a blood cancer. Counts peak in the late afternoon and rise with infection, injury, stress, and exercise.

**Your result:** 568 cells/uL (May 19) → 718 cells/uL (Oct 2), in range, up 26%. Quest and Function optimal 200–950.

**Source:** [raw file](../00-Raw_Sources/L1_Health/function-health/2026-10-02/monocytes.md)

## Neutrophils — in range

**Measures:** the absolute count of neutrophils, the most abundant white cell and the first responders to bacteria and fungi.

**Why it matters:** neutrophils reach an infection within minutes, live only 6–12 hours in blood, and so give a real-time read on frontline defense. Function reads them with the other white cells and with hs-CRP or procalcitonin. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio signals how much inflammation or stress the body carries: high neutrophils with low lymphocytes suggests bacterial infection, the reverse suggests viral.

**High / low:** low neutrophils (neutropenia) raise infection risk and follow chemotherapy, autoimmune disease, severe viral infection, or bone marrow disorders. High neutrophils (neutrophilia) mean bacterial infection, acute inflammation, physical stress, steroids, rheumatoid arthritis, heart attack, or some cancers. Counts are lowest in the early morning and rise through the day. Function notes values that persistently sit near the upper end have associated with higher cardiometabolic and inflammatory risk in some groups.

**Your result:** 3,788 cells/uL (May 19) → 4,262 cells/uL (Oct 2), in range. Quest and Function optimal 1,500–7,800.

**Source:** [raw file](../00-Raw_Sources/L1_Health/function-health/2026-10-02/neutrophils.md)

## White Blood Cell (WBC) Count — in range

**Measures:** the total number of white blood cells (leukocytes), made in bone marrow.

**Why it matters:** the five types (neutrophils, basophils, eosinophils, lymphocytes, monocytes) each have a job, and the pattern across them suggests whether the threat is bacterial, allergic, parasitic, or a deeper imbalance. That pattern helps decide between antibiotics and anti-inflammatory support.

**High / low:** high WBC (leukocytosis) can mean infection, stress from smoking or intense exercise, inflammation such as rheumatoid arthritis, or rarely leukemia. Low WBC (leukopenia) suggests a weakened immune system from deficiencies in B12, iron, or copper, viral infection, autoimmune destruction, or chemotherapy. Function's optimal range is 4,000–11,000 cells/uL. Counts run higher in the afternoon and rise with infection, hard exercise, stress, or sleep loss.

**Your result:** 6.6 Thousand/uL (May 19) → 7.4 Thousand/uL (Oct 2), in range. Quest 3.8–10.8; Function optimal 4–11.
