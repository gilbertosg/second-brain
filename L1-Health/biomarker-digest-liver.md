## Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT) — 🔴 above range

**Measures:** the amount of the enzyme ALT in blood. ALT sits mainly in liver and muscle cells and helps convert amino acids into energy.

**Why it matters:** only traces of ALT reach the blood while cells stay intact. Damage to liver cell membranes from oxidative stress, toxins, or inflammation lets it leak out, so a rising value signals that something is straining the liver. Over time it shows how the liver copes with glucose and lipid imbalance and toxin load. Function pairs it with AST and GGT to separate alcohol, medications, fatty liver, and infection.

**High / low:** high ALT points to liver stress or injury from fatty liver, alcohol, drugs such as acetaminophen, gut dysbiosis, or hepatitis B or C, and very high values call for prompt testing. Low ALT usually reflects a healthy liver, though it can mark a liver change or a shift in vitamin B6 status. Function lists 4–36 U/L as typical for men. Diet changes, alcohol, intense exercise, medications, and supplements can raise it temporarily, and Function cites that about 30% of raised liver-enzyme results return to normal within three weeks.

**Your result:** 31 U/L (May 19) → **94 U/L** (Oct 2), above range. Quest 9–46; Function optimal 0–32 (exported) / 4–36 (page text).

**Causes (Function):** liver disease and genetic disorders (Wilson's disease, hemochromatosis), heart failure, muscle injury, metabolic syndrome and insulin resistance, gut-barrier problems, oxidative stress, a Western-style diet, chronic stress, and poor sleep. Listed drugs include acetaminophen, amoxicillin-clavulanate, statins, methotrexate, diclofenac, and anabolic steroids (the full list is in the raw file). Toxins: heavy metals, traffic-related air pollution, mold, PCBs, and PFAS. Alcohol is the most common substance cause.

**Follow-up (Function):** liver ultrasound, CT, or MRI; biopsy only as a later step; retest about every six months (3–6 months in its summary) unless a clinician sets another interval.

**Levers Function lists** *(its text, for physician discussion)*: moderate exercise, since very intense exercise can transiently alter liver markers; about 7% weight loss when overweight; steady sleep; stress reduction. Foods to favor: colorful produce, cruciferous vegetables, adequate protein. Foods to limit: refined sugar, ultra-processed food, fried food, processed meat, alcohol. Supplements it names: milk thistle, N-acetylcysteine, curcumin, vitamin E, alpha-lipoic acid, selenium (doses are in the raw file). Function advises a clinician review before any supplement.

## Albumin — in range

**Measures:** the main blood protein, made by the liver.

**Why it matters:** albumin holds fluid inside blood vessels and carries hormones, fatty acids, bilirubin, medications, and nutrients to the liver or kidneys for processing. It also binds waste products and free radicals. Function reads it with total protein, bilirubin, ALT, AST, creatinine, BUN, and inflammation markers to judge liver, kidney, and nutritional status.

**High / low:** high albumin is rare and usually means dehydration. Low albumin can mean the liver is not making enough (cirrhosis, hepatitis), too little dietary protein, poor absorption, kidney protein loss, severe infection, or chronic illness, and it can cause leg or belly swelling. Function's optimal range is 4.0–5.0 g/dL. Surgery, infection, and inflammation can move it within months.

**Your result:** 4.8 g/dL (May 19) → 4.7 g/dL (Oct 2), in range. Quest 3.6–5.1; Function optimal 4–5.

## Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) — in range

**Measures:** an enzyme found mostly in the liver (in cells lining the bile ducts), bone, and intestine.

**Why it matters:** ALP rises when bile flow slows or blocks (gallstones, scarring, inflammation) and when bone grows, heals, or breaks down abnormally. Read beside ALT, AST, GGT, and bilirubin: ALP and GGT both high points toward the liver, while ALP alone high points toward bone.

**High / low:** high ALP signals blocked bile ducts, hepatitis, cirrhosis, or bone conditions such as Paget's disease and healing fractures; infections, heart failure, and rare cancers can also raise it. Low ALP is uncommon and rarely significant. Low zinc or magnesium can reduce it, and a rare genetic condition, hypophosphatasia, can cause very low values. Function's optimal range is 30–120 U/L. Healing, growth, pregnancy, nutrient intake, and some medications shift it.

**Your result:** 72 U/L (May 19) → 72 U/L (Oct 2), in range. Quest 36–130; Function optimal 30–120.

## Aspartate Aminotransferase (AST) — 🔴 above range

**Measures:** the blood level of AST (also called SGOT), an enzyme found mostly in liver and also in heart, muscle, and other organs.

**Why it matters:** AST leaks into blood when those cells are injured or stressed. Because it is not liver-specific, Function reads it against ALT. AST higher than ALT (a high AST/ALT ratio) leans toward alcohol-related liver disease, cirrhosis, or muscle or heart injury. ALT higher than AST (a low ratio) leans toward fatty liver, diabetes risk, or metabolic causes.

**High / low:** high AST means liver, heart, or muscle cells are damaged: hepatitis, fatty liver, cirrhosis, heart attack, intense exercise, or muscle injury. Low AST is rarely a concern, though with symptoms, advanced age, or chronic illness it can prompt a check for vitamin deficiency or advanced liver or kidney disease. Function's optimal range is 10–40 U/L for men, with some sources suggesting about 30 as the upper limit when screening for fatty liver. Day-to-day swings of 5–10% are common, and moderate exercise can raise it for up to 24 hours.

**Your result:** 21 U/L (May 19) → **50 U/L** (Oct 2), above range. Quest 10–40; Function optimal 10–40. AST/ALT ratio 0.53.

**Causes (Function):** toxic, metabolic, or mechanical stress on liver, heart, or muscle cells. Diet and lifestyle: high fructose and added sugar, low physical activity, strenuous unaccustomed exercise. Other: rapid weight gain or central obesity, gut-barrier leakage, systemic inflammation, iron or copper excess, insulin resistance, hereditary hemochromatosis and Wilson's disease. Listed drugs: acetaminophen, statins, certain antibiotics, methotrexate, isoniazid, valproic acid, remdesivir. Toxins: heavy metals, PCBs, fine particulate pollution, phthalates and BPA, PFAS, mold. Excess alcohol is a listed substance cause.

**Follow-up (Function):** history and medication review (alcohol, liver-toxic drugs, supplements, strenuous exercise, muscle trauma); the AST/ALT ratio; a comprehensive liver panel with viral, autoimmune, iron, and copper markers; ultrasound or transient elastography when indicated. Retest 4–6 weeks after starting therapy in chronic conditions, or every 3–6 months once stable.

**Levers Function lists** *(its text, for physician discussion)*: moderate exercise while avoiding intense or excessive sessions that injure muscle; weight loss of about 7–10% when overweight; consistent sleep; stress reduction. Foods to favor: colorful produce, cruciferous vegetables, a Mediterranean pattern. Foods to limit: sugary drinks, refined grains, ultra-processed food, fried food, processed meat, alcohol, artificial sweeteners. Supplements it names: milk thistle, curcumin, L-carnitine, vitamin E (doses are in the raw file). Function advises a clinician review before any supplement.

## Gamma-glutamyl Transferase (GGT) — in range (above Function optimal)

**Measures:** an enzyme found mostly in liver and kidney that recycles glutathione, the body's main antioxidant.

**Why it matters:** Function treats GGT as a warning light for oxidative stress, cardiovascular change, and blood sugar and fat irregularities. It can rise early in liver strain and with exposure to air pollution, industrial chemicals, and heavy metals. It pairs with bilirubin, ALT, and AST for a fuller liver picture, and trends tell more than one value.

**High / low:** high GGT typically means liver stress such as inflammation, fatty liver, or gallstones blocking bile flow, and it also associates with obesity and cardiovascular risk. Low GGT rarely matters, though extremely low values (under 5 U/L for women or 7 for men) rarely signal severe liver damage. Function's optimal target is about 5–17 U/L for men; values higher in the normal range track with metabolic syndrome, impaired glucose handling, and coronary calcification risk. Alcohol, medications, activity, diet, and air pollution all move it between draws.

**Your result:** 31 U/L (May 19) → 40 U/L (Oct 2), in Quest range (3–90) and above Function's optimal band (5–17) on both draws.

## Globulin — in range

**Measures:** a group of proteins made by the liver and immune system (calculated from total protein minus albumin).

**Why it matters:** globulins support liver function, clotting, and infection defense. Total globulins can rise in chronic inflammatory disease, multiple myeloma, collagen disease, and rheumatoid arthritis, and fall in liver dysfunction, kidney disease, and some cancers. The raw file carries no further detail.

**Your result:** 2.4 g/dL (May 19) → 2.1 g/dL (Oct 2), in range. Quest 1.9–3.7; Function optimal 1.9–3.7.

## Total Bilirubin — in range

**Measures:** bilirubin, a yellow pigment formed when old red blood cells break down; the liver processes it for removal in bile and stool.

**Why it matters:** it shows how well the liver clears waste and whether red cells break down faster than normal. High bilirubin with low albumin points to liver problems; high bilirubin with otherwise normal liver tests suggests faster red-cell breakdown.

**High / low:** high bilirubin means liver dysfunction, a bile duct blockage such as gallstones, or rapid red-cell breakdown, and very high values cause jaundice. Low bilirubin is usually insignificant, but Function notes research linking low levels to obesity, insulin resistance, fatty liver, and stroke risk. Its optimal range is 0.58–1.20 mg/dL. Heavy alcohol use, some medications, illness, fasting, and intense exercise can shift it temporarily.

**Your result:** 0.7 mg/dL (May 19) → 0.5 mg/dL (Oct 2), in Quest range (0.2–1.2); Function optimal 0.58–1.2, so the Oct value sits just under that band.

## Total Protein — in range

**Measures:** the combined albumin and globulin in blood.

**Why it matters:** these proteins build and repair tissue, support immunity, and keep fluid balanced. A routine panel includes it, and clinicians read it with albumin, globulin, and the A/G ratio to judge liver, kidney, immune, and nutritional status.

**High / low:** high total protein can mean inflammation, infection, a bone marrow disorder such as multiple myeloma, or dehydration. Low total protein can mean malnutrition, liver disease, kidney protein loss, or poor digestion and absorption. Function's optimal range is 6.0–8.5 g/dL. Hydration status moves it most; diet, inflammation, organ function, and blood donation also shift it.

**Your result:** 7.2 g/dL (May 19) → 6.8 g/dL (Oct 2), in range. Quest 6.1–8.1; Function optimal 6–8.5.
