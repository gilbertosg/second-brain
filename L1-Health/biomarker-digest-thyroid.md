## Thyroglobulin Antibodies (TgAb) — in range (May only)

**Measures:** antibodies against thyroglobulin, the thyroid protein used to make T4 and T3.

**Why it matters:** an immune system that treats thyroglobulin as a threat points to autoimmune thyroid disease, most often Hashimoto's thyroiditis and less often Graves' disease, and the antibodies can appear before TSH or hormone levels change. In people treated for thyroid cancer, TgAb interferes with the thyroglobulin tumor marker, and a falling TgAb can signal treatment success. Function reads it with TPO antibodies, TSH, and free T3 and T4, and notes toxins, infections, and gluten can contribute through molecular mimicry.

**High / low:** elevated TgAb suggests an ongoing immune attack on the thyroid that can eventually change hormone levels. Low or undetectable TgAb is normal and shows no active autoimmune response, though a past autoimmune condition can still exist. Function's optimal range is 0–1 IU/mL.

**Your result:** < 2 IU/mL (May 19; not retested), in range. Quest ≤ 2; Function optimal ≤ 1. The result is reported only as under 2, so it cannot show whether it sits under 1.

## Thyroid Peroxidase Antibodies (TPO) — in range (May only)

**Measures:** antibodies against thyroid peroxidase, an enzyme the thyroid needs to make hormone.

**Why it matters:** high TPO antibodies appear in Hashimoto's thyroiditis (underactive thyroid) and Graves' disease (overactive), and they can flag a risk of thyroid dysfunction while hormone levels still look normal. Function uses the marker to catch disease early and to follow progression and treatment.

**High / low:** the optimal level is below 9 IU/mL. Elevated levels suggest an ongoing autoimmune attack that can erode thyroid function over time. A below-range result generally indicates no significant attack but does not rule out Hashimoto's or Graves', since other autoantibodies can cause them; persistent symptoms call for broader autoimmune testing.

**Your result:** < 1 IU/mL (May 19; not retested), in range. Quest < 9; Function optimal 0–9.

## Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone (TSH) — in range (May only)

**Measures:** the pituitary hormone that tells the thyroid to release T4 and T3. It works like a thermostat for thyroid output.

**Why it matters:** TSH is the usual first-line thyroid test. Low TSH with high T3 or T4 means an overactive thyroid; high TSH with low T4 means an underactive one. TSH alone can miss autoimmune thyroid disease, so Function adds TPO antibodies, free T3, and free T4.

**High / low:** high TSH usually means hypothyroidism (fatigue, weight gain, depression, cold sensitivity) from iodine deficiency, autoimmune damage, or thyroid removal. Low TSH usually means hyperthyroidism (rapid heartbeat, weight loss, anxiety, heat intolerance) from autoimmune disease, nodules, or excess iodine. Function's accepted range is 0.45–2.0 mIU/L, with above 4.0 suggesting hypothyroidism and below 0.4 suggesting hyperthyroidism. TSH is higher at night and peaks in the early morning, and illness, stress, medications, and biotin supplements move it. A normal TSH with persistent symptoms calls for a full panel, antibodies, and micronutrient review.

**Your result:** 1.08 mIU/L (May 19; not retested), in range. Quest 0.4–4.5; Function optimal 0.45–2. The vault's earlier "low-trending TSH" note rested on the Jan 2026 Spren value of 0.64.

## Thyroxine (T4) Free — in range (May only)

**Measures:** the unbound thyroid hormone T4, mostly a precursor the body converts into active T3.

**Why it matters:** thyroid hormones touch nearly every organ. TSH rises when T4 falls and drops when T4 rises. Function reads free T4 with TSH, free T3, and antibodies to diagnose thyroid conditions and monitor treatment.

**High / low:** high T4 can mean hyperthyroidism (Graves' disease, overproducing nodules, thyroiditis), with rapid heart rate, weight loss, anxiety, and sweating. Low T4 suggests hypothyroidism (Hashimoto's, iodine deficiency, chronic stress), with fatigue, memory loss, weight gain, constipation, and high cholesterol, and it can also fall during severe non-thyroid illness. Function's optimal range is 1.0–1.5 ng/dL, near the upper third of the lab range. A normal T4 does not rule out subclinical hypothyroidism or poor T4-to-T3 conversion.

**Your result:** 1.5 ng/dL (May 19; not retested), in range at the top of Function's optimal band. Quest 0.8–1.8; Function optimal 1.0–1.5.

## Triiodothyronine (T3) Free — in range (May only)

**Measures:** the active, unbound thyroid hormone made mostly by converting T4 in the liver and kidneys.

**Why it matters:** T3 enters cells and switches genes that set metabolism, temperature, heart rate, digestion, muscle, and brain activity. It shows how much active hormone reaches tissues and catches conversion problems and non-thyroidal illness that TSH can miss. Cortisol can interfere with conversion.

**High / low:** high T3 points to hyperthyroidism (Graves' disease, toxic nodular goiter, excess iodine, too much replacement hormone) and goes with arrhythmias, weight loss, anxiety, and bone loss. Low T3 suggests hypothyroidism or non-thyroidal illness syndrome from chronic illness, stress, or selenium or iodine shortfalls and goes with fatigue, brain fog, depression, and cold intolerance; it also links to chronic kidney disease. Function's optimal range is 3.0–4.4 pg/mL, and some evidence favors the upper half of the lab range. Stress, nutrition, sleep, and seasons move it.

**Your result:** 3.6 pg/mL (May 19; not retested), in range. Quest 2.3–4.2; Function optimal 3.0–4.4.
